Remember Chapter 1: resources are scarce (limited), but wants are unlimited. So every country must choose.

What do we produce? How? Who gets it?

Every country needs a way to answer these questions. That way is called an economic system.

DefinitionEconomic system (système économique)

A particular set of institutions (rules, ownership) and a coordinating mechanism (a way to direct activity) that a society uses to answer the economic questions.

Mrs Ben Hadj Hamida uses three questions to describe any economic system:

  1. Property: who owns the resources (land, capital…)? Private people, or the government?
  2. Coordination: who decides? Decentralized markets (many people), or a central plan?
  3. Incentives: what motivates people to work, produce and invent? Profit and income, or orders and targets?
Three questions classify any system: WHO OWNS? WHO DECIDES? WHAT MOTIVATES?

The answers give the three systems of the next section: laissez-faire capitalism, the command system, and the market (mixed) system.

Memory trick · P-C-I

Property, Coordination, Incentives: “Please Check It.” For any country ask: who is the owner? who is the boss? what is the motor?

Worked example · bread in Ariana

Question: who decides how many baguettes a bakery in Ariana bakes tomorrow?

Market system: the baker decides. He looks at his customers and his prices.

Command system: a government office decides, and sends the order to the bakery.

In French: système économique · facteurs de production (resources) · mécanisme de coordination · économie dirigée / planifiée (command system) · économie de marché (market system).

02

Command system vs market system

The teacher compares three economic systems. Think of a line: total freedom at one end, total state control at the other, and the real world in the middle.

DefinitionLaissez-faire capitalism (capitalisme pur, laissez-faire)

“Let it be.” Private ownership, free markets (prices and production come from voluntary exchange), and a minimal government: only defense, protecting property rights and enforcing contracts.

DefinitionCommand system (économie dirigée / planifiée)

Also called socialism or a planned economy. Public (state) ownership of most resources and capital. A central planning board sets production targets and allocates resources. Prices are often fixed by the administration, not by supply and demand.

DefinitionMarket system (économie de marché, économie mixte)

In practice most economies are mixed market systems: private property and free markets coordinate most activity, and the government plays a limited but active role (rules, public goods, safety nets).

Examples: North Korea and Cuba are the last big command systems (the Soviet Union collapsed in 1992). Pure laissez-faire exists nowhere today: it is a model.

Tunisia is a mixed market system: most shops, cafés, farms and factories are private, but the state provides public health, education and roads, subsidizes bread, and owns firms like STEG.

Worked example · the teacher's sorting quiz

“A central board sets annual steel production targets.” → board + targets → command.

“A family owns a shop and sets its own prices freely.” → private + free prices → capitalism.

“The government regulates food safety but leaves pricing to the market.” → markets + one rule → market (mixed).

“Most factories and farms are state-owned.” → public ownership → command.

Now, the government's role in a market system. Why does it need one at all? Because markets sometimes fail.

DefinitionMarket failure (défaillance du marché)

When the market gives a bad result: it ignores externalities (a factory pollutes and pays nothing) or it under-provides public goods (national defense, street lights, a lighthouse: everyone uses them, nobody wants to pay).

So the government regulates, taxes, provides public goods and redistributes income, without replacing the market.

DefinitionGovernment failure (défaillance de l'État)

When public action (for example poor planning) gives a worse result than the market would. Example: a public agency builds a bridge that nobody uses.

Common mistake

“Market system” does not mean “no government”. Only laissez-faire is like that. In a real market system the government is active but limited, and it can fail too.

03

Features of the market system (property, freedom, self-interest, competition, prices)

How does a market system work without a boss? The teacher gives six key characteristics:

  1. Private property
  2. Freedom of enterprise and choice
  3. Self-interest
  4. Competition
  5. Markets and prices
  6. Technology and capital goods (next section, with specialization and money)

The first five fit together like a chain.

Memory trick · the market story

I OWN it (private property) → I CHOOSE what to do with it (freedom) → I want to GAIN (self-interest) → others try too (competition) → PRICES tell all of us what to do (markets and prices) → to win, firms buy better MACHINES (technology and capital goods).

Six links: Property · Freedom · Self-interest · Competition · Prices · Technology.

1. Private property. Individuals and firms, not the government, own most land and capital. They can sign contracts and leave their property to their children.

Why is it important? Nobody plants olive trees if the state can take the land tomorrow. Property rights encourage people to invest, innovate, exchange and take care of their property.

It also covers intellectual property: patents (inventions), copyrights (books, music, software) and trademarks (brand names).

DefinitionPrivate property (propriété privée)

The right of individuals and firms to own, use, sell and bequeath land, capital and other property. It gives capitalism its name.

2. Freedom of enterprise and choice. Two freedoms:

  • Freedom of enterprise (liberté d'entreprise): firms are free to get resources, produce what they choose, and sell it where they choose.
  • Freedom of choice (liberté de choix): owners use their property and money as they want; workers choose any job they are qualified for; consumers buy what they want with their budget.

These freedoms have legal limits: selling drugs or human organs is forbidden.

3. Self-interest. Each person tries to get what he believes is best for himself. The teacher's short version: households maximize satisfaction (utility); firms maximize profit.

In detail: entrepreneurs want maximum profit. Resource owners want the highest price for their land or labor. Workers want the best job. Consumers want good products at the lowest price.

Self-interest is the motivating force: it pushes people to act. It is not selfishness: a mother who saves money for her children's studies is following her own interest too.

4. Competition. It needs two things: (a) two or more buyers and two or more sellers acting independently (usually many more), and (b) freedom to enter or leave a market.

Competition spreads power: no single seller can dictate the price, because buyers can go to another seller. It disciplines self-interest for the public benefit. The textbook says: competition is the basic regulatory force of the market system.

5. Markets and prices. A market is an institution that brings buyers and sellers together. They interact to set prices, and prices are signals that coordinate millions of decentralized decisions. The market system is the coordinating mechanism, a giant communication network.

Every trade in a market is mutually agreeable: buyer and seller both accept it freely, because each one feels better off. Nobody is forced.

Self-interest = the MOTOR. Competition = the REFEREE (regulator). Markets and prices = the TRAFFIC LIGHTS (coordinator).
Worked example · olive-oil mills competing

A village near Sfax has 3 olive mills. Mills A and B charge 0.25 DT to press 1 kg of olives. Mill C asks 0.35 DT.

Step 1. Farmers compare prices. They take their olives to A and B.

Step 2. Mill C loses customers. Its self-interest says: lower the price, or close.

Step 3. No minister gave an order. Competition alone stopped Mill C from charging too much.

Worked example · self-interest helps others

A baker in Sousse wants profit (self-interest).

To get it, he must bake bread that customers like, at a price they accept. If his bread is bad, they go to the bakery next door (competition).

So, to help himself, he must help his customers.

In French: propriété privée · liberté d'entreprise · liberté de choix · intérêt personnel · concurrence · marché · prix · brevet (patent) · droit d'auteur (copyright) · marque (trademark).

Capital goods, specialization and money

The sixth characteristic is technology and capital goods. Then come two helpers that make the system productive: specialization and money.

Capital goods are tools, machines and buildings used to make other goods. A farmer with bare hands produces little. With a plough, a tractor and a storage room, he produces much more.

The direct way (bare hands) is often the least efficient. Competition and profit push firms to adopt new technology and buy more capital goods, so productivity rises over time.

DefinitionSpecialization (spécialisation)

Using the resources of a person, a firm, a region or a country to produce one or a few goods, and then exchanging them for the other things we want.

Today almost nobody produces what he eats or wears. The textbook says: “self-sufficiency breeds inefficiency.” A jack-of-all-trades is not efficient.

Human specialization is called the division of labor (division du travail). It raises output for three reasons:

  1. Differences in ability: each person does what he is best at (a strong player plays football, a good singer sings).
  2. Learning by doing: even with equal abilities, doing one task every day makes you very good at it.
  3. Saving time: no time lost moving from one job to another.
Memory trick · ALT (like the keyboard key)

Division of labor works because of Ability · Learning by doing · Time saved.

The teacher separates two words. Specialization = people, firms or regions focus on what they do best, then trade. Division of labor = breaking production into separate, repetitive tasks. Both raise output per worker.

Worked example · Tunisia's olive oil (teacher's case)

Farmers grow the olives. Mills press the oil. Exporters find buyers abroad.

Nobody does all three. Each one does one job very well, and they trade with each other.

Result: Tunisia is one of the world's biggest olive-oil exporters.

Geographic specialization: each region makes what its land and climate suit best. Sfax: olive oil. Tozeur and Kebili: dates. Kerkennah: fish. Then the regions exchange.

Worked example · a bakery divides the work

A bakery has 3 workers. Before: each worker does everything (mix, shape, bake): 100 loaves each per day → 300 loaves.

After: one only mixes, one only shapes, one only bakes. Output: 450 loaves.

Gain: 450 − 300 = 150 more loaves, that is +50%, with the same 3 workers.

Why? ALT: the best shaper shapes (ability), each one gets faster (learning), nobody washes his hands to change task (time).

Now a problem. If you specialize, you must exchange. How?

DefinitionBarter (le troc)

Swapping goods directly for goods, with no money. Barter needs a coincidence of wants: the person who has what you want must also want what you have.

A coincidence of wants is rare. That is why we use money.

DefinitionMoney (la monnaie)

Anything that is generally accepted by sellers in exchange for goods and services. Its main job: a medium of exchange (moyen d'échange). In Tunisia: the dinar.

Worked example · fisherman, shoemaker, baker (teacher's exercise)

The fisherman wants shoes. The shoemaker wants bread. The baker wants fish.

With barter: the shoemaker does not want fish, so the fisherman must go around: (1) he gives fish to the baker for bread; (2) he gives that bread to the shoemaker for shoes. Two trades, lots of time.

With money: he sells his fish for dinars and buys shoes with them. One simple step. Money removes the need for a coincidence of wants.

Worked example · the Tunisian triangle (textbook Figure 2.1)

Sfax has extra olive oil and wants dates. Tozeur has extra dates and wants fish. Kerkennah has extra fish and wants olive oil.

With barter: Sfax offers oil to Tozeur. Tozeur says no: it wants fish, not oil. No coincidence of wants → trade stops.

With money (dinars): (1) Sfax sells oil to Kerkennah for dinars. (2) Sfax uses the dinars to buy dates from Tozeur. (3) Tozeur uses the dinars to buy fish from Kerkennah.

Everyone gets what he wants. Money solved the problem.

In history, cattle, shells, stones, cigarettes and metals were used as money. The only test: it must be generally acceptable. As the textbook says, we want money only to part with it (to spend it).

Specialization needs exchange. Exchange needs money (barter needs a coincidence of wants).

In French: biens de capital (biens d'équipement) · spécialisation · division du travail · spécialisation géographique · troc · double coïncidence des besoins · monnaie · moyen d'échange.

05

The five fundamental questions

Every economy, rich or poor, must answer five questions. Here is the teacher's list:

  1. What goods and services will be produced?
  2. How will they be produced?
  3. Who will get the output?
  4. How will the system change (adapt and grow)?
  5. How will the system deal with risk?
Careful

In Chapter 1 you saw three questions (what, how, for whom). Chapter 2 has five. The textbook words the last two a bit differently (“accommodate change” and “promote progress”); on the exam, use the teacher's list above: change and risk.

Memory trick · 3 + 2

3 questions about today: What? How? Who?

2 questions about tomorrow: Change? Risk? (Tomorrow always brings change and risk.)

Here is how the market system answers each one:

QuestionThe market's answer
1. What?Consumer sovereignty: consumers “vote” with their dinars. Profitable goods continue; unprofitable goods are dropped.
2. How?With the least-cost combination of resources, given technology and resource prices (next section).
3. Who?People who are able and willing to pay. That depends on their income.
4. Change?Creative destruction + capital accumulation.
5. Risk?Businesses, not the government, bear most of the risk. Profit rewards success; loss punishes failure.

1. What? No planner orders it. When people stopped renting DVDs and moved to streaming, DVD shops lost money and closed. (More in section 7.)

3. Who? Ability to pay depends on your income. Your income depends on the resources you own and sell (labor, land, capital) and on their prices (wages, rent, interest, profit).

Worked example · who gets more

An engineer earns 3,000 DT per month. A worker earns 750 DT per month.

3,000 ÷ 750 = 4. So the engineer can buy 4 times as much of the economy's output.

(Textbook: a lawyer at $200 per hour vs a worker at $10 per hour → 20 times as much.)

4. Change. Two engines:

DefinitionCreative destruction (destruction créatrice)

New products and new techniques replace old ones and destroy the market of firms stuck with the old ways. Examples: streaming replaced DVDs; smartphones replaced cameras and GPS devices.

Worked example · ride-hailing apps vs taxis (teacher's case)

Ride-hailing apps arrive in Tunis. You book a car on your phone, see the price before, pay easily.

Winners: consumers (easier, often cheaper) and the innovators (profit).

Losers: some taxi drivers lose customers: displaced workers.

This is creative destruction: something new is created, something old is destroyed. Self-driving cars could be the next wave.

DefinitionCapital accumulation (accumulation du capital)

Buying more capital goods (machines, buildings), paid for by savings and profits. It drives long-run growth.

5. Risk. Every business can fail. In a market system, the owner takes this risk.

Worked example · Youssef's restaurant (teacher's case)

Youssef puts his savings (say 40,000 DT) into a restaurant.

If it succeeds: he keeps the profit. Profit is the reward for taking risk successfully.

If it fails: he loses his money. The loss is the penalty.

In a command system: the state owns the restaurant, so the state absorbs the risk, the losses and the profits.

Market system: the business owner bears the risk and keeps the profit or the loss.

In French: questions fondamentales · souveraineté du consommateur · capacité à payer · destruction créatrice · accumulation du capital · risque · profit = récompense du risque.

How to produce: the least-cost technique

Question 2: how will goods be produced? In a market system the answer is simple: with the cheapest technique.

Why? Because of competition. A firm with high costs must charge more. Its rivals with low costs sell cheaper and take its customers. So competition eliminates high-cost producers.

DefinitionLeast-cost technique (technique au moindre coût)

Among the techniques that produce the same output, the one with the lowest total cost in money. The textbook calls this economic efficiency: a given output with the least input of scarce resources, measured in money.

The best technique depends on two things: (1) the technology available, and (2) the prices of the resources.

The method has 3 steps:

Cost of a technique = (units × price) for each resource, then add
  1. For each technique, multiply units × price for each resource.
  2. Add the four results: that is the total cost of the technique.
  3. Choose the technique with the lowest total cost.
Memory trick · Multiply, Add, Pick the smallest

M-A-P: Multiply (units × price), Add, Pick the smallest total.

Never count the units! A unit of capital and a unit of land do not cost the same.

The teacher's exercise. A firm can make the same output with three combinations of labor (L) and capital (K). Wage = 8 DT per unit of labor. Rental = 6 DT per unit of capital.

Economic profit, losses and 'dollar votes'

Question 1: what will be produced? Answer: the goods that make a profit.

DefinitionEconomic profit (profit économique)

Total revenue (TR) minus total cost (TC), where TC includes all opportunity costs: wages, rent, interest, and a normal payment to the entrepreneur.

Economic profit = Total revenue − Total cost

Total revenue = quantity sold × price. Total cost = everything paid for resources, including the owner's own payment.

Three cases:

CaseMeaningWhat happens to the industry
TR > TCeconomic profitexpands: firms grow, new firms enter
TR < TCeconomic losscontracts: firms shrink or leave (exit)
TR = TCzero economic profit (normal profit)stays the same: no entry, no exit
Memory trick · profit is a magnet

Profit = a magnet: it pulls new firms IN (entry).

Loss = a fire alarm: firms run OUT (exit).

Worked example · a café chain in Sfax

Revenue per month: 5,000 DT.

Costs: wages 2,000 + rent 1,000 + interest 500 + payment to the owner 1,000 = 4,500 DT.

Economic profit = 5,000 − 4,500 = +500 DT.

Profit is a magnet: more cafés open, the industry expands.

Worked example · olive-oil mills after a bad year

Revenue: 30,000 DT. Total cost: 34,000 DT.

Economic profit = 30,000 − 34,000 = −4,000 DT (a loss).

Fire alarm: some mills close or move to another activity. The industry contracts.

Common mistake

The payment to the owner (his normal profit) is a cost in economics. Zero economic profit is not a disaster: the owner is already paid a normal return.

But who decides what is profitable? The consumers.

Each dinar you spend is like a vote. The textbook calls it a dollar vote (in Tunisia: a dinar vote). Products that get enough votes become profitable and are produced.

DefinitionConsumer sovereignty (souveraineté du consommateur)

Consumers are “in command”: through their dollar votes they decide which goods and services are produced. Firms must follow them, or face losses and bankruptcy.

Example from the textbook: McDonald's “McHits and McMisses”. The Big Mac and Chicken McNuggets got many dollar votes: they stayed. The Hulaburger and the Arch Deluxe got few votes: they disappeared.

A Tunisian example from the teacher: when people moved from DVDs to streaming, DVD shops lost dinar votes, made losses, and closed. No planner ordered it.

The same is true for resources: workers are hired in car factories because people buy cars.

Last idea: risk. Profit is not a gift. It is the reward for taking risk successfully; a loss is the penalty. In a market system, businesses (not the government) bear most of the risk: remember Youssef's restaurant.

Memory trick · the owner's deal

“My risk, my profit, my loss.” The owner takes the risk, so he keeps the profit, and he also eats the loss.

In French: profit économique · recette totale · coût total · perte · entrée / sortie (du marché) · souveraineté du consommateur · vote en dinars (dollar votes).

The invisible hand

Here is a surprising idea. Everybody follows his own interest. Yet the result is good for society. How?

In 1776, Adam Smith wrote The Wealth of Nations. He said: in a competitive market, people who seek their own interest are led, as if by an “invisible hand”, to promote the interest of society.

DefinitionInvisible hand (la main invisible)

Adam Smith's idea that firms and resource suppliers, seeking their own self-interest in a competitive market system, also promote the public (social) interest, without wanting to.

Two textbook examples:

  • Firms make better products to earn more profit → consumers are better off.
  • Firms use the least-cost technique to earn more profit → scarce resources are saved for other uses.

The key word is competition. Competition controls self-interest, so that it serves society.

Memory trick

Self-interest + competition = social good. Remove competition, and the hand stops working.

Worked example · the bakery again

The baker wants profit (self-interest). Competition from other bakeries forces him to bake good bread at a low price, using the cheapest technique.

Result: the neighbourhood gets good, cheap bread, and resources are not wasted. Nobody planned it. That is the invisible hand.

The textbook gives the market system three virtues:

  1. Efficiency: resources go to the goods people want most, produced with efficient techniques.
  2. Incentives: skills, hard work and innovation are rewarded.
  3. Freedom: activity is coordinated without force (no one is ordered what to do).
Memory trick · E-I-F

The three virtues: Efficiency, Incentives, Freedom: “Every Idea is Free.”

But the hand has limits. It can fail when there is:

  • pollution (externalities): a factory's smoke hurts people who are not paid for it;
  • monopoly power: one big seller with no competition can charge too much;
  • information gaps: buyers do not know the real quality of what they buy.

These limits are why a market system keeps an active but limited government.

Common mistake

The invisible hand needs competition. With a monopoly, pollution or hidden information, self-interest can hurt society.

In French: la main invisible · intérêt personnel / intérêt général · efficacité · incitations · liberté.

Why command systems failed

The Soviet Union, eastern Europe and China (before its reforms) used central planning. Why did it fail? The textbook gives two big problems.

DefinitionCoordination problem (problème de coordination)

Planners could not gather and process the information needed to set prices and quantities for millions of goods. And the output of one industry is the input of another.

Worked example · the tractor chain

The plan: a factory must make 1,000 tractors.

It needs labor, machines, electricity, steel, tyres, glass, paint and transport.

Step 1. The iron mines miss their target.

Step 2. So the steel mills cannot make enough steel.

Step 3. So the tractor, car and transport factories all miss their targets. One mistake spreads like dominoes.

As economies grew, these bottlenecks became normal. Planners also cut product variety to make planning easier.

There was also no good success indicator. A market economy uses profit, which shows demand, efficiency and quality. A command economy used quantity targets, so quality and variety were sacrificed.

Worked example · the nails story (true!)

Target in weight (“make 10 tonnes of nails”) → the factory makes only big, heavy nails. Fewer nails to make, and the weight is reached fast.

Target in number (“make 1 million nails”) → the factory makes only tiny nails.

In both cases, people could not find the nails they really needed.

DefinitionIncentive problem (problème d'incitation)

Without profit or competition, people had weak incentives to innovate, cut costs and respond to what consumers want.

Planners decided the output mix. When they guessed wrong (too many shoes, not enough chickens), there were shortages and surpluses that lasted for years.

Managers were rewarded for meeting the target, not for pleasing customers. With no prices and profits as signals, nobody had a reason to adjust.

There was also no entrepreneurship: no profit, no reward for innovation. To get ahead, people climbed the Communist Party ladder instead.

Memory trick · C and I

Coordination = “Can't connect the pieces” (millions of linked decisions).

Incentive = “I don't care” (no profit, no reward, no reason to adjust or innovate).

The textbook compares the two Koreas (2008): GDP per person was about $1,800 in North Korea (command) and $24,500 in South Korea (market). More than 13 times higher.

The teacher adds other factors: sanctions, resource shocks (for example a fall in oil prices) and political instability.

Her Last Word is Venezuela's crisis, seen from three views: the policy view (nationalizations, price controls, printing money), the external-shock view (the fall in oil prices), and the institutional view (weak institutions, sanctions).

Common mistake

Do not mix the two problems. Missing inputs, chain reactions, nails, no success indicator → coordination. Nobody adjusts, no innovation, rewards for meeting targets, party careers → incentive.

In French: problème de coordination · problème d'incitation · planificateur · objectif de production · indicateur de réussite · pénurie · excédent.

The circular flow model

Last idea, and a key graph for the exam. The circular flow model shows the whole market economy in one picture.

It has two groups of decision makers:

  • Households (ménages): people and families. In the model, they own all the resources (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurial ability).
  • Businesses (entreprises): firms that buy resources and produce goods.

And two markets:

DefinitionResource market (marché des facteurs)

Where households SELL resources and businesses BUY them. The payments (wages, rent, interest, profit) are costs for businesses and income for households.

DefinitionProduct market (marché des produits / des biens et services)

Where businesses SELL goods and services and households BUY them. Households' spending (consumption expenditures) is the revenue of businesses.

Households SELL resources and BUY goods. Businesses BUY resources and SELL goods. Both are buyers AND sellers.
Worked example · the Ben Salah family

Mr Ben Salah works in a textile factory. He sells his labor in the resource market and gets 1,200 DT of wages (money income).

The family also rents a small plot of land to a neighbouring farmer for 300 DT (land → rent).

Total income: 1,200 + 300 = 1,500 DT.

Then they buy bread, couscous, clothes in the product market. If they spend all of it, the firms get 1,500 DT of revenue.

That money pays wages and rent again… and the circle goes on.

In the teacher's slides, the top loop is the product market and the bottom loop is the resource market. Our picture follows her.

Read the picture in two rings:

  • Inner ring, green (real flow): things. Resources go from households to businesses; goods and services go from businesses to households. It turns counterclockwise.
  • Outer ring, dashed (money flow): money. Income goes to households; spending goes to businesses. It turns clockwise.
Memory trick · money always pays for things

Money always goes the opposite way of the thing it pays for. Labor goes → to the firm, so wages go ← to the family.

And the markets: Resource market = where you Rent out yourself (your work, land, savings). Product market = where you Pay for things.

Teacher's picture: Products on top (like a shop window), Resources at the roots (bottom).

Careful with directions

Some books draw the picture mirrored, so the turning direction can look different. What never changes: money always goes the opposite way of the thing it pays for. In our picture and in the textbook's Figure 2.2, things go counterclockwise and money clockwise.

Common mistake

The classic trap: “households sell products” or “businesses sell resources”. Both are wrong. Households SELL resources (resource market) and BUY products (product market).

What to remember

  • Three systems: laissez-faire (government only for defense, police, contracts) · command (state owns, planning board, targets) · market = mixed (markets + an active but limited government). Market failure (pollution, lighthouse) vs government failure (useless bridge).
  • Six characteristics: Property → Freedom → Self-interest (motor) → Competition (referee) → Markets and prices (coordinator) → Technology and capital goods.
  • Capital goods + specialization (division of labor: Ability, Learning, Time) raise output. Specialization needs exchange; barter needs a coincidence of wants; money = medium of exchange.
  • Five questions: What? How? Who? Change (creative destruction, capital accumulation)? Risk (businesses bear it; profit = reward, loss = penalty)?
  • How: least-cost technique = Multiply, Add, Pick the smallest total. Not the fewest units. A cheaper resource → used more.
  • What: economic profit = TR − TC (TC includes the owner's normal pay). Profit → entry, expand. Loss → exit, contract. Consumers rule with dollar votes.
  • Invisible hand (Adam Smith, 1776): self-interest + competition = social good. Limits: pollution, monopoly power, information gaps.
  • Command systems failed: coordination problem (chain reactions, nails, no success indicator) and incentive problem (no reason to adjust or innovate).
  • Circular flow (teacher: product market on top, resource market at the bottom): households SELL resources (resource market, get wages, rent, interest, profit) and BUY goods (product market, spending = business revenue). Things counterclockwise, money clockwise.
