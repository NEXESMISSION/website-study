Walk into a big shopping centre like Tunisia Mall in Les Berges du Lac. You see thousands of products: phones, shoes, coffee, books, perfumes. Here is the strange part: nobody ordered all of this. No minister wrote a list. So who decided which goods to make? Who chose the machines and the workers? Who decides which people get the products? And why do new and better products keep appearing? This chapter answers these questions.

Remember Chapter 1: resources are scarce, but wants are unlimited. This is the economizing problem. Every society must organize itself to deal with it. The way a society organizes itself is called its economic system (système économique).

DefinitionEconomic system (système économique)

A particular set of institutional arrangements (laws, customs, organizations) and a coordinating mechanism that a society uses to respond to the economizing problem. It must decide what is produced, how it is produced, who gets it, how to accommodate change and how to promote technological progress.

Economic systems differ in two ways:

  1. Who owns the factors of production (land and capital): the government, or private people and firms?
  2. The method used to motivate, coordinate and direct economic activity: a central plan, or markets and prices?

Using these two questions, we get two extreme ("polar") types of economic system: the command system and the market system.

The command system

DefinitionCommand system (économie planifiée / dirigée)

An economic system in which the government owns most property resources and economic decisions are made through a central economic plan. It is also called socialism or communism.

How does it work in practice?

  • A central planning board, appointed by the government, makes nearly all the big decisions: how resources are used, which goods are made, how output is shared, and how production is organized.
  • The government owns most business firms. They produce what the government tells them to produce.
  • The planning board sets a production target for each enterprise and gives it the resources it needs to reach that target.
  • The board also decides how output is split between capital goods (machines, factories) and consumer goods (food, clothes).

Pure command systems hardly exist. Even the Soviet Union allowed some private ownership and some markets before it collapsed in the early 1990s. After that, Russia and most of eastern Europe moved toward market systems. China also reduced central planning, but the government still owns a large part of the economy. Today, North Korea and Cuba are the last prominent examples of largely centrally planned economies. The textbook also names a few other economies that still rely mainly on the command system: Turkmenistan, Laos, Belarus, Libya, Myanmar and Iran.

The market system

DefinitionMarket system (économie de marché)

An economic system based on private ownership of resources and the use of markets and prices to coordinate and direct economic activity. It is also called capitalism.

  • People and firms act in their own self-interest.
  • Anyone who is willing and able can produce goods or offer resources. So there is competition among many independent buyers and sellers.
  • Decision making is widely dispersed: it is spread over millions of people, not kept in one office.
  • Success can bring large money rewards. This gives people a strong incentive to innovate (to create new products and new methods).

Laissez-faire and the real world

The purest form of capitalism is called laissez-faire capitalism. Good news for you: this term is French! It means "let it be" or "let people do". Under laissez-faire, the government does only two things: it protects private property and it sets up a legal environment where markets can work. The idea is that any other government action would disturb the efficiency of the market.

In reality, no country is pure laissez-faire. In the United States and most other countries, the government plays a substantial role. It:

  • makes the rules (laws, contracts, standards);
  • tries to keep the economy stable and growing;
  • provides goods and services the market would underproduce (for example street lighting or national defense);
  • changes the distribution of income (taxes, help for poor families).

Even so, in these countries the market is still the dominant force that decides what is produced, how, and for whom. That is why we still call them market systems. In everyday language, economists call this reality a mixed economy (économie mixte).

Real economies are mixtures. The question is not "market OR government" but "how much of each".

Tunisian example

Tunisia is a mixed economy. Most cafés, shops, farms, factories and call centres are private, and most prices are set by markets. But the state also plays a big role. It owns large firms such as STEG (electricity and gas) and SONEDE (water). It fixes the price of some basic goods, like subsidized bread, semolina and fuel. It also runs public schools and hospitals. So Tunisia sits between the two ends of the spectrum, like most countries.

Common mistake

"In a market system, there is no government." Wrong. That describes only pure laissez-faire, which does not exist. Real market economies have an active but limited government. Also wrong: "A command system has no markets at all." Even the Soviet Union tolerated some private ownership and some markets.

Exam alert

Know the other names: command system = socialism = communism; market system = capitalism; laissez-faire = pure capitalism. Also know the two ways systems differ: who owns the resources, and how activity is coordinated.

1. Private property

In a market system, individuals and businesses — not the government — own most of the property resources (land and capital). This is where the name "capitalism" comes from: private people own the capital.

DefinitionPrivate property (propriété privée)

The right of private persons and firms to obtain, own, control, use, sell and bequeath land, capital and other property. ("Bequeath" (léguer) means to give your property to your heirs when you die.)

Private property comes with the freedom to negotiate binding legal contracts (contrats). For example, you can sign a contract to sell your car or rent your apartment, and the law will enforce it.

Why are property rights so important?

  • They encourage investment, innovation, exchange and economic growth. As the textbook puts it, nobody would "stock a store, build a factory, or clear land for farming" if someone else, or the government, could take that property.
  • They extend to intellectual property (propriété intellectuelle) through patents (brevets), copyrights (droits d'auteur) and trademarks (marques déposées). These protect ideas and creations, so people are encouraged to write books, make music, develop software and invent.
  • They make exchange easier. A title to a car or a deed to a ranch proves who the owner is. The buyer knows they are buying from the real owner.
  • They encourage owners to maintain and improve their property, because they keep the benefits.
  • They let people use their time and resources to produce, instead of spending them to guard what they already have.
Example · an olive grove near Sfax

Hédi owns an olive grove near Sfax, with an official land title (titre foncier). Young olive trees need several years before they give a good harvest. Hédi plants 200 young trees today because he is sure that the future olives — and the money from the oil — will be his. Now imagine the government could take his land at any time. Would he plant trees that pay only years later? Probably not. Without secure property rights, nobody invests for the future.

Example · intellectual property and exchange

Salma, a student in Tunis, spends six months building a mobile app to book seats in a louage. If anyone could copy her code for free, she would earn nothing from her work. Copyright protects her code and a trademark protects the app's name and logo. So she has a reason to create. And when her brother sells his car, the carte grise proves he is the owner, so the buyer can pay with confidence. That is property rights making exchange easier.

2. Freedom of enterprise and choice

DefinitionFreedom of enterprise (liberté d'entreprise)

The freedom of entrepreneurs and private businesses to obtain and use resources to produce their choice of goods and services, and to sell them in the markets they choose.

DefinitionFreedom of choice (liberté de choix)

The freedom of owners to employ or dispose of their property and money as they see fit; of workers to enter any line of work for which they are qualified; and of consumers to buy the goods and services that best satisfy their wants, within their budgets.

These freedoms are not unlimited. They exist only within broad legal limits. For example, selling human organs or illicit drugs is illegal. In Tunisia, some activities also need a licence, like opening a pharmacy or driving a taxi.

Example

Freedom of enterprise: after her degree, Yasmine opens a coffee shop in Sousse. She chooses the location, the menu, the prices and the number of workers. Freedom of choice: as a student with 10 TND for lunch, you choose between a bowl of lablabi and a sandwich. A worker in Sfax can choose to leave a textile factory to work in a call centre if he is qualified.

3. Self-interest

DefinitionSelf-interest (intérêt personnel)

The motivating force of the market system: each economic unit (a firm, a property owner, a worker, a consumer) tries to do what is best for itself — to achieve its own goals.

Who?What "self-interest" means for them
EntrepreneursMaximize profit or minimize loss
Property ownersGet the highest price when they sell or rent their resources (land, buildings, machines)
WorkersMaximize utility (satisfaction) from their job: the best combination of wages, hours, fringe benefits and working conditions
ConsumersGet the products they want at the lowest price, and spend their money to maximize their utility

Self-interest gives direction and consistency to what could otherwise be a chaotic economy. Everybody knows roughly how others will behave. And here is the key point: in a market, you usually reach your own goal by delivering something of value to others. A firm makes profit only if customers want its product.

Example · a café in Sfax

Karim owns a café in Sfax. He wants profit (self-interest). To get it, he must serve good coffee, keep the place clean and charge a fair price — otherwise customers go to the café next door. His self-interest pushes him to serve his customers well.

Common mistake

Self-interest is not selfishness. Self-interest only means that each person pursues his or her own goals. Those goals can include helping family, saving for your children or giving to charity. A selfish person ignores others; a self-interested person in a market must serve others to succeed.

4. Competition

DefinitionCompetition (concurrence)

The presence in a market of many independent buyers and sellers who compete with one another, and the freedom of buyers and sellers to enter or leave the market.

So competition needs two conditions:

  1. Independent action by two or more buyers and two or more sellers of a product (usually many more than two).
  2. Freedom of sellers and buyers to enter or leave markets, based on their self-interest.

What does competition do?

  • It spreads (diffuses) economic power. With many buyers and sellers, no single one can dictate the price. If a seller asks too much, others will undercut him.
  • It gives flexibility. Because firms can enter and leave, industries can adjust to changes in tastes, technology and resource availability, and stay efficient.
  • It limits the abuse of power. A producer who charges more than the competitive price loses sales to other producers. An employer who pays less than the competitive wage loses workers to other employers. A firm that ignores new technology loses profits to firms that use it.
"Competition is the basic regulatory force in the market system." It replaces a government controller.
Example · Marché Central, Tunis

At the Marché Central, twenty stalls sell oranges from Cap Bon. Most sellers ask about 3 TND per kilo. One seller tries 5 TND. Buyers simply walk five metres to the next stall. Very quickly, he must lower his price or sell nothing. Nobody forced him — competition did. In the same way, Tunisia's mobile operators keep offering cheaper internet packages, because a customer who finds a better offer can switch operator.

5. Markets and prices

Here is a puzzle. In a market system, millions of people act in their own self-interest. Consumers may want breakfast cereal, while firms decide to make running shoes and resource suppliers want to make software. Why doesn't this create chaos? Because markets and prices coordinate all these decisions.

DefinitionMarket (marché)

An institution or mechanism that brings buyers ("demanders") and sellers ("suppliers") into contact. A market can be a physical place, like the souk or the Marché Central, or not a place at all, like an online selling website.

In a market, prices act as signals. They tell consumers what is cheap or expensive, they tell entrepreneurs what is profitable, and they tell resource owners where their resources are most wanted. The textbook calls the market system an "elaborate communication network". People who respond to the signals are rewarded with profit and income. People who ignore them are penalized with losses.

Example · the price of tomatoes

In winter, fresh tomatoes become expensive in Tunisia. The high price sends two messages at the same time. To farmers: "Grow more tomatoes in greenhouses — it pays." To families: "Use fewer fresh tomatoes; use more tomato paste (tomate concentrée) instead." Nobody gave an order. The price alone coordinated thousands of farmers and families.

Competition is the regulator. Markets and prices are the coordinator.
Quick review 2.1

The market system rests on private property and freedom of enterprise and choice. It allows people to pursue their self-interest. Competition spreads economic power and limits its abuse. Markets and prices are the coordinating mechanism.

How the market system works (2): capital goods, specialization, money, government

6. Technology and capital goods

In a market system, competition, freedom of choice, self-interest and personal reward all push people to find better technology. Why? Because the money rewards for a new idea go to the innovator. This also encourages the wide use and fast development of complex capital goods (biens d'équipement / biens de capital): tools, machinery, large factories, storage, communication, transportation and marketing facilities.

DefinitionCapital goods (biens d'équipement)

Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services — tools, machines, factories, trucks, warehouses. They are not consumed directly; they help production.

An important idea: the most direct way of producing is often the least efficient. Producing capital goods first (for example making a tractor) takes time, but then you can produce far more.

Example · textbook and Tunisia

Textbook: a farmer working with bare hands grows a little food. The same farmer with plows, tractors and storage bins produces a much larger output. Tunisia: olives can be picked by hand and pressed in an old stone press (maâsra). A modern farm uses mechanical shakers and a modern continuous olive mill (huilerie). Building the machines costs money and time, but then far more oil is produced per worker.

7. Specialization

DefinitionSpecialization (spécialisation)

Using the resources of an individual, a firm, a region or a nation to produce one or a few goods or services, rather than the entire range of goods and services. People then exchange to get the full range of products they want.

Most of us produce almost none of what we consume. The textbook's examples: a worker who installs windows in commercial aircraft may rarely fly in a plane; a dairy farmer sells his milk to the dairy and then buys margarine at the local store. The textbook's message: "Self-sufficiency breeds inefficiency." A "jack-of-all-trades" (a touche-à-tout, who does everything alone) is not an efficient producer.

Division of labor: specialization of people

DefinitionDivision of labor (division du travail)

Human specialization: each worker concentrates on one task (or a few tasks) instead of doing everything.

Division of labor increases output for three reasons:

  1. It makes use of differences in ability. People are born with different talents. A gifted athlete such as football star Peyton Manning plays football; a gifted singer like Beyoncé sings. Both produce more than if each tried to do both.
  2. It fosters learning by doing. Even if two people have identical abilities, the one who spends all day on one task becomes skilled at it. As the textbook says, you learn to be a good lawyer by studying and practicing law.
  3. It saves time. A worker who does one job loses no time moving between different jobs, and no time "fumbling around" with tasks he is not trained for.
Example · a bakery in Tunis

A small bakery in Bab Souika has three workers. If each one did everything (knead, shape, bake, sell), they would constantly switch tasks and waste time. Instead, Ali kneads (he is the strongest), Sami watches the oven (he has learned exactly when the bread is ready — learning by doing), and Nour serves customers (she is fast with change and friendly). Together they produce many more loaves per day.

Geographic specialization

DefinitionGeographic specialization (spécialisation géographique)

Regions and nations produce the goods their resources are best suited for, then trade their surpluses with other regions and nations.

Textbook: Nebraska grows wheat and Florida grows oranges, then they swap. Internationally, the United States produces commercial aircraft and computers and exchanges them for video recorders from Japan, bananas from Honduras and woven baskets from Thailand. Both human and geographic specialization are needed for efficiency.

8. Use of money

Specialization creates a problem: if you produce only one thing, you must exchange to get everything else. Money makes this exchange easy.

DefinitionMedium of exchange (moyen d'échange)

Any item that sellers generally accept and buyers generally use to pay for goods and services. Money is first and foremost a medium of exchange.

DefinitionBarter (troc)

Swapping goods directly for other goods, without money — for example, wheat for oranges.

DefinitionCoincidence of wants (double coïncidence des besoins)

The situation in which each of two traders has what the other wants, and wants what the other has. Barter works only when there is a coincidence of wants.

The textbook's example (Figure 2.1): Nebraska has surplus wheat and wants potatoes. Florida has surplus oranges and wants wheat. Idaho has surplus potatoes and wants oranges. Look at each pair: Nebraska wants Idaho's potatoes, but Idaho does not want wheat. Florida wants Nebraska's wheat, but Nebraska does not want oranges. Idaho wants Florida's oranges, but Florida does not want potatoes. There is no coincidence of wants, so barter stalls. Money solves it in three steps:

  1. Nebraska sells its wheat to Florida for money.
  2. Nebraska uses that money to buy potatoes from Idaho.
  3. Idaho uses the money to buy oranges from Florida.

Everybody gets what they wanted, and the money returns to Florida, where it started.

Same logic, Tunisian version

Sfax has surplus olive oil and wants dates. Tozeur has surplus dates and wants oranges. Nabeul has surplus oranges and wants olive oil. Check each pair: Tozeur does not want oil, Sfax does not want oranges, Nabeul does not want dates — no coincidence of wants, so barter fails. With dinars: (1) Sfax sells olive oil to Nabeul for dinars; (2) Sfax uses those dinars to buy dates from Tozeur; (3) Tozeur uses the dinars to buy oranges from Nabeul. Sfax plays Nebraska's role, Nabeul plays Florida's, and Tozeur plays Idaho's.

What can be money? In history, people used cattle, cigarettes, shells, stones and pieces of metal as money. To serve as money, an item must pass only one test: it must be generally acceptable to sellers in exchange for goods and services. Money is socially defined: whatever society accepts as money is money. Today, most economies use paper currency and coins, like the Tunisian dinar (1 dinar = 1,000 millimes). Between countries, currency markets let people exchange dollars, euros, yen, pounds or dinars, so international trade can happen without barter.

Understand the quote

"We want money only to part with it." Most people do not want money for itself: you cannot eat a 20-dinar note. We want money because we can give it away in exchange for the goods and services we really want. That is exactly what a medium of exchange is.

9. Active but limited government

The market system has some built-in weaknesses, called market failures (défaillances du marché). Government can help the market system work better in these cases — for example with rules, public services and help for the poor. The details come in Chapter 4. For now, remember the phrase: active but limited government.

Quick review 2.2

The market system uses advanced capital goods extensively. Specialization (division of labor and geographic specialization) raises efficiency. Money makes possible the exchange that specialization requires. Government plays an active but limited role.

Show the model answer

To get all the other goods he wants, he must exchange his surplus with other specialists. So specialization and exchange always go together.

(b) With barter, a trade happens only if there is a coincidence of wants: each trader must have what the other wants and want what the other has. This is rare (Nebraska–Florida–Idaho: no pair matches), so trade stalls. Money is a medium of exchange that everyone accepts. You sell your product to whoever wants it, receive money, and use the money to buy from whoever has what you want. The two sides of the trade no longer need to match.

Question 1: what will be produced? The profit test

In a market system, goods and services that can be produced at a continuing profit will be produced. Goods and services whose production brings a continuing loss will not be produced.

Economic profit (or loss) = Total revenue (TR) − Total cost (TC)

Be careful: economists measure total cost as the total opportunity cost of all the resources used. It includes:

  • wages paid for labor,
  • rent paid for land,
  • interest paid for capital,
  • and a payment to the entrepreneur — the normal reward needed to keep the entrepreneur in this business.

So if TR = TC, the entrepreneur already receives a normal reward. Any extra is economic profit (bénéfice économique). A shortfall is an economic loss (perte).

SituationWhat it meansWhat happens to the industry
TR > TC (continuing economic profit)Society wants more of this productExpands: existing firms grow and new firms enter
TR = TC (normal profit)Resources earn exactly their opportunity costStays the same size
TR < TC (continuing economic loss)Society wants less of this productContracts: firms shrink or leave the industry

Consumer sovereignty and dollar votes

DefinitionConsumer sovereignty (souveraineté du consommateur)

The idea that consumers are "in command" of the economy: by spending their income on what they are most willing and able to buy, they decide which goods and services are produced.

DefinitionDollar votes (vote monétaire)

The money consumers spend on a product. Each purchase is like a vote for that product. If a product gets enough dollar votes to be profitable, businesses produce it. In Tunisia you could call them "dinar votes".

Through dollar votes, consumers direct resources toward the industries that meet their wants. This means firms are not as free as they think. A firm may choose to make anything, but if consumers do not buy it, the firm suffers losses and eventually goes bankrupt. Entrepreneurs are at the helm (au gouvernail), but they must follow the consumers' orders.

The same is true for resource suppliers. Car workers have jobs because people buy cars. There are few professors of early Latin because few people want to study early Latin. In Tunisia, call centres hire many French-speaking agents because their French-speaking customers buy their services.

Question 2: HOW will goods be produced? Least-cost production

In a market system, firms produce in the combinations and ways that minimize the cost per unit of output. Why? Because of competition. A firm that uses an expensive method must charge more or earn less. Competitors who use cheaper methods can sell at lower prices and take its customers. So competition eliminates high-cost producers.

Least-cost production means two things: using the right mix of labor and capital (given their prices and productivity) and choosing the best location for the plant.

DefinitionEconomic efficiency (efficacité économique)

Obtaining a given output with the smallest possible input of scarce resources, when those inputs are measured in money (dollars, dinars). In other words: producing at the least cost.

The most efficient technique depends on two things:

  1. the available technology — which combinations of resources can produce the output;
  2. the prices of the resources — which of those combinations is cheapest.

Table 2.1: three ways to make $15 of bar soap

A firm can make $15 worth of bar soap with three different techniques. Each technique uses a different mix of the four resources. Resource prices: labor $2, land $1, capital $3, entrepreneurial ability $3 per unit.

How to compute the cost of a technique:

Cost = (units × price) for each resource, then add the four results
  • Technique 1: 4 × $2 + 1 × $1 + 1 × $3 + 1 × $3 = $8 + $1 + $3 + $3 = $15
  • Technique 2: 2 × $2 + 3 × $1 + 1 × $3 + 1 × $3 = $4 + $3 + $3 + $3 = $13
  • Technique 3: 1 × $2 + 4 × $1 + 2 × $3 + 1 × $3 = $2 + $4 + $6 + $3 = $15

All three techniques produce the same output ($15 of soap). Technique 2 uses only $13 of resources, so it is the most efficient (least-cost) technique. With technique 2, the firm earns an economic profit of $15 − $13 = $2. With technique 1 or 3, it earns $15 − $15 = $0 economic profit. Competition will force producers to use technique 2: a firm using technique 1 or 3 could be undersold by firms using technique 2.

Common mistake

Do not choose the technique by counting units. Techniques 1 and 2 both use 7 units of resources, yet technique 2 is cheaper. You cannot add a unit of land ($1) to a unit of capital ($3) as if they were the same thing. Efficiency is measured in money cost.

Technology stays the same, but labor becomes much cheaper. Recompute every technique with labor at $0.50 (other prices unchanged):

Change the resource prices, and the least-cost technique can change. The market automatically economizes on the expensive (scarce) resources.
Tunisian example · a bakery choosing how to knead

A bakery in Tunis must knead the same amount of dough every day. Method A: 3 workers knead by hand. Method B: 1 worker with a kneading machine that costs 60 TND a day to rent. If a worker cost only 25 TND a day: A = 3 × 25 = 75 TND; B = 25 + 60 = 85 TND → hand kneading becomes cheaper. Same technology, different prices, different best method.

Questions 3–5: WHO gets the output, how the system ADAPTS, and how it PROGRESSES

Question 3: who will get the output?

In a market system, products go to consumers according to their ability and willingness to pay the market price. The textbook's example: if a sailboat costs $3,000, then the people who are able and willing to pay $3,000 "sail, sail away". People who cannot or will not pay that price do not get it.

  • Ability to pay depends on your income and on the prices of products.
  • Willingness to pay depends on your preferences (tastes).

And what decides your income? Two things:

  1. the quantities of property and human resources you supply (how many hours you work, how much land or capital you own);
  2. the prices those resources get in the resource market: wages for labor, rent for land, interest for capital, profit for entrepreneurial ability.
Income = quantity of resources supplied × price of each resource
Textbook example · lawyer vs recreation worker

A lawyer earns $200 an hour. A recreational worker earns $10 an hour. Both work the same number of hours. The lawyer's income is $200 ÷ $10 = 20 times larger, so the lawyer can take 20 times as much of society's output.

Tunisian example

Imagine a specialist doctor in Tunis who earns about 100 TND an hour and a café waiter who earns about 5 TND an hour. With the same hours, the doctor's income is 100 ÷ 5 = 20 times larger. In the market system, the doctor can buy 20 times more goods and services. Who gets the output depends on the resources you own and their prices.

Question 4: how will the system accommodate change?

Economies change all the time: consumer preferences, technology and resource supplies change. The market system adapts automatically. Follow the textbook's example step by step: consumers decide they want more fruit juice and less milk.

  1. Spending shifts. Consumers spend more on juice and less on milk.
  2. Prices and profits change. In the juice industry, prices and profits rise. In the milk industry, prices and profits fall (losses may appear).
  3. Self-interest reacts. Existing juice firms expand and new firms enter the juice industry. Some milk producers shrink or leave the milk industry.
  4. Resources move. Higher profits let the juice industry pay more to attract resources — land, workers, machines — away from less urgent uses, like milk.
  5. The resource market follows. Expanding industries hire more workers and pay good wages; contracting industries hire fewer. Income flows guide resources from the contracting industry to the expanding one.

The market system works like a gigantic communications system. This is called the guiding function of prices (fonction d'orientation des prix).

DefinitionGuiding (directing) function of prices

The ability of price and profit changes to signal changes in what society wants and to move resources from industries that are contracting to industries that are expanding — with no planner needed.

Without this function, a government planning board would have to decide which industry should grow and which should shrink, and order resources to move.

Tunisian example · bottled water

Suppose Tunisian families buy more bottled water and fewer soft drinks. Bottled-water prices and profits rise, so bottling companies add new production lines and new firms enter. Soft-drink sales fall, so soft-drink firms cut production. Trucks, plastic, workers and money move from soft drinks to bottled water. No minister ordered this — the price and profit signals did.

Question 5: how will the system promote progress?

Society wants a higher standard of living over time. The market promotes progress in two ways.

(a) Technological advance (progrès technique). The market gives a strong incentive to innovate:

  • A firm that introduces a popular new product earns revenue and profit at the expense of its rivals.
  • A firm that finds a cheaper production method can cut its prices and gain sales.
  • Rivals must follow the innovator or suffer losses. So new technology spreads quickly.
DefinitionCreative destruction (destruction créatrice)

The process by which the creation of new products and new production methods completely destroys the market positions of firms that stay attached to the old products and old methods. (The economist Joseph Schumpeter made this idea famous.)

Examples of creative destruction

Textbook: compact discs (CDs) demolished vinyl records; then MP3 players like the iPod and digital music began replacing CDs. Today: streaming services replaced CDs and DVDs; smartphones replaced separate cameras, GPS devices, alarm clocks and torches. Tunisia: the "publinets" (internet cafés) that were everywhere in Tunisian towns have mostly closed because almost everyone now has a smartphone with mobile internet; many neighbourhood photo studios disappeared for the same reason. The new is created; the old is destroyed.

(b) Capital accumulation (accumulation du capital). Most technological advances need more capital goods (new machines, new factories). How does the market get them? Through dollar votes for capital goods:

  • Entrepreneurs and business owners use part of their profit income to buy capital goods.
  • Households save part of their income. Firms attract these savings by paying interest (on loans) or by selling shares.
Quick review 2.3

The output mix is decided by profits, which depend on consumer preferences. Profits make industries expand; losses make them contract. Competition forces least-cost production. Competitive markets reallocate resources when tastes, technology or resource supplies change. Income and prices decide who gets the output. Competitive markets reward technological advance and capital accumulation, which raise living standards.

Common mistake

"Creative destruction is bad because firms die." The textbook presents it as a source of progress: old firms and jobs disappear, but new, better products and new jobs appear, and society as a whole becomes richer.

The "invisible hand"

In 1776, the Scottish economist Adam Smith published The Wealth of Nations (La Richesse des nations). He noticed something surprising: in a market system, private interests and social interests are united. Firms and resource suppliers each pursue their own interest. But, inside a competitive market system, they at the same time promote the interest of society — as if they were guided by an "invisible hand".

DefinitionInvisible hand (main invisible)

The tendency of competition to make firms and resource suppliers, who seek only their own self-interest, automatically and unintentionally promote the interest of society as a whole.

The textbook's examples:

  • Firms try to build better products to increase their profits. Result: society's well-being rises.
  • Firms use the least costly combination of resources to increase profits. Result: resources are saved and can be used to produce other things society wants.

The key is competition: it controls and guides self-interest so that self-interest automatically and unintentionally serves society's best interest.

Example · the baker in Tunis

Adam Smith wrote that we expect our dinner not from the kindness of the butcher, the brewer or the baker, but from their concern for their own interest. Think of a baker in Tunis who wakes up at 4 a.m. He does not do it out of love for the neighbourhood: he wants to earn a living. But because he competes with other bakeries, he must bake good bread early and sell it at a fair price. Result: the neighbourhood has fresh bread every morning. Nobody planned it — the invisible hand did.

The three virtues of the market system

VirtueWhat it means
1. EfficiencyThe market guides resources to the goods society wants most, forces firms to use efficient (least-cost) techniques, and encourages new, better techniques.
2. IncentivesThe market rewards skills, hard work and innovation. Entrepreneurs who take risks successfully are rewarded with profit. People work harder when they keep the reward.
3. FreedomThe market coordinates economic activity without coercion (without force). People choose their jobs, businesses and purchases. This is the main non-economic argument for the market system.
Memory trick

The three virtues spell E-I-F: Efficiency, Incentives, Freedom — "Every Investor is Free".

Common mistake

The invisible hand does not mean that firms want to help society, and it does not mean the market is perfect. Firms help society unintentionally, because competition forces them to. And the textbook says clearly that no economic system is flawless: the market's shortcomings come in Chapter 4.

The demise of the command systems

The Soviet Union, the countries of eastern Europe and China before its reforms all used central planning. These systems faced two problems they could not overcome: the coordination problem and the incentive problem.

1. The coordination problem

DefinitionCoordination problem (problème de coordination)

The difficulty for central planners of coordinating the millions of individual decisions of consumers, resource suppliers and businesses, so that each industry gets exactly the inputs it needs at the right time.

Take the textbook's example. Planners set a target: a factory must produce 1,000 tractors. To do it, the factory needs labor, machinery, electric power, steel, tires, glass, paint and transportation. Each of these inputs comes from another industry — which also needs inputs from others. The outputs of one industry are the inputs of other industries. So if one industry misses its target, the damage spreads like a chain reaction:

As the planned economies grew larger and more complex, coordination became harder, not easier. Bottlenecks and production stoppages became normal. To simplify their job, planners often suppressed product variety — fewer models, fewer choices.

There was a second coordination difficulty: the lack of a reliable success indicator. In a market economy, success is measured by profit, which depends on demand, efficiency and product quality. In a command economy, success was measured by quantitative production targets. Managers only had to reach the number. So they sacrificed quality and variety.

Textbook example · the nails story
Target measured in…What the nail factory didResult
Weight (tons of nails)Made only large, heavy nails — the easiest way to reach a weight targetShortage of small nails
Quantity (thousands of nails)Made only small nails — the easiest way to reach a number targetShortage of large nails

The textbook says this is precisely what happened. A profit-seeking firm in a market would make the mix of nails that customers want, because that is what sells.

2. The incentive problem

DefinitionIncentive problem (problème d'incitation)

In a command system, people had little reason to produce what consumers wanted, to fix shortages and surpluses, or to innovate, because rewards came from meeting plan targets and climbing the party hierarchy — not from prices and profits.

  • Shortages and surpluses. Planners chose the output mix. When they guessed wrong about how many cars, shoes, shirts or chickens people wanted at the government's prices, there were persistent shortages (pénuries) of some goods and surpluses (excédents) of others.
  • No reason to adjust. Managers were rewarded for meeting their targets, so they had no incentive to correct these mistakes. With no price and profit signals, some goods were unavailable while others sat in warehouses for years.
  • No entrepreneurship. There was no profit motive and no reward for innovation. To get ahead, people had to climb the hierarchy of the Communist Party, which brought better housing, better health care and access to special stores. This did not encourage technological advance.

P10Open question

Why did planners in command economies worry so much about the availability of inputs, while firms in market economies rarely do?

Show the model answer

Command economy: inputs are given to each enterprise by the plan. Each industry depends on others that are also planned. If one supplier misses its target, there is no automatic correction: no price rises to call in new supply, and no other firm is free to step in. The shortage spreads in a chain reaction (iron → steel → tractors). So planners had to worry about every single input.

Market economy: if an input becomes scarce, its price rises. The higher price is a signal: existing suppliers produce more, new suppliers enter because it is profitable, and users economize or switch to substitutes. There are also many independent suppliers competing, so a firm can buy from another one. Prices and profits coordinate supply automatically, so shortages are usually small and temporary.

The circular flow model (key graph)

The circular flow diagram is a simple picture of how a market economy works. It is one of the most important graphs of the chapter. To make it simple, it groups all private decision makers into two groups and all markets into two markets.

DefinitionCircular flow diagram (circuit économique)

An illustration showing the flow of resources, goods and services, and money (income and spending) between households and businesses through the resource market and the product market.

The two groups of decision makers

  • Households (ménages): individuals and families. In this model, households own all the economic resources — directly, as workers and entrepreneurs, or indirectly, as owners of corporations (through shares).
  • Businesses (entreprises): firms that buy resources and use them to produce goods and services.

The two markets

DefinitionResource market (marché des facteurs de production)

The market where households sell resources (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurial ability) — or the services of those resources — and businesses buy them.

In the resource market, resources flow from households to businesses. Money flows the other way: businesses pay for the resources. These payments are costs for businesses, but the very same payments are income for households: wages (for labor), rent (for land), interest (for capital) and profit (for entrepreneurial ability).

DefinitionProduct market (marché des biens et services)

The market where businesses sell the goods and services they produce, and households buy them.

In the product market, goods and services flow from businesses to households. Households use their (limited) money income to buy them. This spending, called consumption expenditures (dépenses de consommation), becomes the sales revenue (recettes) of businesses. Businesses then compare their revenue with their costs to see whether they are making a profit.

Both groups are buyers AND sellers. Households sell resources and buy products. Businesses buy resources and sell products.

Reading the diagram: real flows and money flows

DefinitionReal flow vs money flow

A real flow (flux réel) is a flow of actual things: resources going to businesses, and goods and services going to households. A money flow (flux monétaire) is the payment that goes the opposite way: income going to households, and spending going to businesses.

MarketSellersBuyersReal flow (what is traded)Money flow (payment)
Resource market (top)HouseholdsBusinessesLand, labor, capital, entrepreneurial ability → to businessesWages, rents, interest, profits → to households (costs for businesses = income for households)
Product market (bottom)BusinessesHouseholdsGoods and services → to householdsConsumption expenditures → to businesses (= their revenue)

Directions (as in the textbook figure): the real flow of resources and finished goods and services runs counterclockwise. The money flow of income and consumption expenditures runs clockwise. The two flows always go in opposite directions, because every time something real moves one way, a payment moves the other way.

Tunisian example · follow one family around the circle

Amira works at a call centre in Tunis. She sells her labor in the resource market and receives a wage of 1,300 TND a month. For the call centre, this wage is a cost; for Amira's household it is income.

Her father owns a small shop space that he rents to a pharmacy: he sells the use of a property resource and receives rent. Her uncle's savings are lent to a business through the bank, and he receives interest. Her aunt owns shares in a company and receives part of its profits.

Now the family spends this income in the product market: food at the supermarket, a phone plan, transport, coffee with friends. Goods and services flow to the household; the money (consumption expenditures) flows to the businesses as revenue. The businesses use that revenue to pay wages, rent, interest and profits again — and the circle continues.

How to draw it in an exam (5 steps)

(1) Draw Businesses on the left and Households on the right. (2) Draw the Resource market at the top and the Product market at the bottom. (3) Inner ring, counterclockwise: households → resource market → businesses ("land, labor, capital, entrepreneurial ability"), then businesses → product market → households ("goods and services"). (4) Outer ring, clockwise: businesses → resource market → households ("costs" → "money income: wages, rents, interest, profits"), then households → product market → businesses ("consumption expenditures" → "revenue"). (5) Label every arrow.

Common mistake

Many students write "households sell in the product market". No: households buy in the product market and sell in the resource market. Another mistake: putting a manager's salary or the price of farmland in the product market. Salaries (labor) and land are resources, so their prices are set in the resource market.

Good to know (beyond the quiz)

This simple model leaves out the government and other countries, and it assumes households spend all their income. Later chapters add these pieces.

Last Word: "Shuffling the deck"

At the end of the chapter, the economist Donald Boudreaux uses a deck of playing cards to explain why the market system is so impressive.

  • Shuffle a normal deck of 52 cards. The order you get has almost certainly never existed before. Why? Because 52 cards can be arranged in about 8.066 × 1067 different ways — an unimaginably large number.
  • Even only 20 items can be arranged in 2,432,902,008,176,640,000 ways (more than 2 billion billion).
  • The real world has tens of billions of resources: workers, machines, buildings, land, raw materials. If they were arranged at random, the result would be useless — we would starve.
  • Yet we live in one of the tiny number of productive arrangements. Why? Thanks to private property. Each owner looks for the use of his resource that promises the highest reward. Billions of small daily decisions, each one adjusting to the others, produce a complex and productive arrangement. Boudreaux calls this "mutual accommodation".
DefinitionMutual accommodation

The process by which millions of property owners, each seeking the highest reward for what they own, continually adjust to each other's decisions, so that resources end up in a productive arrangement that nobody designed.

Tunisian example · an empty shop on a busy street

The owner of an empty ground-floor shop in Tunis can rent it to a café, a pharmacy or a phone shop. He rents it to whoever pays the highest rent — and the one who can pay the most is usually the business whose customers value that location most. Without any plan, the shop goes to a productive use. Multiply this by billions of decisions, and you get the "shuffled deck" that actually works.

Link to the rest of the chapter

This is the invisible hand again: nobody arranges the whole economy, but private property, self-interest and prices arrange it for us. It also shows why central planners failed: no planning board could test even a tiny part of all the possible arrangements.

Recap and key terms

What to remember for Tuesday

  • Every society needs an economic system. Systems differ in who owns the resources and how activity is coordinated.
  • Command system (socialism/communism): government ownership + central planning board. Market system (capitalism): private ownership + markets and prices. Laissez-faire = pure capitalism; real economies are mixed, but the market dominates.
  • Market characteristics: private property, freedom of enterprise and choice, self-interest, competition, markets and prices, technology and capital goods, specialization, use of money, active but limited government.
  • Competition = 2+ independent buyers and sellers + free entry and exit. It is the basic regulatory force; markets and prices are the coordinating mechanism.
  • Division of labor raises output: differences in ability, learning by doing, saving time. Barter needs a coincidence of wants; money (a medium of exchange) removes that need. Its only test: general acceptability.
  • Five questions (Ch 1 taught three): what, how, who, accommodating change, promoting progress.
  • What: goods produced at a continuing profit (TR > TC). Profit → expansion; loss → contraction. Consumers rule through dollar votes (consumer sovereignty).
  • How: least-cost technique (units × prices, add, compare). If a resource gets cheaper, firms use more of it (Table 2.1: T2 = $13 is best; with labor at $0.50, T1 = $9 is best).
  • Who: ability and willingness to pay; income = resources supplied × their prices (lawyer $200/h vs $10/h → 20 times).
  • Change: guiding function of prices (juice up, milk down). Progress: technological advance, creative destruction, capital accumulation (profits + savings).
  • Invisible hand (Adam Smith, 1776): competition turns self-interest into social interest. Three virtues: efficiency, incentives, freedom.
  • Command systems failed because of the coordination problem (chain reactions, no success indicator — nails) and the incentive problem (shortages, surpluses, no innovation). Two Koreas: $1,800 vs $24,500 per person.
  • Circular flow: resource market (households sell, businesses buy) at the top; product market (businesses sell, households buy) at the bottom. Real flows counterclockwise, money flows clockwise.

Glossary

Economic system
The institutional arrangements and coordinating mechanism a society uses to respond to the economizing problem (système économique).
Command system
Government owns most property resources; decisions are made through a central economic plan. Also socialism or communism (économie planifiée / dirigée).
Market system
Private ownership of resources, with markets and prices coordinating economic activity. Also capitalism (économie de marché).
Laissez-faire capitalism
Pure capitalism: government only protects private property and provides a legal environment for markets.
Mixed economy
A real-world economy that combines markets with a substantial government role (économie mixte).
Private property
The right of private persons and firms to obtain, own, control, use, sell and bequeath land, capital and other property (propriété privée).
Intellectual property
Property rights over creations of the mind: patents, copyrights, trademarks (propriété intellectuelle).
Freedom of enterprise
Freedom of firms to obtain resources, produce the goods they choose and sell them in the markets they choose (liberté d'entreprise).
Freedom of choice
Freedom of owners to use their property and money as they wish, of workers to enter any line of work they are qualified for, and of consumers to buy what they want within their budgets (liberté de choix).
Self-interest
Each economic unit pursues what is best for itself; the motivating force of the market system. Not the same as selfishness (intérêt personnel).
Competition
Many independent buyers and sellers, with freedom to enter and leave markets (concurrence).
Market
An institution or mechanism that brings buyers (demanders) and sellers (suppliers) into contact (marché).
Capital goods
Man-made tools, machines and buildings used to produce other goods (biens d'équipement).
Specialization
Using the resources of a person, firm, region or nation to produce one or a few goods, then exchanging (spécialisation).
Division of labor
Specialization of workers in separate tasks (division du travail).
Geographic specialization
Regions and nations producing what their resources suit best and trading the surplus (spécialisation géographique).
Medium of exchange
An item generally accepted in payment for goods and services; the main function of money (moyen d'échange).
Money
Any item that is generally acceptable to sellers in exchange for goods and services. It is socially defined (monnaie).
Barter
Exchanging goods directly for other goods, without money (troc).
Coincidence of wants
Each trader has what the other wants and wants what the other has; required for barter (double coïncidence des besoins).
Market failure
A built-in shortcoming of the market system that government can help correct (défaillance du marché). Details in Chapter 4.
Economic cost
The total opportunity cost of all resources used: wages, rent, interest and a normal payment to the entrepreneur.
Economic profit
Total revenue minus total (economic) cost. Positive → industry expands; negative (loss) → industry contracts.
Consumer sovereignty
Consumers decide, through their spending, which goods and services are produced (souveraineté du consommateur).
Dollar votes
Consumer spending seen as "votes" for products; enough votes make a product profitable (vote monétaire).
Economic efficiency
Producing a given output with the least input of scarce resources measured in money; least-cost production (efficacité économique).
Guiding function of prices
Price and profit changes signal society's wants and move resources from contracting to expanding industries.
Technological advance
New products and better production methods, encouraged by profit (progrès technique).
Creative destruction
New products and methods destroy the market positions of firms tied to old ones (destruction créatrice).
Capital accumulation
Increasing the stock of capital goods, financed by reinvested profits and household saving (accumulation du capital).
Invisible hand
Adam Smith's idea that competition leads self-interested people to promote society's interest unintentionally (main invisible).
Coordination problem
Planners' difficulty in coordinating millions of interdependent decisions; leads to chain reactions and bottlenecks.
Incentive problem
Without prices and profits, planners and managers had no reason to fix shortages and surpluses or to innovate.
Circular flow diagram
A model of the flows of resources, products, income and spending between households and businesses (circuit économique).
Households
Individuals and families; they own all resources, sell them in the resource market and buy products (ménages).
Businesses
Firms that buy resources and sell goods and services (entreprises).
Resource market
Where households sell, and businesses buy, land, labor, capital and entrepreneurial ability (marché des facteurs de production).
Product market
Where businesses sell, and households buy, goods and services (marché des biens et services).
Real flow / money flow
Real flow: resources and products (counterclockwise). Money flow: income and consumption expenditures (clockwise).
