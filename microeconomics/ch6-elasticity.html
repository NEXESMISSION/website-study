Price elasticity of demand: how strongly do buyers react?

The textbook opens the chapter with four puzzles. Read them slowly: by the end of this page you will be able to answer all four.

  • When the price of ocean cruises rises, buyers cut their purchases a lot. When the price of gasoline (petrol) rises, they cut their purchases only a little. Why?
  • When the price of chicken rises, farmers greatly increase their output. When the price of gold rises, the output of gold increases only slightly. Why?
  • When people's incomes rise, they buy many more books, but only a little more milk. Why?
  • Most buyers pay less than the most they would be willing to pay, and most sellers receive more than the least they would accept. How much do they gain?

All four puzzles are about size, not direction. The law of demand (Chapter 3) says that when the price of a product rises, the quantity demanded falls. It does not say by how much. Elasticity measures this "how much".

DefinitionPrice elasticity of demand (élasticité-prix de la demande)

A measure of how responsive (sensitive) consumers are to a change in the price of a product. It tells us by how much the quantity demanded changes when the price of that product changes.

  • Demand is elastic (demande élastique) when a modest price change causes a large change in the quantity demanded. Textbook example: restaurant meals. If restaurants raise their prices, many families simply eat at home.
  • Demand is inelastic (demande inélastique) when even a large price change causes only a small change in the quantity demanded. Textbook example: toothpaste. If toothpaste costs more, you still brush your teeth.

The word comes from physics: an elastic material (élastique) stretches a lot when you pull it; a rigid one hardly moves. In economics, the "pull" is the price change, and the "stretch" is the change in quantity.

The elasticity formula

Economists measure elasticity with a number called the price elasticity coefficient, written Ed ("E" for elasticity, "d" for demand). It compares two percentage changes:

Ed = (% change in quantity demanded of X) ÷ (% change in price of X)

A percentage change is a change divided by a base value. For example, if a price goes from 10 TND to 12 TND, the change is 2 TND, and 2 ÷ 10 = 0.20 = 20%.

Read the coefficient like this: Ed = 3 means "a 1% change in the price causes a 3% change in the quantity demanded" (in the opposite direction). Ed = 0.5 means "a 1% change in the price causes only a 0.5% change in the quantity demanded".

A problem: is the price going up or down?

Which value should be the base? If we always use the starting value, we get a strange problem. Look at the same two prices:

  • From $4 to $5, the price rises by $1 ÷ $4 = 25%.
  • From $5 to $4, the price falls by $1 ÷ $5 = 20%.

Same two prices, two different percentages! Quantities have the same problem: from 10 units to 20 units is +100%, but from 20 units back to 10 is only −50%. So the elasticity between the same two points would depend on the direction of the move. The solution is the midpoint formula.

DefinitionMidpoint formula (formule du point milieu)

A way to compute percentage changes that uses the average of the two values (the midpoint) as the base, instead of the starting value. It gives the same elasticity whether the price rises or falls between two points. The textbook uses it for all elasticity coefficients, and so should you in the exam.

Ed = [ change in quantity ÷ ((Q1 + Q2) ÷ 2) ] ÷ [ change in price ÷ ((P1 + P2) ÷ 2) ]
Worked example · the textbook numbers, step by step

The price of a product falls from $5 to $4. The quantity demanded rises from 10 to 20 units.

Step 1 — % change in quantity. Change = 20 − 10 = 10 units. Average quantity = (10 + 20) ÷ 2 = 15. Percentage change = 10 ÷ 15 = 0.667 = 66.7%.

Step 2 — % change in price. Change = $5 − $4 = $1. Average price = ($5 + $4) ÷ 2 = $4.50. Percentage change = 1 ÷ 4.5 = 0.222 = 22.2%.

Step 3 — divide. Ed = 66.7% ÷ 22.2% = 0.667 ÷ 0.222 = 3.

Step 4 — interpret. A 1% change in price causes a 3% change in quantity demanded. Quantity reacts much more than price, so demand is elastic in this price range.

Why is the midpoint formula better? Compare it with the "starting value" method for the same two points:

Tunisian example 1 · espresso in a Sousse café

A café raises the price of an espresso from 1.800 TND to 2.200 TND. Its daily sales fall from 260 to 220 cups.

% change in Q = 40 ÷ ((260 + 220) ÷ 2) = 40 ÷ 240 = 16.7%.
% change in P = 0.400 ÷ ((1.800 + 2.200) ÷ 2) = 0.400 ÷ 2.000 = 20%.

Ed = 16.67% ÷ 20% = 0.83. The quantity reacts less than the price: demand is inelastic. Many customers are attached to their café and to their daily coffee.

Tunisian example 2 · a mobile data pack

A phone operator cuts the price of its 20 GB monthly pack from 25 TND to 20 TND. Subscriptions rise from 8,000 to 12,000.

% change in Q = 4,000 ÷ ((8,000 + 12,000) ÷ 2) = 4,000 ÷ 10,000 = 40%.
% change in P = 5 ÷ ((25 + 20) ÷ 2) = 5 ÷ 22.5 = 22.2%.

Ed = 40 ÷ 22.2 = 1.8: elastic. Customers can easily move between Ooredoo, Orange and Tunisie Télécom, so they react strongly to price differences.

Why percentages and not simple changes?

Reason 1 — the units would change the answer. The textbook example: the price of a bag of popcorn falls from $3 to $2, and sales rise from 60 to 100 bags.

  • Measured in dollars, a price change of 1 unit ($1) brings a change of 40 bags. That looks very responsive.
  • Measured in pennies (cents), the same price change is 100 units (100 cents) for the same 40 bags. That looks hardly responsive at all.

It is the same market! Percentages have no units, so they remove the problem: Ed = (40 ÷ 80) ÷ (1 ÷ 2.5) = 0.50 ÷ 0.40 = 1.25, whether you count in dollars or in cents. In Tunisia it is the same with dinars and millimes: 1 TND = 1,000 millimes, but the percentage change is identical.

Reason 2 — to compare different products. A $1 price rise is tiny for a $10,000 used car (1 ÷ 10,000 = 0.01%) but huge for a $1 soft drink (1 ÷ 1 = 100%). In dinars: 1 TND more on a 20,000 TND used car is a 0.005% rise; 1 TND more on a 1 TND bottle of water is a 100% rise. Percentages put all products on the same scale.

What about the minus sign?

Because of the law of demand, price and quantity demanded always move in opposite directions. One percentage change is positive and the other is negative, so the coefficient is always negative (for example −3). Economists simply ignore the minus sign and use the absolute value (valeur absolue): we write Ed = 3, not −3.

Common mistake · comparing negative numbers

As numbers, −4 is smaller than −2. But in elasticity, an Ed of 4 (from −4) shows greater responsiveness than an Ed of 2 (from −2). Drop the signs first, then compare: 4 > 2, so the first demand is more elastic. Careful: this rule is only for the price elasticity of demand. In section 08 you will see that for cross elasticity and income elasticity, the sign is the whole message, so you must keep it. Supply elasticities (section 07) are positive anyway.

Exam alert

Unless the question says otherwise, always use the midpoint formula. Show the three steps (% change in Q, % change in P, divide), and finish with a sentence: "Ed = 1.8 > 1, so demand is elastic in this price range." A number without its interpretation loses points. Rounding: this page usually shows percentages rounded to one decimal, but each coefficient is computed from the unrounded values and rounded only at the end (usually to two decimals). Do the same on your calculator. If you divide percentages that are already rounded, the last digit can change: in Key Question 8, 18.2% ÷ 9.5% gives 1.92, but the exact answer is 1.91.

Show the full solution

(a) Midpoint. % change in Q = 200 ÷ ((1,100 + 900) ÷ 2) = 200 ÷ 1,000 = 20%. % change in P = 2 ÷ ((10 + 12) ÷ 2) = 2 ÷ 11 = 18.2%. Ed = 20 ÷ 18.2 = 1.10. 02

Interpreting Ed: elastic, inelastic, unit elastic and the two extremes

The magic number is 1. If quantity changes by a larger percentage than price, Ed is above 1. If it changes by a smaller percentage, Ed is below 1. Here are the three main cases, with the textbook's examples of a 2% price cut.

DefinitionElastic demand (demande élastique): Ed > 1

The percentage change in quantity demanded is larger than the percentage change in price. Textbook example: a 2% cut in the price of cut flowers raises the quantity demanded by 4%, so Ed = 4% ÷ 2% = 2.

DefinitionInelastic demand (demande inélastique): Ed < 1

The percentage change in quantity demanded is smaller than the percentage change in price. Textbook example: a 2% cut in the price of coffee raises the quantity demanded by only 1%, so Ed = 1% ÷ 2% = 0.5.

DefinitionUnit elasticity (élasticité unitaire): Ed = 1

The percentage change in quantity demanded is equal to the percentage change in price. Textbook example: a 2% cut in the price of chocolate raises the quantity demanded by 2%, so Ed = 2% ÷ 2% = 1.

The two extreme cases

DefinitionPerfectly inelastic demand (demande parfaitement inélastique): Ed = 0

A change in price causes no change at all in the quantity demanded. The demand curve is a vertical line. Textbook examples: an acute diabetic's demand for insulin, and an addict's demand for heroin. Whatever the price, they want the same quantity.

DefinitionPerfectly elastic demand (demande parfaitement élastique): Ed = ∞ (infinite)

At one price, buyers will take any quantity; at a price even slightly higher, they buy nothing. A tiny price cut would make buyers increase their purchases from zero to all they can get. The demand curve is a horizontal line. Textbook example: the demand facing a single firm in a purely competitive market, such as one wheat farmer among thousands. If he asks one cent more than the market price, buyers go to his neighbors.

Using Ed to predict

Once you know Ed, you can predict the change in quantity: % change in Q = Ed × % change in P (in the opposite direction).

Worked example · two predictions

Petrol. The textbook estimates the short-run Ed of gasoline at about 0.2. If the price of petrol rises by 10%, the quantity demanded falls by about 0.2 × 10% = 2%. People still drive to work.

Croissants. Suppose a café's croissants have Ed = 2. If the café cuts their price by 5%, the quantity demanded rises by about 2 × 5% = 10%.

Consider this · "a bit of a stretch"

The textbook compares elasticity to two kinds of straps. An Ace elastic bandage stretches a lot when you pull it: that is elastic demand (a small price "pull" stretches the quantity a lot). A firm rubber tie-down (the strap used to tie luggage on a car roof) stretches only a little, even when you pull hard: that is inelastic demand (a big price change moves the quantity only a little). Remember the strap, and you remember the definition.

Common mistake · "inelastic" is not "perfectly inelastic"

"Inelastic" does not mean that quantity does not react. It means it reacts less than proportionally (0 < Ed < 1). Cigarettes have Ed ≈ 0.25: a 10% price rise still cuts the quantity by about 2.5%. Only Ed = exactly 0 is perfectly inelastic. In the same way, "elastic" does not mean "perfectly elastic": Ed = 2 is elastic, but the curve is not horizontal.

Exam alert

The dividing line is 1, not 0 and not 0.5. Ed = 0.9 is inelastic; Ed = 1.1 is elastic. Also learn the shapes: vertical = perfectly inelastic (Ed = 0), horizontal = perfectly elastic (Ed = ∞). Students often swap them. Trick: on a vertical line, quantity cannot move at all.

03

The total-revenue test

Elasticity is not only a classroom number. For a business it answers a money question: if I change my price, will I earn more or less?

DefinitionTotal revenue (recette totale, TR)

The total amount of money a seller receives from selling a product in a period: TR = P × Q (price × quantity sold). It is also the total amount that buyers spend on the product.

On a demand graph, total revenue is a rectangle under a point of the demand curve: its height is the price and its width is the quantity. Example: at a price of $2, buyers purchase 10 units, so TR = $2 × 10 = $20.

Two effects pull in opposite directions

When a seller cuts its price, two things happen at the same time:

  1. A loss: every unit it was already selling now brings in less money.
  2. A gain: it sells extra units, because of the law of demand.

Which effect is bigger? It depends on elasticity. If demand is elastic, the quantity reacts strongly, so the gain wins and TR rises. If demand is inelastic, the quantity reacts weakly, so the loss wins and TR falls. This gives a simple test.

DefinitionTotal-revenue test (test de la recette totale)

A way to find out whether demand is elastic or inelastic by watching what happens to total revenue when the price changes:

  • Elastic demand: price and TR move in opposite directions.
  • Inelastic demand: price and TR move in the same direction.
  • Unit-elastic demand: TR does not change when the price changes.

Worked example · case (a) in four steps

Step 1 — TR before and after. Before: $2 × 10 = $20. After: $1 × 40 = $40.

Step 2 — the loss. The 10 units that were already sold now bring $1 less each: 10 × $1 = $10 lost (the red rectangle).

Step 3 — the gain. 30 extra units are sold at the new price of $1: 30 × $1 = $30 gained (the green rectangle).

Step 4 — conclusion. Net change = $30 − $10 = +$20, so TR rose from $20 to $40. The price fell and TR rose: they moved in opposite directions, so demand is elastic. Check with the midpoint formula: (30 ÷ 25) ÷ (1 ÷ 1.5) = 1.2 ÷ 0.667 = 1.8 > 1 ✓.

In case (b), the opposite happens: the price cut loses $30 on the 10 old units and gains only $10 on the 10 new ones. TR falls from $40 to $20. Price and TR moved in the same direction, so demand is inelastic (midpoint Ed = 0.56). In case (c), the loss ($20) exactly equals the gain ($20), so TR stays at $30: unit elastic. Memory trick · "TR follows the stronger side"

TR = P × Q, and P and Q always move in opposite directions. So TR follows whichever of the two moves by the bigger percentage. Elastic: quantity moves more, so TR follows quantity, which is the opposite of price. Inelastic: price moves more, so TR follows price. Unit elastic: a tie, so TR stays the same. If you remember this sentence, you never need to memorize the table.

Tunisian example 1 · a cinema in Tunis cuts its ticket price

A cinema cuts its ticket price from 12 TND to 9 TND. Weekly tickets sold rise from 2,000 to 3,000.

TR before = 12 × 2,000 = 24,000 TND. TR after = 9 × 3,000 = 27,000 TND. The price fell and TR rose (opposite directions), so demand is elastic. The midpoint formula agrees: (1,000 ÷ 2,500) ÷ (3 ÷ 10.5) = 40% ÷ 28.6% = 1.4 > 1.

Tunisian example 2 · a bakery raises the price of tabouna bread

A bakery raises the price of its tabouna bread from 0.500 TND to 0.600 TND. Daily sales fall only from 400 to 380 loaves.

TR before = 0.500 × 400 = 200 TND. TR after = 0.600 × 380 = 228 TND. The price rose and TR rose (same direction), so demand is inelastic. Midpoint: (20 ÷ 390) ÷ (0.100 ÷ 0.550) = 5.1% ÷ 18.2% = 0.28 < 1. Bread is a necessity, and customers like the bakery near their home.

Common mistakes

(1) The total-revenue test tells you only the type of demand (elastic, inelastic or unit elastic), not the exact coefficient. If the question asks for Ed, use the midpoint formula. (2) The test works for demand only. Along a supply curve, price and quantity move in the same direction, so TR always moves with the price: there is no total-revenue test for supply (section 07). (3) Total revenue is not profit. Profit = TR − costs. A price cut can raise TR and still lower profit if the extra units are expensive to produce.

Exam alert

The classic question: "The price of X rises and total revenue falls. Is demand elastic or inelastic?" Answer in one line: "Price and TR moved in opposite directions, so demand is elastic." Practise the combinations below until they are automatic.

  3. Slope is not elasticity. The slope of the line is the same everywhere (−$1 per 1,000 tickets), but the elasticity is different at every point. Slope uses absolute changes (dollars and tickets). Elasticity uses percentage changes, and the same absolute change is a different percentage at different points.
Along a straight demand line, the slope is constant, but elasticity falls as you move down the line: elastic at high prices, unit elastic in the middle, inelastic at low prices.

Steepness also depends on the units on the axes, which you can change at will. You may compare steepness only for two curves passing through the same point: at that point, the flatter curve is the more elastic one. The two extremes are safe: a vertical line is perfectly inelastic, a horizontal line is perfectly elastic.

What makes demand elastic or inelastic? The four determinants

Why is the demand for salt so inelastic, and the demand for restaurant meals so elastic? The textbook gives four determinants (déterminants) of the price elasticity of demand. They are general rules. A real product can be pulled in different directions by different determinants, so always think about all four.

1. Substitutability: how many good substitutes?

The more good substitutes (produits de substitution) a product has, the more elastic its demand. If its price rises, buyers can easily switch to something else.

  • Textbook: there are many other candy bars, so the demand for Snickers bars is highly elastic. There is no real substitute for a tooth repair, so the demand for dental work is inelastic.
  • The narrower the definition of a product, the more elastic its demand. The demand for Reebok sneakers is more elastic than the demand for shoes in general. If only Reebok raises its prices, you buy Nike or Adidas instead. If all shoes cost more, you still need shoes.
  • Tunisian examples: the demand for Délice yogurt (one brand) is much more elastic than the demand for yogurt in general. The demand for one brand of harissa (Le Phare du Cap Bon, Sicam…) is elastic; the demand for harissa as a whole is not. The demand for one phone operator's data packs is elastic; the demand for mobile data in general is much less so.

2. Proportion of income: how big is the bill?

Other things equal, the larger the share of a buyer's budget (part du revenu) a product takes, the more elastic its demand.

  • Textbook: a 10% rise in the price of a pencil or a pack of chewing gum is a few cents: almost nobody notices. A 10% rise in the price of a car or a house is thousands of dollars: buyers think twice, delay, or buy something cheaper.
  • Tunisian example: 10% more on a 0.300 TND pack of chewing gum is 30 millimes. 10% more on a 40,000 TND car is 4,000 TND, several months of an average salary. The car buyer reacts much more strongly.

3. Luxuries versus necessities

The demand for necessities (biens de première nécessité) is inelastic. The demand for luxuries (biens de luxe) is elastic. You can give up a luxury when it becomes expensive; you cannot give up a necessity.

  • Textbook: electricity and appendicitis surgery are necessities. Vacation travel and jewelry are luxuries.
  • The textbook's best example, salt: its demand is highly inelastic for three reasons at once: it has no good substitute, it takes a tiny share of the budget, and it is a necessity.
  • Tunisian examples: STEG electricity, bread and medicines are necessities (inelastic). A summer holiday abroad or a dinner in a Gammarth restaurant are luxuries (elastic).

4. Time: how long have buyers had to adjust?

Demand is more elastic the longer the period after a price change. Consumers need time to change their habits, discover substitutes and change their equipment.

  • Textbook: if the price of beef rises, people at first keep buying beef. With time, they learn to cook chicken, pork or fish instead.
  • Textbook numbers: the price elasticity of demand for gasoline is about 0.2 in the short run but about 0.7 in the long run. In the long run, people buy smaller cars, move closer to work or use public transport.
  • Tunisian example: when petrol prices rise, people keep driving at first. After some months or years, some buy a smaller car, share rides, or switch to the louage, the train or the metro.
Common mistake · "more elastic" is not always "elastic"

Gasoline is more elastic in the long run (0.7) than in the short run (0.2), but 0.7 is still below 1, so long-run demand is still inelastic. "More elastic" compares two numbers. "Elastic" means Ed > 1.

Applications: big harvests, excise taxes and the drug debate

The textbook shows three real-world uses of the price elasticity of demand. In each case, the total-revenue test is the key.

Application 1 · Large crop yields: why a big harvest can hurt farmers

The demand for most farm products is highly inelastic: estimates are around 0.20 to 0.25. People do not eat twice as many tomatoes when tomatoes are cheap.

Now suppose the weather is excellent and farmers produce a bumper crop (une récolte record). Supply increases: the supply curve shifts right (Chapter 3). To sell the much larger quantity, the price must fall a lot, because buyers react only weakly to lower prices. And with inelastic demand, a lower price means a lower total revenue. So farmers, as a group, earn less money from a bigger harvest.

Worked example · tomatoes in a record year

Last year: farmers sold 100,000 tonnes (100,000,000 kg) of tomatoes at 1.000 TND per kg. TR = 100,000,000 × 1.000 = 100 million TND.

This year: perfect weather, 120,000 tonnes. Because demand is inelastic, the price must fall to 0.500 TND per kg to sell it all. TR = 120,000,000 × 0.500 = 60 million TND.

Check: midpoint Ed = (20,000 ÷ 110,000) ÷ (0.500 ÷ 0.750) = 18.2% ÷ 66.7% = 0.27, inelastic. The harvest grew by 20 thousand tonnes, but farmers' total revenue fell by 40 million TND.

This is why, in years of very big olive, tomato or watermelon harvests, Tunisian farmers often complain that prices collapse. It also explains a policy idea in the textbook: to raise farm incomes, governments sometimes try to restrict farm output (for example, by limiting the land that can be planted). A smaller crop sold at a much higher price brings more total revenue.

Application 2 · Excise taxes: why governments tax cigarettes, alcohol and petrol

DefinitionExcise tax (droit d'accise)

A tax on the production or sale of a specific product, often a fixed amount per unit (per pack, per litre). Examples: taxes on cigarettes, alcohol and petrol. It is different from a general tax on almost all goods, like VAT (TVA).

A government that wants tax revenue prefers to tax products with inelastic demand. The tax raises the price, but buyers keep buying almost the same quantity, so the tax is collected on many units.

Worked example · the textbook's numbers

Product A, inelastic demand: a tax of $1 per unit. Sales stay at about 10,000 units. Tax revenue = $1 × 10,000 = $10,000.

Product B, elastic demand: a higher tax of $1.50 per unit, but buyers react strongly and sales fall to 4,000 units. Tax revenue = $1.50 × 4,000 = $6,000.

The higher tax rate brings less money, because it shrinks the number of units being taxed. That is why liquor, gasoline and cigarettes are the classic targets of excise taxes, in the United States and in Tunisia alike.

Governments also like taxing cigarettes and alcohol for health reasons. But notice the tension: because demand is inelastic, the tax raises a lot of money but reduces consumption only a little.

Application 3 · Decriminalization of illegal drugs: a debate about elasticity

In the United States, some people propose to decriminalize (legalize) drugs such as heroin and cocaine. The textbook uses this debate to show how elasticity matters. There are two sides:

  • Proponents (in favour): the demand of addicts is highly inelastic. Legalization would lower the price. With inelastic demand, consumption would rise only slightly, and total spending on drugs would fall (price ↓ → TR ↓). Addicts would then need less money, so the street crime committed to pay for drugs would fall.
  • Opponents (against): many users are occasional users ("dabblers") whose demand is elastic. A lower price would make their consumption rise a lot, and new users would start. One study cited by the textbook estimated that legalization could cut street prices by up to 60%, raise the number of occasional heroin users by 54% and of occasional cocaine users by 33%, and increase the total quantity consumed by about 100% for heroin and 50% for cocaine.

Notice what economics can and cannot do here (remember positive vs normative from Chapter 1). Measuring the elasticities is a positive question: studies can test it. Deciding whether drugs should be legal is a normative question that also involves health, values and law. In Tunisia, as in most countries, these drugs are illegal; the textbook uses the American debate only as an example of elasticity.

Exam alert

"Why are excise taxes put on cigarettes and gasoline?" → Inelastic demand: the quantity barely falls, so the tax revenue is large and predictable.

Price elasticity of supply

Elasticity also works on the sellers' side. When the price rises, the law of supply says that producers offer more (Chapter 3). How much more?

DefinitionPrice elasticity of supply (élasticité-prix de l'offre)

A measure of how responsive producers are to a change in the price of their product: the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price.

Es = (% change in quantity supplied of X) ÷ (% change in price of X)

We use the midpoint formula again (averages as bases). Because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, Es is always positive: there is no minus sign to drop.

Worked example · the textbook numbers

The price of a product rises from $4 to $6, and the quantity supplied rises from 10 to 14 units.

% change in Qs = 4 ÷ ((10 + 14) ÷ 2) = 4 ÷ 12 = 33.3%. % change in P = 2 ÷ ((4 + 6) ÷ 2) = 2 ÷ 5 = 40%.

Es = 33.3 ÷ 40 = 0.83. The quantity supplied rises by a smaller percentage than the price, so supply is inelastic in this range.

The interpretation is the same as for demand: Es > 1 elastic, Es < 1 inelastic, Es = 1 unit elastic. The extremes: Es = 0 is perfectly inelastic supply (a vertical supply curve: the quantity is fixed); Es = ∞ is perfectly elastic supply (a horizontal supply curve).

The main determinant: time

Supply elasticity depends on how easily and how quickly producers can move resources (workers, machines, land) into or out of making the product. The key factor is the amount of time producers have to react. The textbook uses three periods.

DefinitionMarket period (période de marché, très courte période)

A period so short that producers cannot change their output at all after a price change. Supply is perfectly inelastic: a vertical line.

Textbook example: a small farmer brings one truckload of tomatoes to the market. That is all he has for the season. Whether the price is high or low, he sells the whole load, because tomatoes are perishable: tomorrow they will rot. The price only decides who gets the fixed quantity. His production costs are already spent, so they no longer affect his decision to sell. (Goods that can be stored, like olive oil or wheat, have a little elasticity even in the market period: a seller can keep part of the stock and sell it later.)

DefinitionShort run (courte période, court terme)

A period too short to change plant capacity (the land, the size of the factory, the big machines), but long enough to use the existing capacity more or less intensively. Supply is more elastic than in the market period.

The tomato farmer cannot buy more land this season, but he can hire more workers, use more fertilizer and water, and pick more carefully. So a higher price brings some extra output.

DefinitionLong run (longue période, long terme)

A period long enough for firms to change their plant size (more land, bigger factories) and for firms to enter or leave the industry. Supply is the most elastic.

Over several years, tomato farmers can rent more land and build greenhouses, and new farmers can start growing tomatoes. A higher price brings a large increase in output.

The more time producers have to adjust, the more elastic supply is: the same rise in demand raises the price a lot in the market period, less in the short run, and least in the long run.
Tunisian example · Deglet Nour dates

Suppose the world demand for Tunisian Deglet Nour dates rises. Market period: this season's harvest is already picked, so the price jumps and the quantity stays the same. Short run: farmers in Tozeur and Kebili can irrigate better, fertilize more and lose fewer dates, so output rises a little. Long run: new palm trees can be planted, but they need several years before they produce. Only then does output rise a lot and the price come back down. This slow response explains why date prices can stay high for years after a rise in demand.

No total-revenue test for supply

For demand, price and quantity move in opposite directions, so TR can go either way. For supply, price and quantity supplied move in the same direction. If the price rises, the quantity supplied rises too (or stays the same), so TR = P × Q always rises with the price, whatever the elasticity. That is why there is no total-revenue test for supply.

Two applications

Antiques and reproductions. The supply of genuine antiques is highly inelastic: nobody can produce more 19th-century furniture. A one-of-a-kind object (an original painting, a unique vase) has a perfectly inelastic supply. So when the demand for antiques rises, their prices rise a lot. Reproductions (modern copies) have an elastic supply: factories can make more of them quickly, so their prices rise only slightly when demand rises. Tunisian example: an antique hand-knotted Kairouan carpet from the 1800s, compared with a new machine-made carpet with the same pattern.

Volatile gold prices. The supply of gold is highly inelastic: exploring, opening mines and refining gold take years and cost a lot, and shutting a mine down is also costly. Meanwhile, the demand for gold shifts a lot: in times of inflation, war or financial turmoil, people buy gold as a safe store of value, and speculators buy and sell it. Large shifts in demand + an inelastic supply = large price swings. You can see it in the gold souk of the Tunis medina (Souk El Berka): when the world price of gold jumps, the price of jewelry there jumps too.

Common mistakes

(1) Using the total-revenue test for supply: impossible, because TR always moves with price along a supply curve. (2) Mixing up the periods: in the market period the quantity is fixed (vertical supply), so the price change is the largest, not the smallest. (3) Forgetting that Es is positive: if your answer is negative, check your calculation.

Cross elasticity and income elasticity of demand

So far we changed the product's own price. But in Chapter 3 you saw that demand also depends on the prices of related goods and on income. Two more elasticities measure these effects. Here, unlike Ed, the sign (+ or −) is the most important part of the answer.

Cross elasticity of demand

DefinitionCross elasticity of demand (élasticité croisée de la demande)

A measure of how sensitive the quantity demanded of one product (X) is to a change in the price of another product (Y).

Exy = (% change in quantity demanded of X) ÷ (% change in price of Y)

The sign tells us how the two goods are related:

  • Positive (Exy > 0) → substitutes (biens substituables). When the price of Y rises, people buy more X instead. Textbook: Evian and Dasani bottled water. The larger the positive coefficient, the stronger the substitutes. Tunisian examples: Safia and Sabrine water; Ooredoo and Orange data packs; the louage and the train between Tunis and Sousse.
  • Negative (Exy < 0) → complements (biens complémentaires). When the price of Y rises, people buy less Y, and therefore less X, because the two are used together. Textbook: digital cameras and memory sticks. Tunisian examples: cars and petrol, tea and sugar, a printer and its ink cartridges.
  • Zero or near zero → independent goods (biens indépendants). The price of one has no effect on the demand for the other. Textbook: walnuts and plums. Tunisian example: harissa and phone chargers.
Worked example · two quick cross elasticities

Data packs. Suppose Orange raises the price of its data pack by 10%. The quantity of Ooredoo packs demanded rises by 6%. Exy = +6% ÷ +10% = +0.6. Positive → substitutes.

Petrol and big cars. The price of petrol rises by 20%. The quantity of big cars demanded falls by 8%. Exy = −8% ÷ +20% = −0.4. Negative → complements.

Worked example · with the midpoint formula

A café raises the price of its coffee from 1.500 TND to 2.500 TND. Its daily sales of croissants (which customers eat with their coffee) fall from 220 to 180.

% change in Q of croissants = −40 ÷ ((220 + 180) ÷ 2) = −40 ÷ 200 = −20%. % change in P of coffee = +1 ÷ ((1.500 + 2.500) ÷ 2) = 1 ÷ 2 = +50%.

Exy = −20 ÷ 50 = −0.4. Negative → coffee and croissants are complements. Keep the minus sign: it is the answer!

Why firms and governments care about cross elasticity

Business decisions: "cannibalization". The Coca-Cola Company sells both Coke and Sprite. If it cuts the price of Sprite, will Sprite steal sales from Coke? A firm cannibalizes its own product when a cheaper or newer product takes customers away from another product of the same firm. If the cross elasticity between Sprite and Coke is low, they are weak substitutes, so a lower Sprite price will win new customers without hurting Coke much. If it is high, the price cut will mostly move customers from one of the company's drinks to the other. Tunisian example: a phone operator that sells a cheap "youth" pack and a standard pack must check how many standard customers would move to the cheap pack.

Government: competition policy (antitrust). Governments use cross elasticity to judge mergers (fusions). If two firms sell products with a high positive cross elasticity (strong substitutes, like Coca-Cola and Pepsi), a merger would remove real competition, so it would probably be blocked. If the cross elasticity is near zero (Coca-Cola and Shell: cola and gasoline), a merger would not reduce competition in either market, so it would be allowed. In Tunisia, the Conseil de la Concurrence (Competition Council) plays this role.

Income elasticity of demand

DefinitionIncome elasticity of demand (élasticité-revenu de la demande)

A measure of how much the quantity demanded of a product changes, at a given price, when consumers' income changes.

Ei = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in income)
  • Positive (Ei > 0) → normal goods (biens normaux), also called superior goods: people buy more when their income rises. Textbook estimates: automobiles ≈ +3, housing ≈ +1.5, restaurant meals ≈ +1.4, most farm products ≈ +0.20.
  • Negative (Ei < 0) → inferior goods (biens inférieurs): people buy less when their income rises, because they switch to better products. Textbook examples: retread (recycled) tires, cabbage, long-distance bus tickets, used clothing, cheap muscatel wine. Tunisian example: second-hand clothes from the fripe; as their income rises, many people buy new clothes instead.

Among normal goods, the size of Ei also matters. If Ei > 1, demand is income elastic: the quantity grows faster than income. These goods behave like luxuries (cars, restaurant meals, travel). If 0 < Ei < 1, demand is income inelastic: the quantity grows more slowly than income. These goods behave like necessities (most food, toothpaste).

Worked example · two income elasticities

Restaurant meals. Incomes rise by 10% and the quantity of restaurant meals demanded rises by 14%. Ei = +14% ÷ +10% = +1.4: a normal good, income elastic.

Fripe clothes. Incomes rise by 5% and purchases of second-hand clothes fall by 3%. Ei = −3% ÷ +5% = −0.6: an inferior good.

Insight · who suffers most in a recession?

In a recession (récession), incomes fall. Products with a high income elasticity suffer most: cars, housing, restaurant meals, luxury holidays, big television sets. Products with a low income elasticity barely change: food eaten at home (Ei ≈ 0.20), toothpaste. Tunisian illustration: when household budgets are squeezed, families first cut restaurant outings, summer trips and new cars; their purchases of semolina, bread and oil hardly change, and the fripe may even get more customers.

Consumer surplus and producer surplus

Remember the fourth puzzle of section 01: most buyers pay less than the most they would be willing to pay, and most sellers receive more than the least they would accept. Economists measure these gains with two tools.

Consumer surplus

DefinitionConsumer surplus (surplus du consommateur)

The difference between the maximum price a consumer (or consumers) is willing to pay for a product and the actual price paid. It is the extra benefit buyers get because they pay less than the product is worth to them.

Consumer surplus = maximum price willing to pay − actual price

The textbook example (Table 6.5): six people want a bag of oranges. Each wants one bag, and each has a different maximum price, depending on how much he or she likes oranges. The market price is $8.

PersonMaximum price willing to payActual priceConsumer surplus
Bob$13$8$5 (= $13 − $8)
Barb$12$8$4 (= $12 − $8)
Bill$11$8$3 (= $11 − $8)
Bart$10$8$2 (= $10 − $8)
Brent$9$8$1 (= $9 − $8)
Betty$8$8$0 (= $8 − $8)
Total$15

Bob would have paid $13 but pays only $8: he gets $5 of extra satisfaction for free. Betty pays exactly her maximum, so she gets no surplus: for her, buying or not buying is equally good. The total consumer surplus in this market is $5 + $4 + $3 + $2 + $1 + $0 = $15.

On a graph (Figure 6.5 in the textbook), the demand curve shows the maximum price that buyers would pay for each unit. So consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the market price, out to the quantity bought. With a straight-line demand curve, it is a triangle.

Consumer surplus and price are inversely related. If the price rose to $10, only Bob, Barb, Bill and Bart would buy, and the total consumer surplus would fall to $3 + $2 + $1 + $0 = $6. If the price fell to $7, all six would buy and gain $6 + $5 + $4 + $3 + $2 + $1 = $21. A higher price means a smaller surplus.

Producer surplus

DefinitionProducer surplus (surplus du producteur)

The difference between the actual price a producer receives and the minimum acceptable price (the lowest price at which it would be willing to sell). The minimum acceptable price reflects the producer's marginal cost.

Producer surplus = actual price − minimum acceptable price

The textbook example (Table 6.6): six orange growers each have one bag to sell. Their minimum acceptable prices differ because their production costs differ. The market price is again $8.

Courtney$4$8$4 (= $8 − $4)
Chuck$5$8$3 (= $8 − $5)
Cindy$6$8$2 (= $8 − $6)
Craig$7$8$1 (= $8 − $7)

Total producer surplus = $5 + $4 + $3 + $2 + $1 + $0 = $15. On a graph (Figure 6.6 in the textbook), the supply curve shows the minimum acceptable price for each unit, so producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the market price.

Producer surplus and price are directly related. At a price of $6, only Carlos, Courtney, Chuck and Cindy would sell, for a total surplus of $3 + $2 + $1 + $0 = $6. At $10, all six would sell and gain $7 + $6 + $5 + $4 + $3 + $2 = $27. A higher price means a bigger producer surplus.

Tunisian example 1 · concert tickets at the Carthage festival

Five students would pay at most 30, 25, 20, 15 and 10 TND for a concert ticket at the Festival international de Carthage. The ticket costs 15 TND.

The first four buy (their maximum is at least 15 TND). Consumer surplus = (30 − 15) + (25 − 15) + (20 − 15) + (15 − 15) = 15 + 10 + 5 + 0 = 30 TND. The fifth student (maximum 10 TND) does not buy, so he adds nothing.

Tunisian example 2 · second-hand textbooks at ISB

Five second-year students want to sell their old microeconomics textbook. Their minimum acceptable prices are 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 TND. The going price is 22 TND.

The three students with minimums of 10, 15 and 20 TND sell. Producer surplus = (22 − 10) + (22 − 15) + (22 − 20) = 12 + 7 + 2 = 21 TND. The other two keep their books.

(2) Thinking consumer surplus is money saved in a bank account. It measures extra satisfaction (net benefit), expressed in money. (3) Mixing up the directions: price ↑ → consumer surplus ↓ but producer surplus ↑.

Efficiency revisited and deadweight losses

Now put consumer surplus and producer surplus together. This gives a powerful way to see why competitive markets are efficient, and to measure what is lost when a market produces the "wrong" quantity.

Two ways to read the curves

  • The demand curve shows, for each unit, the maximum price some buyer is willing to pay. That is the marginal benefit (MB) of that unit (remember MB and MC from Chapter 1).
  • The supply curve shows, for each unit, the minimum price some seller is willing to accept. That reflects the marginal cost (MC) of producing that unit.

At the competitive equilibrium quantity Q1 (where the two curves cross), three things are true at the same time:

  1. MB = MC: the benefit of the last unit equals its cost (the Chapter 1 rule for the best amount);
  2. maximum willingness to pay = minimum acceptable price for the last unit;
  3. the combined consumer and producer surplus is as large as it can be.

The textbook adds that competition also brings productive efficiency (each unit is produced with the least costly technique) and allocative efficiency (the right quantity is produced: not too little, not too much). You met both in Chapter 3; consumer and producer surplus now let us measure allocative efficiency.

At the competitive equilibrium, MB = MC, and the total surplus (consumer surplus + producer surplus) is maximized. That is allocative efficiency.
Worked example · the oranges market again

Put Tables 6.5 and 6.6 together (the figure in section 09). For each bag, the net gain to society is the buyer's maximum price minus the seller's minimum price:

Bag 1: $13 − $3 = $10. Bag 2: $12 − $4 = $8. Bag 3: $11 − $5 = $6. Bag 4: $10 − $6 = $4. Bag 5: $9 − $7 = $2. Bag 6: $8 − $8 = $0.

Total = $10 + $8 + $6 + $4 + $2 + $0 = $30 = consumer surplus ($15) + producer surplus ($15). Every bag up to the 6th is worth at least what it costs, and the market trades exactly six bags at $8. If only 3 bags were traded, society would lose $4 + $2 + $0 = $6 of net benefit.

A market in dinars, drawn to scale

Consider the weekly market for strawberries in a region. Demand: P = 14 − 2Q. Supply: P = 2 + Q (P in TND per kg, Q in thousands of kg). The curves cross at Q1 = 4 thousand kg and P1 = 6 TND (check: 14 − 2 × 4 = 6 and 2 + 4 = 6).

  • Consumer surplus = the triangle between the demand curve and the price = ½ × base × height = ½ × 4 × (14 − 6) = 16 thousand TND.
  • Producer surplus = the triangle between the price and the supply curve = ½ × 4 × (6 − 2) = 8 thousand TND.
  • Total surplus at the equilibrium = 16 + 8 = 24 thousand TND. No other quantity gives more.

Deadweight losses: producing too little or too much

DefinitionEfficiency losses, or deadweight losses (pertes sèches, pertes d'efficience)

Reductions in the combined consumer and producer surplus that happen when a market produces less or more than the efficient quantity. Nobody receives this lost surplus: it simply disappears.

Underproduction (Q2 < Q1). Suppose only 2 thousand kg are produced. For every kilo between 2 and 4 thousand, some buyer values it more than it costs some seller to produce it. At Q2 = 2, a buyer would pay up to 10 TND (point d on the demand curve), while a seller would accept 4 TND (point e on the supply curve). These units are not produced, so their net benefit is lost: the triangle dbe.

Deadweight loss = ½ × (4 − 2) × (10 − 4) = ½ × 2 × 6 = 6 thousand TND

The textbook gives a one-unit version: if a buyer is willing to pay $10 for a unit and a seller is willing to sell it for $6, but the unit is not produced, society loses $10 − $6 = $4 of net benefit.

Overproduction (Q3 > Q1). Now suppose 6 thousand kg are produced. Every kilo between 4 and 6 thousand costs more to produce than buyers think it is worth. At Q3 = 6, the last kilo costs 8 TND (point f on the supply curve) but is worth only 2 TND to its buyer (point g on the demand curve). These units destroy value: the triangle bfg.

Deadweight loss = ½ × (6 − 4) × (8 − 2) = ½ × 2 × 6 = 6 thousand TND

Textbook one-unit version: if a unit that buyers value at most $7 is produced at a minimum acceptable price (cost) of $10, society loses $10 − $7 = $3. As the textbook puts it, "there can be too much as well as too little of a good thing."

What can push a market away from Q1? You will meet the causes in later chapters. One you already know: a binding price ceiling (Chapter 3) reduces the quantity supplied below Q1, so it creates a deadweight loss of the "dbe" type.

Common mistakes

(1) "More output is always better." No: beyond Q1, extra units cost more than they are worth (MC > MB), so overproduction also creates a deadweight loss. (2) "The deadweight loss goes to someone." No: it is a pure loss for society; neither buyers nor sellers receive it. (3) Forgetting the ½ in the area of a triangle.

Exam alert

Key Question 15: "Explain why competitive markets are efficient, and what deadweight losses are." Use this skeleton: at the equilibrium, MB = MC and the maximum willingness to pay equals the minimum acceptable price, so the combined surplus is maximized. Below Q1, units worth more than they cost are not produced (loss = triangle dbe). Above Q1, units are produced that cost more than they are worth (loss = triangle bfg).

Last Word: elasticity and pricing power

Two passengers on the same plane may have paid very different prices for the same kind of seat. This is no accident. A firm that knows the elasticities of different groups of customers can earn more by charging them different prices.

DefinitionPrice discrimination (discrimination par les prix)

Selling the same product to different groups of buyers at different prices, when the difference is not explained by a difference in cost. It works when the groups have different price elasticities, when the seller can tell the groups apart, and when buyers cannot easily resell the product to each other.

The rule follows directly from the total-revenue test: charge a higher price to the group with inelastic demand (its spending rises with price), and a lower price to the group with elastic demand (its spending rises when the price falls).

The textbook's three examples

  • Airlines. Business travellers have inelastic demand: they must fly on a given day, and often their employer pays. Leisure travellers have elastic demand: they can change dates, take the car, or not travel at all. So airlines charge business travellers more. To stop business travellers from using the cheap tickets, cheap fares come with restrictions: non-refundable tickets, purchase at least two weeks in advance, or a stay over a Saturday night (business trips usually end before the weekend).
  • Discounts for children. Haircuts, baseball games and ski lifts are cheaper for children. Families' demand for children's activities is more elastic (the cost multiplies with each child, and these activities are easy to give up), so lower prices attract them.
  • College tuition. American colleges announce one tuition price but give financial aid to many students, so the net price (tuition − aid) differs from student to student. Students from low-income families have more elastic demand, so they receive more aid. When colleges announce a tuition increase, they often add aid at the same time, so the increase falls mostly on the less elastic, higher-income students.

Tunisian examples

  • Student fares: students pay less for transport subscriptions (metro, bus, train) than other passengers. Students have small budgets, so their demand is more elastic, and the student card makes it easy to separate the two groups.
  • Off-peak phone offers: night and weekend bundles are cheaper. People who call during working hours (often for work) have less elastic demand.
  • Hotels in Hammamet and Djerba: early-booking prices for flexible holidaymakers are lower than last-minute prices for people who must travel on precise dates, and off-season prices are lower than August prices.
  • Cinemas and museums: student and children's tickets are cheaper in many places, for the same reasons as the textbook's children's discounts.
Worked example · why charging two prices raises revenue

A Djerba hotel has two groups of customers in July. With one single price of 150 TND per night, it fills 60 rooms: TR = 150 × 60 = 9,000 TND per night.

With two prices, it charges 200 TND to 30 last-minute guests (inelastic: they come anyway) and 120 TND to 50 early bookers (elastic: they come only if it is cheap). TR = 200 × 30 + 120 × 50 = 6,000 + 6,000 = 12,000 TND per night.

Same hotel, same rooms: 3,000 TND more per night, just by using the difference in elasticities.

Common mistake

Not every price difference is price discrimination. A business-class seat is bigger and comes with more service, so part of its higher price reflects a higher cost. Price discrimination means the same product (the same economy seat, the same haircut) sold at different prices because the groups have different elasticities.

Question 1 · do the units matter?

P7Q1

In Table 6.1 (movie tickets), the quantities could be written as 1, 2, 3 … 8 "units" or as 1,000, 2,000, 3,000 … 8,000 tickets. Explain why this choice makes no difference to the elasticity coefficients.

Show the model answer

Elasticity is built from percentage changes, and a percentage is a ratio: change ÷ average. If every quantity is multiplied by 1,000, both the change and the average are multiplied by 1,000, so the ratio is the same.

Example, $8 → $7: with "units", 1 ÷ 1.5 = 66.7%; with tickets, 1,000 ÷ 1,500 = 66.7%. Identical. The same is true for prices in dollars or cents (the popcorn example), or in dinars or millimes. This is the whole reason economists use percentages: the result does not depend on the units of measurement. (Problem P3 showed it again: the Radès market, with every price and quantity multiplied by 5, has exactly the same elasticities.)

The price rises and supply is inelastic.

Again supply: price ↑ and quantity supplied ↑ (or unchanged) → TR increases. There is no total-revenue test for supply.

The price rises and demand is inelastic.

Inelastic → same direction. Price ↑ → TR increases: buyers cut their purchases only a little.

The price falls and demand is elastic.

Questions 6 and 7 · two puzzles

P10Q6

A university adopts a rule that all its students must live in university dormitories. What effect would this rule have on the price elasticity of demand for dormitory space? What might it mean for room rates?

Show the model answer

The rule removes the substitutes (renting a flat in town, living with family). Students must buy dorm space, whatever the price, so the demand for dorm space becomes highly inelastic, almost perfectly inelastic (a nearly vertical demand curve).

Consequence: the university can raise room rates without losing occupants. With inelastic demand, price and total revenue move in the same direction, so its revenue from the dorms rises. (This is why such rules are controversial for students.)

P11Q7

In November 1998, a self-portrait by Vincent van Gogh sold at auction for $71.5 million. Show this sale with a demand-and-supply diagram and comment on the elasticity of supply. Then answer this puzzle: if a painting can be copied well enough to fool some experts, why is the original worth so much more than the copy?

Show the model answer

Diagram: the supply of this painting is a vertical line at a quantity of 1: there is only one original, and no one can produce another. Supply is perfectly inelastic (Es = 0). The demand curve of the rich collectors at the auction crosses this vertical line at $71.5 million. The price is set entirely by demand: if more collectors had wanted it, the vertical supply would have met a higher demand curve and the price would have been even higher.

The puzzle: a copy, even a perfect one, is not the same good. Buyers pay for authenticity, history (provenance) and uniqueness: owning the canvas van Gogh himself painted. A forgery can be produced again and again, so its supply is elastic and its price is low. The original's value comes from its perfectly inelastic supply plus a strong demand for that uniqueness.

Key Question 15 and Question 16 · explain in words

P13KQ15

Use the ideas of consumer surplus and producer surplus to explain why economists say competitive markets are efficient. Why are output levels below or above the equilibrium inefficient?

Show the model answer

Why competitive markets are efficient. The demand curve shows buyers' maximum willingness to pay (marginal benefit); the supply curve shows sellers' minimum acceptable price (marginal cost). At the competitive equilibrium quantity Q1, the two curves cross: MB = MC, and the maximum willingness to pay equals the minimum acceptable price for the last unit. Every unit up to Q1 is worth more to a buyer than it costs a seller, and all of them are traded. So the combined consumer and producer surplus is maximized: allocative efficiency. Competition also pushes firms to use least-cost methods: productive efficiency.

Below the equilibrium (Q2 < Q1). Some units that buyers value more than they cost to produce are not produced. Their net benefit is lost: the deadweight loss triangle dbe.

Above the equilibrium (Q3 > Q1). Units are produced whose cost (MC) is higher than their value to buyers (MB). They destroy value: the deadweight loss triangle bfg. Too little or too much both reduce total surplus.

P14Q16 (Last Word)

What is the purpose of charging different groups of customers different prices? Add two examples of your own to the three examples of the Last Word.

Show the model answer

Purpose: to earn more revenue by using differences in price elasticity between groups. The seller charges a higher price to the group with inelastic demand (its spending rises when the price rises) and a lower price to the group with elastic demand (its spending rises when the price falls). For this to work, the seller must be able to identify the groups and stop resale between them.

Examples (any two): senior-citizen discounts; cheaper cinema tickets in the afternoon (matinées); "early-bird" menus in restaurants; student licences for software; happy-hour prices in cafés; off-season hotel rates in Hammamet; night and weekend phone bundles; student transport cards.

Recap and key terms

What to remember for class and the midterm

  • Price elasticity of demand: Ed = % change in Qd ÷ % change in P. Use the midpoint formula (averages as bases) so the answer is the same up or down: $5 → $4 with 10 → 20 units gives (10 ÷ 15) ÷ (1 ÷ 4.5) = 3. Percentages remove units (popcorn: 1.25 in dollars or cents) and allow comparisons between products.
  • Drop the minus sign for Ed only. Ed > 1 elastic; Ed < 1 inelastic; Ed = 1 unit elastic; Ed = 0 perfectly inelastic (vertical: insulin); Ed = ∞ perfectly elastic (horizontal: one competitive firm). Inelastic is not "no reaction".
  • Total-revenue test (TR = P × Q): elastic → P and TR move in opposite directions; inelastic → same direction; unit elastic → TR unchanged. Figure 6.2: $20 → $40 (elastic), $40 → $20 (inelastic), $30 → $30 (unit). TR follows the stronger side.
  • Linear demand: constant slope, changing elasticity. Table 6.1: Ed = 5.00, 2.60, 1.57, 1.00, 0.64, 0.38, 0.20; elastic at high prices, inelastic at low prices; TR is maximum ($20,000) in the unit-elastic range ($5–$4). Slope ≠ elasticity.
  • Determinants of Ed: substitutes (more → more elastic; narrow product definitions are more elastic), share of income, luxury vs necessity, time (gasoline 0.2 short run, 0.7 long run).
  • Applications: bumper crops lower farm revenue (inelastic demand); excise taxes target inelastic goods ($1 × 10,000 = $10,000 vs $1.50 × 4,000 = $6,000); the drug debate turns on addicts' (inelastic) vs occasional users' (elastic) demand.
  • Price elasticity of supply: Es = % change in Qs ÷ % change in P, always positive ($4 → $6, 10 → 14: Es = 0.83). Main determinant: time: market period (vertical) → short run → long run (most elastic); Pm > Ps > Pl. No TR test for supply. Antiques and gold: inelastic supply → big price swings.
  • Cross elasticity Exy: + substitutes, − complements, ≈ 0 independent (cannibalization, merger policy). Income elasticity Ei: + normal (Ei > 1 income elastic), − inferior. Keep the sign for both. Recessions hit high-Ei goods hardest.
  • Consumer surplus = maximum willingness to pay − price (area below D, above P; Table 6.5 = $15); falls when price rises. Producer surplus = price − minimum acceptable price (area above S, below P; Table 6.6 = $15); rises with price.
  • Efficiency: at the competitive equilibrium, MB = MC, max willingness to pay = min acceptable price, and CS + PS is maximized. Producing less (triangle dbe) or more (triangle bfg) creates deadweight losses.
  • Price discrimination: higher prices for inelastic groups (business travellers), lower prices for elastic groups (leisure travellers, children, students).

Glossary

Price elasticity of demand
How responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in the product's own price; Ed = % change in Qd ÷ % change in P (élasticité-prix de la demande).
Midpoint formula
Computes percentage changes using the average of the two values as the base, so the elasticity is the same for a rise or a fall (formule du point milieu).
Absolute value
A number without its sign; Ed is reported as an absolute value (valeur absolue).
Elastic demand
Ed > 1: quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price (demande élastique).
Inelastic demand
Ed < 1: quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price (demande inélastique).
Unit elasticity
Ed = 1: quantity demanded changes by the same percentage as price (élasticité unitaire).
Perfectly inelastic demand
Ed = 0: quantity does not respond to price; a vertical demand curve (demande parfaitement inélastique).
Perfectly elastic demand
Ed = ∞: any quantity at one price, none above it; a horizontal demand curve (demande parfaitement élastique).
Total revenue (TR)
Price × quantity sold; what sellers receive and buyers spend (recette totale).
Total-revenue test
Judging elasticity from the direction of TR after a price change: opposite = elastic, same = inelastic, unchanged = unit elastic (test de la recette totale).
Determinants of Ed
Substitutability, proportion of income, luxury vs necessity, and time (déterminants de l'élasticité-prix).
Excise tax
A tax on a specific product, often per unit, such as cigarettes, alcohol or petrol (droit d'accise).
Price elasticity of supply
How responsive the quantity supplied is to a change in price; Es = % change in Qs ÷ % change in P, always positive (élasticité-prix de l'offre).
Market period
A period too short to change output; supply is perfectly inelastic (période de marché, très courte période).
Short run
Plant capacity is fixed, but it can be used more or less intensively (courte période, court terme).
Long run
Firms can change plant size and enter or leave the industry; supply is most elastic (longue période, long terme).
Cross elasticity of demand
% change in the quantity demanded of X ÷ % change in the price of Y; + substitutes, − complements, 0 independent (élasticité croisée de la demande).
Substitute goods
Goods used in place of each other; positive cross elasticity (biens substituables).
Complementary goods
Goods used together; negative cross elasticity (biens complémentaires).
Independent goods
Goods with no relation; cross elasticity near zero (biens indépendants).
Cannibalization
When a firm's new or cheaper product takes sales from another of its own products (cannibalisation).
Income elasticity of demand
% change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income (élasticité-revenu de la demande).
Normal good
A good with positive income elasticity; demand rises with income (bien normal).
Inferior good
A good with negative income elasticity; demand falls as income rises (bien inférieur).
Consumer surplus
Maximum price a consumer is willing to pay minus the actual price; the area below demand and above the price (surplus du consommateur).
Producer surplus
Actual price received minus the minimum acceptable price; the area above supply and below the price (surplus du producteur).
Marginal benefit / marginal cost
The extra benefit / extra cost of one more unit; shown by the demand and supply curves (bénéfice marginal / coût marginal).
Allocative efficiency
Producing the quantity society values most, where MB = MC and total surplus is maximized (efficacité allocative).
Productive efficiency
Producing each unit at the lowest possible cost (efficacité productive).
Efficiency loss (deadweight loss)
The loss of combined consumer and producer surplus from producing less or more than the efficient quantity (perte sèche, perte d'efficience).
Price discrimination
Charging different groups different prices for the same product, based on their different elasticities (discrimination par les prix).
