There is an old joke: "Teach a parrot to say 'demand and supply' and you have an economist." It is a joke, but there is truth in it. Almost every price you see — the price of a coffee, of a kilo of tomatoes, of an apartment in Tunis, of the euro — is explained by demand and supply. In this chapter you learn how the model works, step by step.

Remember Chapter 2: in a market system, prices coordinate the decisions of millions of buyers and sellers. Chapter 3 now opens the "black box" and shows how a price is set.

DefinitionMarket (marché)

An institution or mechanism that brings together buyers ("demanders") and sellers ("suppliers") of a particular good or service.

Markets come in many forms. Some are physical places; some exist only on a screen. Some are local, some are national, some are international. In some you know the seller personally; in others the buyer and the seller never meet ("faceless" markets).

Which markets does this chapter study?

This chapter studies highly competitive markets. In these markets:

  • there are many independent buyers and many independent sellers, so no single person can control the price;
  • the product is standardized (produit standardisé / homogène): one seller's product is the same as another's. A bushel of corn from one farm is the same as a bushel from the next farm.

Examples: a grain exchange, the stock market, the foreign currency market. In these markets nobody "sets" the price. The price is discovered through the actions of all the buyers and sellers together.

Tunisian example · Bir El Kassaa at 5 a.m.

Every morning, hundreds of farmers bring tomatoes to the wholesale market of Bir El Kassaa, near Tunis, and hundreds of retailers come to buy them. One farmer's tomatoes are almost the same as another's. If one farmer asks much more than the others, nobody buys from him. If he asks much less, he loses money for nothing. So everybody ends up trading at about the same price — and that price changes from day to day with the amounts that buyers want and that farmers bring. This is a competitive market, very close to the textbook model.

Demand (the buyers' side) + Supply (the sellers' side) → the market price and the quantity traded.

02

Demand and the law of demand

In everyday French, "la demande" often means "a request". In economics, demand has a precise meaning. It is not one number. It is a whole list that shows how much buyers would purchase at each possible price.

DefinitionDemand (demande)

A schedule or a curve that shows the various amounts of a product that consumers are willing and able to purchase at each of a series of possible prices during a specified period of time, other things equal.

Three parts of this definition are very important. Read them slowly.

  1. "Willing and able." Wanting a product is not enough. You must also have the money. You may dream of a new iPhone, but if you have only 50 dinars, your wish is not demand in the economic sense. Economists say only effective demand (willingness backed by money) counts. The opposite is also true: a rich person who does not want a product does not demand it either.
  2. "During a specified period of time." "I buy 10 coffees" means nothing. 10 coffees per day? Per week? Per year? A quantity demanded must always have a time unit: "10 bushels per week".
  3. "Other things equal" (ceteris paribus, toutes choses égales par ailleurs — you met it in Chapter 1). When we draw a demand schedule, we let only the price of the product change. Income, tastes, the prices of other goods and so on stay constant.

The demand schedule

DefinitionDemand schedule (barème de demande) and quantity demanded (quantité demandée)

A demand schedule is a table that lists prices and, next to each price, the quantity demanded: the amount buyers are willing and able to buy at that specific price.

The textbook uses the example of Joe, one buyer of corn. A bushel (boisseau) is an American unit for grain. For corn, one bushel weighs about 25 kg.

Common mistake · "demand" vs "quantity demanded"

Demand = the whole table (all five rows together) or the whole curve. Quantity demanded = one number, at one price. "At $3, the quantity demanded is 35 bushels." It is wrong to say "at $3, the demand is 35 bushels". This difference will become very important in section 05.

The law of demand

Look at the table again. When the price goes down, the quantity demanded goes up. When the price goes up, the quantity demanded goes down. This is true for almost every product in the world, so economists call it a "law".

DefinitionLaw of demand (loi de la demande)

Other things equal, as price falls, the quantity demanded rises, and as price rises, the quantity demanded falls. There is a negative or inverse relationship (relation inverse) between price and quantity demanded.

Price ↑ → Quantity demanded ↓   ·   Price ↓ → Quantity demanded ↑

Why is "other things equal" so important? The textbook gives the example of Nike shoes. Suppose the price of Nike shoes rises by $5. The law of demand says people will buy fewer Nikes — if the prices of other shoes do not change. But suppose the prices of Reebok, Adidas and New Balance shoes also rise by $5. Then Nikes are not more expensive compared with the other brands. People may buy more Nikes, fewer, or the same number. What matters for the law of demand is the relative price (prix relatif): the price of a product compared with the prices of similar products.

Tunisian example · relative prices of yogurt

Suppose the price of a Délice yogurt rises by 100 millimes. If the other yogurt brands keep their old prices, many families switch to another brand, and the quantity of Délice yogurts demanded falls. But if all yogurt brands raise their prices by 100 millimes at the same time (for example because the price of milk went up), Délice is not more expensive than its competitors. Its sales may fall only a little. "Other things equal" means: only the price of Délice changes; everything else, including competitors' prices, stays the same.

Why does the law of demand hold? Three explanations

The textbook gives three explanations. You must know all three for the exam.

Explanation 1 — Common sense and observation. Price is an obstacle that stops people from buying. The higher the obstacle, the less people buy. Businesses believe in the law of demand: that is why they organize "sales" (soldes) to get rid of unsold products. They never raise prices to sell more. In Tunisia, the official winter and summer sales periods (soldes d'hiver, soldes d'été) fill the shops for exactly this reason.

Explanation 2 — Diminishing marginal utility. Utility (utilité) means satisfaction. Marginal means "extra" (Chapter 1: one more unit).

DefinitionDiminishing marginal utility (utilité marginale décroissante)

In a given period of time, each additional (successive) unit of a product gives a consumer less extra satisfaction than the unit before.

The textbook example: the second Big Mac gives you less satisfaction than the first, and the third even less. A Tunisian example: on a cold January morning, a first bowl of lablabi is wonderful. A second bowl is still nice but less exciting. You probably do not want a third. Because each extra unit gives less satisfaction, consumers buy more units only if the price is lower.

Worked example · from satisfaction to a demand schedule

Rania walks on the beach in Hammamet in August. The most she would pay for each bottle of cold water is:

BottleMost she would pay (her marginal benefit)
1st bottle2.000 TND
2nd bottle1.200 TND
3rd bottle0.700 TND
4th bottle0.300 TND

She buys a bottle only if it is worth at least its price. So: at a price of 1.500 TND she buys 1 bottle (only the first is worth 1.500). At 1.000 TND she buys 2. At 0.500 TND she buys 3. At 0.250 TND she buys 4. We have just built her demand schedule — and it obeys the law of demand. The reason is diminishing marginal utility: the 3rd bottle is worth less to her, so she buys it only at a low price.

Explanation 3 — The income effect and the substitution effect. When the price of a product falls, two things happen at the same time.

DefinitionIncome effect (effet revenu)

A lower price increases the purchasing power (pouvoir d'achat) of a buyer's money income. With the same money, the buyer can now buy more of the product than before. (A higher price has the opposite effect.)

DefinitionSubstitution effect (effet de substitution)

At a lower price, buyers have an incentive to substitute (replace) other, now relatively more expensive products with the now cheaper product. (A higher price pushes buyers toward substitutes.)

Worked example · the price of chicken falls

Textbook: when the price of chicken falls, a shopper can buy more chicken with the same income (income effect). Chicken is now cheaper compared with pork, lamb, beef and fish, so the shopper buys chicken instead of some of them (substitution effect). Both effects increase the quantity of chicken demanded.

With numbers: the Ben Salah family spends 36 TND per week on chicken. At 9 TND per kg, that buys 36 ÷ 9 = 4 kg. If the price falls to 6 TND per kg, the same 36 TND buys 36 ÷ 6 = 6 kg: the family is "richer" in chicken (income effect). And because chicken is now much cheaper than lamb (often more than 30 TND per kg in Tunisia), the family also replaces some lamb meals with chicken meals (substitution effect).

Exam alert

"Explain why the demand curve slopes downward." Give the three explanations: (1) common sense — price is an obstacle (sales prove it); (2) diminishing marginal utility; (3) the income effect and the substitution effect. The textbook quick quiz answer is: "lower prices create income and substitution effects that lead consumers to purchase more".

03

The demand curve and market demand

From a table to a curve

A demand schedule can be drawn as a graph. Economists follow one convention that surprises math students: the price goes on the vertical axis (the y-axis) and the quantity goes on the horizontal axis (the x-axis). Each row of the table becomes one point. Joining the points gives the demand curve.

DefinitionDemand curve (courbe de demande)

A graph of a demand schedule, with price on the vertical axis and quantity demanded on the horizontal axis. Because of the law of demand, it slopes downward from left to right (pente négative).

How to draw it in an exam (4 steps)

(1) Draw two axes. Write P (price) on the vertical axis and Q (quantity per period) on the horizontal axis. (2) Choose a regular scale for each axis and write the numbers. (3) Plot one point for each row of the table. (4) Join the points with a smooth line and label it D.

Market demand: add the quantities horizontally

Joe is only one buyer. A market has many buyers. To get the market demand (demande du marché), we add the quantities that all the buyers demand at each price.

DefinitionMarket demand (demande du marché)

The total quantity demanded by all buyers at each possible price. It is found by adding the individual quantities demanded at each price. On a graph, this is called horizontal summation (somme horizontale) of the individual demand curves.

Common mistake · never add the prices

Horizontal summation means: fix one price, then add the quantities across the row. Some students add the prices ("$5 + $5 + $5 = $15"). That is meaningless: all three buyers face the same market price. On the graph, you add the horizontal distances (quantities), never the vertical ones (prices).

04

The five determinants of demand

When we drew Joe's demand curve, we changed only the price of corn. We kept everything else constant ("other things equal"). But in real life, the "other things" do change. When one of them changes, buyers want to buy a different amount at every price, so the whole demand curve moves. These "other things" are called the determinants of demand.

DefinitionDeterminants of demand (déterminants de la demande) — "demand shifters"

The factors other than the product's own price that affect how much consumers buy. They are held constant when a demand curve is drawn. When one of them changes, the demand curve shifts.

The textbook lists five determinants:

  1. Tastes (preferences) of consumers
  2. Number of buyers in the market
  3. Incomes of consumers
  4. Prices of related goods
  5. Expectations of consumers
Memory trick

T-N-I-P-E: "Tunisians Need Inexpensive Phones Everywhere" = Tastes, Number of buyers, Income, Prices of related goods, Expectations.

DefinitionIncrease and decrease in demand

An increase in demand means buyers want more at every price: the demand curve shifts to the right (from D1 to D2). A decrease in demand means buyers want less at every price: the curve shifts to the left (from D1 to D3).

1. Tastes (preferences)

A favorable change in consumer tastes (because of fashion, advertising, health news or new products) means more is demanded at each price: demand increases. An unfavorable change decreases demand.

  • Textbook: digital cameras greatly decreased the demand for film cameras. Health concerns increased the demand for broccoli, low-calorie drinks and fresh fruit, and decreased the demand for beef, veal, eggs and whole milk. Over the years, the demand for coffee drinks, table wine, DVDs and iPhones increased.
  • Tunisia: the popularity of fitness and running in Tunis increased the demand for gym memberships and sports shoes. As more people try to eat less sugar, the demand for sugary soft drinks decreases. When a product goes viral on TikTok or Instagram, its demand can jump overnight.

2. Number of buyers

More buyers in a market → demand increases. Fewer buyers → demand decreases.

  • Textbook: as people live longer, there are more older people, so the demand for motor homes, medical care and retirement communities increases. Immigration into the U.S. Southwest increased the demand for many products there. Trade agreements gave American farmers new foreign buyers. Young people leaving small rural towns decreased the demand for housing, home appliances and auto repair in those towns.
  • Tunisia: every summer, millions of foreign tourists and Tunisians living abroad arrive in Djerba, Hammamet and Sousse: the demand for hotel rooms, restaurant meals and car rentals increases. In September, thousands of new students arrive in Tunis: the demand for rooms and apartments near the universities increases.

3. Income: normal goods and inferior goods

How does a change in income affect demand? It depends on the product.

DefinitionNormal good (bien normal), also called superior good

A good whose demand varies directly with money income: when income rises, demand increases; when income falls, demand decreases. Most goods are normal goods.

DefinitionInferior good (bien inférieur)

A good whose demand varies inversely with money income: when income rises, demand decreases; when income falls, demand increases. People buy these goods when they cannot afford better alternatives.

Common mistake · "inferior" does not mean "bad quality"

An inferior good is defined only by how its demand reacts to income. "Inferior" is a technical word, not an insult. The same good can be normal for one person and inferior for another. A student who gets a scholarship may buy more second-hand clothes (normal for him), while a manager who gets a raise buys fewer (inferior for her). In an exam, use the definition: "demand varies inversely with income".

4. Prices of related goods: substitutes, complements, independent goods

The price of another product can shift the demand for this product. Everything depends on how the two goods are related.

DefinitionSubstitute good (bien substituable)

A good that can be used in place of another good. If the price of one rises, the demand for the other increases (same direction).

DefinitionComplementary good (bien complémentaire)

A good that is used together with another good. If the price of one rises, the demand for the other decreases (opposite direction).

DefinitionIndependent goods (biens indépendants)

Goods that are not related at all. A change in the price of one has no effect on the demand for the other.

5. Consumer expectations

What consumers expect about the future changes what they buy today.

  • Expected higher future prices → people buy now, before the price rises → current demand increases. Textbook: in "hot" real-estate markets, buyers rush in because they think prices will keep rising; speculators buy for the same reason. Tunisia: a few weeks before Eid al-Adha, if families expect the price of sheep to rise even more in the last days, many buy earlier, so demand increases now. Rumours that a subsidized product (sugar, semolina, cooking oil) will soon be missing push people to buy several packs at once — which can create the shortage they feared.
  • Expected lower future prices → people wait → current demand decreases. Example: many people postpone buying clothes in January because the sales (soldes) are coming.
  • Expected higher future income → people spend more now. Textbook: first-round draft picks in the U.S. National Football League buy luxury cars before they receive their first big contract. Tunisia: a graduate who has just signed a well-paid job contract may buy a car on credit before his first salary arrives.
  • Fear of losing one's job → people spend less now. Textbook: demand for vacation travel decreases.

Change in demand vs change in quantity demanded

This is the most important distinction of the chapter, and the one students lose most marks on. The words sound almost the same, but they describe two different things.

DefinitionChange in demand (variation de la demande)

A shift of the whole demand curve (to the right = increase, to the left = decrease). It is caused by a change in one of the five determinants.

DefinitionChange in quantity demanded (variation de la quantité demandée)

A movement from one point to another point on the same demand curve. It is caused only by a change in the price of the product itself.

Now we move to the other side of the market: the sellers. Good news: supply works like demand, with the same tools (schedule, curve, law, determinants, shift vs movement). Only the direction of the law is reversed.

The law of supply

DefinitionLaw of supply (loi de l'offre)

Other things equal, as price rises, the quantity supplied rises, and as price falls, the quantity supplied falls. There is a positive or direct relationship (relation directe) between price and quantity supplied. So the supply curve slopes upward (pente positive).

Price ↑ → Quantity supplied ↑   ·   Price ↓ → Quantity supplied ↓
Compare

Demand: inverse relationship, curve goes down. Supply: direct relationship, curve goes up. For a buyer, price is an obstacle. For a seller, price is revenue, so it is an incentive.

Why does the supply curve slope upward? Two reasons

Reason 1 — Price is an incentive. For a producer, the price is money received for each unit (revenue). A higher price makes production more attractive. The textbook's corn farmer: when the price of corn rises, the farmer can cover the costs of more seed, fertilizer and pesticides, and even plant corn on land he used for other crops. So he offers more corn. When the price falls, he plants less.

Reason 2 — Rising marginal cost. Marginal cost (coût marginal) is the extra cost of producing one more unit. A factory has a fixed size in the short run: a fixed number of machines and a fixed building. To produce more, the manager adds workers. At first this works well. But after some point, the new workers must wait for a free machine, or work in each other's way. Each extra unit then costs more to produce. A producer will make these more expensive units only if the price is high enough to cover their cost.

Worked example · baklawa before Eid in Sfax

A pastry shop in Sfax makes boxes of baklawa. Its oven and its kitchen are fixed. Its marginal cost rises as it produces more:

At 35 TND, even night-shift boxes pay, so she supplies 180. Higher price → larger quantity supplied: the law of supply, explained by rising marginal cost.

The total quantity supplied by all producers at each possible price. Like market demand, it is found by horizontal summation: at each price, add the quantities supplied by all sellers.

The six determinants of supply, and shift vs movement

A supply curve is drawn with "other things equal". When one of these other things changes, producers want to sell a different amount at every price: the whole supply curve shifts.

DefinitionDeterminants of supply (déterminants de l'offre) — "supply shifters"

The factors other than the product's own price that affect how much producers offer for sale. The textbook lists six: (1) resource prices; (2) technology; (3) taxes and subsidies; (4) prices of other goods; (5) producer expectations; (6) number of sellers in the market.

Memory trick

"Rich Tech Tycoons Pay Extra Now" = Resource prices, Technology, Taxes and subsidies, Prices of other goods, Expectations, Number of sellers.

The key idea: anything that changes the COST of production (other than the quantity produced) shifts the supply curve. Lower costs → supply increases (right). Higher costs → supply decreases (left).
DefinitionIncrease and decrease in supply

An increase in supply: producers offer more at every price; the supply curve shifts to the right (S1 → S2). A decrease in supply: producers offer less at every price; the curve shifts to the left (S1 → S3).

1. Resource prices

Resources (land, labor, capital, raw materials) are inputs. If they become more expensive, each unit costs more to make, so firms offer less at each price: supply decreases. If they become cheaper, supply increases.

  • Textbook: higher prices of sand, crushed rock and Portland cement increase the cost of making concrete and reduce its supply. A lower price of flat-panel glass increases the supply of big-screen TVs. Cheaper microchips increase the supply of computers (Table 3.2).
  • Tunisia: when fertilizer, diesel for tractors and irrigation water become more expensive, the supply of tomatoes and potatoes decreases. When the minimum wage paid to workers rises, it raises costs in labor-intensive industries such as textiles.

2. Technology

Better technology lets firms produce the same output with fewer resources. Costs fall, so supply increases.

  • Textbook: flat-panel computer monitors have become much cheaper to produce; better wireless technology increased the supply of cell phones.
  • Tunisia: drip irrigation (goutte-à-goutte) and greenhouses in regions such as Sidi Bouzid let farmers grow more vegetables with less water: supply increases. Modern olive-oil mills extract more oil from the same olives.

3. Taxes and subsidies

Businesses treat most taxes as costs. A higher sales tax or property tax raises production costs and reduces supply. A subsidy (subvention) is money the government pays to producers. The textbook calls it "a tax in reverse": it lowers costs and increases supply.

  • Textbook: a higher excise tax on cigarettes reduces their supply; lower state subsidies to public universities reduce the supply of higher education.
  • Tunisia: the state subsidizes the flour that bakeries use to make the cheap subsidized baguette. Without that subsidy, bakeries would supply far fewer baguettes at the official low price. A new tax per pack of cigarettes would reduce the supply of cigarettes.

4. Prices of other goods

Many firms can produce several products with the same resources. If the price of another product they could make rises, they move resources to it, and the supply of the first product decreases. This is called substitution in production.

  • Textbook: a firm that makes soccer balls can also make basketballs and volleyballs. If the prices of basketballs and volleyballs rise, it makes more of them and fewer soccer balls: the supply of soccer balls decreases. Another example: a higher price of cucumbers reduces the supply of watermelons (farmers plant cucumbers on the same land).
  • Tunisia: a farmer in Cap Bon can grow tomatoes or strawberries on the same land. If the price of strawberries rises, she plants more strawberries and fewer tomatoes: the supply of tomatoes decreases.
Do not confuse

"Prices of related goods" (demand side) is about goods that consumers use instead of or together with the product. "Prices of other goods" (supply side) is about goods that producers could make with the same resources. Different sides, different logic.

5. Producer expectations

Expectations about the future price can change how much producers offer today. The effect is hard to predict in general:

  • A farmer who expects a higher wheat price later may hold back some of today's harvest to sell later: current supply decreases. (Textbook: an expected rise in future log prices reduces today's supply of logs.)
  • A manufacturer who expects higher prices may add a shift of workers and produce more now: current supply increases.
  • A producer who expects a lower price later prefers to sell as much as possible now: current supply increases.
  • Tunisia: olive-oil producers who expect world prices to rise in the coming months keep some oil in their tanks: today's supply decreases.

6. Number of sellers

Other things equal, the more suppliers there are, the greater the market supply. More firms → supply shifts right; fewer firms → left.

  • Textbook: the U.S. and Canada restricted haddock fishing and the U.S. government even bought some fishing boats to take them out of service: fewer sellers, so the supply of haddock decreased. More tattoo parlors increased the supply of tattoos; new women's professional basketball leagues increased the supply of women's games.
  • Tunisia: when many new cafés open in a neighbourhood like Lac 2, the supply of café services there increases. During the seasonal fishing ban for trawlers ("repos biologique"), fewer boats fish, so the supply of fish decreases.
What about the weather?

Droughts, floods and diseases are not one of the six named determinants, but they clearly shift supply: the same land and work give less output. Example: a drought reduces the olive harvest, so the supply of olive oil decreases (shift left). In an exam, simply say "supply decreases (shifts left) because the drought destroys part of the harvest".

DefinitionChange in supply (variation de l'offre)

A shift of the whole supply curve, caused by a change in one of the six determinants. Right = increase; left = decrease.

DefinitionChange in quantity supplied (variation de la quantité offerte)

A movement from one point to another on the same supply curve, caused only by a change in the product's own price. Textbook: when the price of corn falls from $4 to $3, the quantity supplied falls from 10,000 to 7,000 bushels: a move from point b to point a on S1.

Common mistake

"The price of tomatoes rose, so the supply of tomatoes increased." Wrong. The product's own price causes a movement along the curve: "the quantity supplied increased". And be careful with taxes: a new tax does not "increase the supply because the price goes up". A tax is a cost: it decreases supply (shift left).

Increase in supply = right, NOT up

When supply increases, the curve moves to the right (more at each price). Because the curve slopes upward, a shift to the right looks lower on the graph. Many students draw an "increase" as a curve moved up — that is a decrease! Always think horizontally: right = more, left = less.

Market equilibrium: where supply meets demand

We now have the two sides of the corn market: 200 buyers (D1) and 200 farmers (S1). Let us put the two schedules side by side and ask the big question: which price will the market actually reach? This is the textbook's key graph, Figure 3.6.

Look for the row where the two quantities are equal. At $3, farmers want to sell 7,000 bushels and buyers want to buy 7,000 bushels. Everybody who wants to buy at $3 can buy, and everybody who wants to sell at $3 can sell. Nothing pushes the price up or down. This is the equilibrium.

DefinitionEquilibrium price (prix d'équilibre) — also "market-clearing price"

The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. On a graph, it is where the demand curve and the supply curve cross. "Equilibrium" means "in balance" or "at rest": once the market reaches it, there is no pressure to change.

DefinitionEquilibrium quantity (quantité d'équilibre)

The quantity demanded and supplied at the equilibrium price. In the corn market: 7,000 bushels per week at $3.

DefinitionSurplus (excédent, excès d'offre)

The amount by which quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at a given price. It happens when the price is above the equilibrium price.

DefinitionShortage (pénurie, excès de demande)

The amount by which quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at a given price. It happens when the price is below the equilibrium price.

Surplus = Qs − Qd (when P > equilibrium)  ·  Shortage = Qd − Qs (when P < equilibrium)

Why surpluses push prices down

Suppose the price of corn is $4. Farmers bring 10,000 bushels to the market, but buyers want only 4,000. 6,000 bushels stay unsold. What does a farmer with unsold corn do? Rather than keep it in the barn, he offers a slightly lower price to attract buyers. Other farmers do the same: the sellers compete with each other, and the price starts to fall. As the price falls, two things happen at the same time:

  • the quantity demanded rises (law of demand: movement down along D);
  • the quantity supplied falls (law of supply: movement down along S), because the high-cost farmers produce less.

The surplus shrinks, and it disappears completely at $3.

Why shortages push prices up

Now suppose the price is $2. Buyers want 11,000 bushels, but farmers offer only 4,000. 7,000 bushels are missing. Some buyers go home empty-handed. To get the scarce corn, buyers start to offer more than $2: they bid the price up. As the price rises, the quantity demanded falls (some buyers give up) and the quantity supplied rises (farmers are encouraged to sell more). The shortage shrinks and disappears at $3.

Surpluses drive prices DOWN. Shortages drive prices UP. Only at the equilibrium price is there no pressure to change.
Tunisian examples

Watermelons in late July (a surplus): trucks full of watermelons arrive from the farms. If sellers keep the price too high, many watermelons stay unsold and start to spoil. So sellers cut the price, more people buy, and the market clears at a lower price.

Sheep on the last days before Eid (a shortage): if the sellers in a market ask a price that is too low for the number of sheep available, the sheep are gone by mid-morning and many families find nothing. The next day, sellers ask more, and families who really want a sheep accept to pay more. The price rises until the number of sheep that families want equals the number available.

Common mistake

Many students think "shortage" means "the product is expensive". No: a shortage is a quantity gap at a price that is too low. Similarly, a surplus does not mean "the product is cheap"; it is a gap that appears when the price is too high. Also, the sentence "surpluses drive prices up; shortages drive them down" is false — it is the reverse (end-of-chapter Key Question 8).

The rationing function of prices

DefinitionRationing function of prices (fonction de rationnement des prix)

The ability of the competitive forces of supply and demand to establish a price at which selling and buying decisions are consistent (the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer).

At $3, the market "rations" the corn: every buyer who is willing and able to pay $3 gets corn; buyers who are not willing or able to pay $3 go without. Every seller who is willing and able to sell at $3 sells; the others do not. No minister, no queue, no coupon is needed: the price does the rationing. Remember Chapter 2: this is the market system answering "who gets the output?"

Efficient allocation: productive and allocative efficiency

A competitive market does not only find a price. It also tends to produce an efficient result. Economists use two kinds of efficiency.

DefinitionProductive efficiency (efficacité productive)

Producing any particular good in the least costly way. Competition forces producers to use the best technologies and the cheapest combination of resources.

Why it matters: suppose society has $100 of resources. If a bushel of corn can be produced with $3 of resources, producing it leaves $97 of resources for other goods. If it were produced in a wasteful way costing $5, only $95 would be left. Least-cost production frees resources for other uses, so society gets more goods in total.

DefinitionAllocative efficiency (efficacité allocative)

Producing the particular mix of goods and services most highly valued by society. Resources go to the products people want most.

Textbook examples: society wants land to grow corn, not dandelions; it wants diamonds used for jewelry, not mixed into concrete; it wants MP3 players, not cassette players.

The link with Chapter 1 (MB = MC): the demand curve shows how much buyers value each extra bushel: it reflects the marginal benefit (MB) of corn. The supply curve shows the extra cost of producing each extra bushel: it reflects the marginal cost (MC). At the equilibrium (7,000 bushels), the two curves cross, so MB = MC — exactly the optimal rule you learned in Chapter 1.

  • If the market produced less than 7,000 bushels, the next bushel would be worth more to buyers than it costs to produce (MB > MC): too few resources would go to corn (under-allocation).
  • If it produced more than 7,000, the extra bushels would cost more than they are worth (MC > MB): too many resources would go to corn (over-allocation).
Competitive equilibrium: MB = MC → no under-allocation and no over-allocation of resources → allocative efficiency.

Changes in supply, demand, and equilibrium

In real markets, the determinants of demand and supply change all the time: incomes rise, a drought hits, a new technology appears, Ramadan arrives. Each time, a curve shifts and the market moves to a new equilibrium. Exam questions on this topic are very common, so learn the method.

The 4-step method (use it for every question)

Step 1. Which curve does the event affect: demand, supply, or both? (Ask: does it change what buyers want, or what it costs sellers to produce?) Step 2. Which direction: increase (right) or decrease (left)? Step 3. Draw the old curves, then the new curve. Step 4. Compare the new crossing point with the old one: did the price rise or fall? Did the quantity rise or fall?

Consider this · salsa and coffee beans

Suppose a salsa company sold 1 million bottles at $4 in year 1, 2 million at $5 in year 2, and 3 million at $6 in year 3. Price up, quantity up: does this refute the law of demand? No. The law of demand holds other things equal, along one curve. Over these three years, tastes for salsa and incomes changed: the demand curve shifted right each year. The three points are on three different demand curves. Price and quantity both rose because demand increased (panel a).

Same idea for coffee: in some years the price of coffee rises while the amount harvested falls. This does not refute the law of supply. Bad weather decreased supply (the curve shifted left), so the price rose and the quantity fell (panel d).

The laws of demand and supply are about movements along ONE curve, other things equal. When "other things" change over time, the curves shift — and the laws are not broken.

Government-set prices: price ceilings and price floors

Sometimes a government decides that the market price is "too high" for consumers or "too low" for producers, and fixes a legal price. The supply-and-demand model shows what happens next — and why good intentions often lead to bad results.

Price ceilings

DefinitionPrice ceiling (prix plafond)

A maximum legal price that a seller may charge for a product or service. The usual reason: to help consumers obtain an "essential" good they could not afford at the equilibrium price. Examples: rent controls, usury laws (maximum interest rates).

A ceiling matters only if it is set below the equilibrium price. A ceiling above equilibrium changes nothing: the market price is already lower than the legal maximum.

Textbook example · a price ceiling on gasoline (Figure 3.8)

Rising incomes around the world increase the demand for gasoline, and the equilibrium price reaches P0 = $3.50 per gallon. Consumers complain, and the government sets a ceiling at Pc = $3.00.

At $3.00, consumers want to buy Qd gallons, but sellers offer only Qs. The result is a persistent shortage of Qd − Qs. In a free market the shortage would push the price back up to $3.50 — but now that is illegal. So the shortage stays.

(If the ceiling were set at $4.00, above the $3.50 equilibrium, it would have no effect.)

What problems does a ceiling create?

  1. The rationing problem. The price no longer decides who gets the scarce gasoline. Some other method must. Maybe first come, first served: long queues at gas stations. Maybe favoritism: sellers keep gasoline for friends and regular customers. To be fair, the government may print ration coupons (tickets de rationnement) and give each family the same number of coupons. But then a large family that needs more gasoline gets the same amount as a small one.
  2. Black markets (marchés noirs). Some buyers are ready to pay more than $3.00, and some sellers are happy to sell to them. Illegal trading above the legal price appears. People may even sell or counterfeit (fake) ration coupons.
  3. Costs and pressure. The government needs a costly bureaucracy to print coupons, control stations and punish cheaters. And once a ceiling exists, there is often political pressure to set it even lower.

Rent controls

About 200 U.S. cities, including New York, Boston and San Francisco, have used rent controls (contrôle des loyers) at some time: a maximum legal rent for apartments. The goal was good — to protect low-income families from high rents. But the effects on supply were bad:

  • At the low controlled rent, the quantity demanded of apartments is high.
  • Some landlords sell their buildings or convert them into condominiums (apartments for sale), which are not rent-controlled. The number of rental units falls.
  • With low rents, landlords have little money and little reason to repair and renovate. Buildings decay; some are abandoned.
  • Investors build office buildings, shopping malls or motels instead of rental housing, because those are not controlled. Too few resources go to rental housing.

So rent controls are a primary cause of the housing shortages they were meant to fix. That is why most American cities have abandoned rent controls or are dismantling them.

Tunisian link · subsidized bread and basic goods

Tunisia fixes low consumer prices for several basic goods, such as subsidized bread, flour, semolina, cooking oil and sugar. These fixed prices work like a price ceiling: sellers may not charge more than the official price. The difference with the pure textbook case is that the state also pays subsidies (through the compensation fund, the Caisse Générale de Compensation) so that producers and importers can keep supplying at the low price.

The model still helps to understand what happens when supply cannot keep up: at the low fixed price, the quantity demanded is large, so a shortage appears. In 2022–2023, for example, shops often ran short of some subsidized products, and queues formed (there were several causes, including difficulties in financing imports). And, as the model predicts, some subsidized goods were diverted to other uses or resold above the official price, which the authorities try to control. This is the rationing problem and the black-market problem of a price ceiling.

Price floors

DefinitionPrice floor (prix plancher)

A minimum legal price fixed by the government. The usual reason: to support the incomes of certain producers or workers. Examples: price supports for farm products, and the minimum wage (a price floor on the price of labor).

A floor matters only if it is set above the equilibrium price.

Textbook example · a price floor on wheat (Figure 3.9)

The equilibrium price of wheat is $2 per bushel. To raise farmers' incomes, the government sets a floor at Pf = $3. At $3, farmers offer Qs bushels but buyers want only Qd: a persistent surplus of Qs − Qd. In a free market the surplus would push the price down to $2 — but that is now illegal.

How can the government deal with the surplus?

  1. Make the surplus smaller: restrict supply (for example "acreage allotments": farmers agree to plant fewer acres) or increase demand (for example by researching new uses for wheat).
  2. Buy the surplus at $3 and store it or dispose of it.

Side effects of a price floor:

  • Misallocation of resources: the high price keeps inefficient, high-cost farmers in wheat production. Too many resources go to wheat: no allocative efficiency.
  • Consumers pay more for wheat and for wheat products such as bread and pasta.
  • Taxpayers pay for buying and storing the surplus.
  • Environmental damage: farmers plant wheat even on poor, erosion-prone land, and use more fertilizer and pesticides.
  • International problems: the high domestic price attracts imports, so the government adds tariffs (import taxes); other countries may retaliate with tariffs on its exports.
Price ceiling below equilibrium → shortage. Price floor above equilibrium → surplus. Good intentions, bad economic outcomes.
Memory trick · the names look upside down

A ceiling (plafond) is a lid that keeps the price down, so to matter it sits low, below equilibrium → too little is supplied → shortage. A floor (plancher) keeps the price up, so to matter it sits high, above equilibrium → too much is supplied → surplus.

Tunisian link · the minimum wage (SMIG)

The SMIG (salaire minimum interprofessionnel garanti) is a minimum legal price for labor: a price floor. In the simple model, a floor set above the equilibrium wage creates a surplus of labor (more people want to work at that wage than employers want to hire). Whether real minimum wages cause much unemployment is debated by economists; you will study labor markets later. For now, just recognize it as the textbook's example of a price floor.

Typical question: "Show graphically the effect of a price ceiling / floor, and list its consequences." Then list the consequences: ceiling → rationing problem, black markets, enforcement costs; floor → surplus purchases by government, consumers pay more, misallocation, taxpayers' cost, environment, trade conflicts.

Last Word · a legal market for human organs?

In the United States in 2007, about 97,000 people were on waiting lists for an organ transplant (kidney, liver, heart…), and about 6,000 died each year because there were not enough organs. Why? Selling organs is illegal, so the legal price is effectively zero, and the only supply is donations.

  • The case for a market: a positive price would encourage more people to supply organs (S2 slopes upward). The quantity of transplants would rise from Q1 to Q2, and more lives would be saved.
  • Objections: (1) moral — it is wrong to turn the human body into a commodity, and poor or uninsured patients would be left out; (2) health costs — paying for organs would make medical care more expensive.
  • Reply of supporters: an illegal market worth an estimated $1 billion per year already exists (for example, poor villagers in Pakistan selling a kidney to pay their debts). A legal, regulated market would increase legal supply, lower the price and reduce these abuses.

In Tunisia, as in almost every country, selling organs is illegal; organs can only be donated.

Link to Chapter 1 · positive vs normative

This debate mixes two kinds of statements. Positive statements describe what is or what would happen and can be checked with data: "A legal market would increase the number of organs supplied." Normative statements say what ought to be, based on values: "It is immoral to sell a kidney." Economics can answer the positive questions; the final choice is normative.

A technological advance lets firms make each tire with fewer resources, so the cost per tire falls. At every price, producers now offer more tires: supply increases (shift right).
Q93KQ6 (b)

A decline in the number of firms in the tire industry.

Market supply adds up the quantities of all firms. With fewer firms in the industry, fewer tires are offered at every price: supply decreases (shift left), like the textbook haddock example.
Q94KQ6 (c)

An increase in the price of rubber used in the production of tires.

Rubber is a resource. A higher input price raises costs → supply decreases.
Q95KQ6 (d)

The expectation that the equilibrium price of auto tires will be lower in the future than it is currently.

Producer expectations: if tires will sell for less later, producers prefer to sell as many as possible now → current supply increases.
Q96KQ6 (e)

A decline in the price of large tires used for semi trucks and earth-hauling rigs (with no change in the price of auto tires).

Price of another good the firms could produce: truck tires are now less profitable, so producers shift resources to auto tires → supply of auto tires increases.
Q97KQ6 (f)

The levying of a per-unit tax on each auto tire sold.

Firms treat a per-unit tax as an extra cost on every tire they sell. To offer the same number of tires they now need a higher price, so at every price they offer fewer: supply decreases (shift left).
Q98KQ6 (g)

The granting of a 50-cent-per-unit subsidy for each auto tire produced.

A subsidy is a "tax in reverse": it lowers the cost per tire → supply increases.

Questions 7 and 12 · using the words correctly

Critically evaluate each statement: is the vocabulary used correctly?

Q99Q7 (statement 1)

"In the corn market, demand often exceeds supply and supply sometimes exceeds demand."

Incorrect. Demand and supply are whole schedules (curves); one curve cannot "exceed" another. The correct statement is: "At some prices, the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied (shortage), and at other prices the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded (surplus)."
Q100Q7 (statement 2)

"The price of corn rises and falls in response to changes in supply and demand."

Correct. Here "supply" and "demand" mean the curves, and shifts of these curves do change the equilibrium price.
Q101Q12

"Figure 3.7b (demand decreases) shows that a smaller amount is demanded at a lower price. This refutes the law of demand."

Incorrect. The law of demand describes movements along one demand curve, other things equal. In Figure 3.7b the demand curve itself shifted left. The lower price and smaller quantity are two points on two different curves, so the law is not refuted.

Key Question 14 · price ceiling and price floor on wheat

P14KQ14

Refer to the KQ8 table. (a) Suppose the government establishes a price ceiling of $3.70 for wheat. What might prompt the government to do this? Explain the effects of this ceiling and show them on a graph. (b) Now suppose the government sets a price floor of $4.60. Explain why it might do this, and the effects.

Show the full solution

(a) Ceiling at $3.70. Why: the government may want to keep bread and other wheat products affordable for consumers, for example after a sharp price rise, or under political pressure. Effect: $3.70 is below the $4.00 equilibrium, so it is binding. Quantity demanded = 80,000; quantity supplied = 73,000 → shortage of 80 − 73 = 7 thousand bushels. Consequences: a rationing problem (queues, favoritism, maybe ration coupons) and black markets where wheat is sold above $3.70.

(b) Floor at $4.60. Why: to raise farmers' incomes. Effect: $4.60 is above the equilibrium, so it is binding. Quantity supplied = 79,000; quantity demanded = 65,000 → surplus of 79 − 65 = 14 thousand bushels. The government must buy and store the surplus (or restrict supply / increase demand); consumers pay more for wheat products; taxpayers pay; resources are misallocated.

Question 16 · finding equilibrium with algebra

P15Q16 (advanced)

Demand: P = 10 − 0.2Qd. Supply: P = 2 + 0.2Qs. (P is the price, Qd and Qs the quantities.) Find the equilibrium price and quantity. Then compute the surplus or shortage at P = 8 and at P = 4.

Show the full solution

Step 1 — at equilibrium, Qd = Qs = Q, so the two prices are equal: 10 − 0.2Q = 2 + 0.2Q.

Step 2 — solve: 10 − 2 = 0.2Q + 0.2Q → 8 = 0.4Q → Q = 20.

Step 3 — find P: P = 10 − 0.2 × 20 = 10 − 4 = 6. Check with supply: 2 + 0.2 × 20 = 2 + 4 = 6. ✓

Equilibrium: P = 6, Q = 20

At P = 8 (above equilibrium): demand: 8 = 10 − 0.2Qd → Qd = 2 ÷ 0.2 = 10. Supply: 8 = 2 + 0.2Qs → Qs = 6 ÷ 0.2 = 30. Qs > Qd → surplus of 20.

At P = 4 (below equilibrium): Qd = (10 − 4) ÷ 0.2 = 30; Qs = (4 − 2) ÷ 0.2 = 10. Qd > Qs → shortage of 20.

Exam tip: write each equation as "Q = …" first if it helps: Qd = 50 − 5P and Qs = 5P − 10. Then 50 − 5P = 5P − 10 → 60 = 10P → P = 6, Q = 50 − 30 = 20. Same answer.

Questions 10, 11 and 13 · chains of effects

P16Q10

In 2001 an outbreak of foot-and-mouth disease in Europe led to the burning of millions of cattle carcasses. What impact might this have had on the supply of cattle hides, hide prices, the supply of leather goods, and the price of leather goods?

Show the model answer

Fewer cattle → fewer hides: the supply of cattle hides decreased (shift left) → the price of hides rose.

Hides are the main resource for leather. A higher input price raises the cost of leather goods → the supply of leather goods decreased (shift left) → the price of leather goods rose (and the quantity sold fell).

Notice the chain: one supply shock in one market becomes a resource price change for the next market.

P17Q11

Suppose the government greatly increases its subsidies to public colleges and universities. Predict the effects on (a) tuition and enrollments at public colleges and (b) tuition and enrollments at private colleges.

Show the model answer

(a) Public colleges: the subsidy lowers their costs → the supply of public education increases (shift right) → public tuition falls and enrollment rises.

(b) Private colleges: public and private colleges are substitutes. The price of the substitute (public tuition) fell → the demand for private education decreases (shift left) → private tuition falls and private enrollment falls.

P18Q13

On a stock exchange, the number of shares sold always equals the number of shares bought. Some people conclude: "Supply always equals demand, so supply and demand cannot explain why share prices change." Explain the error.

Show the model answer

Every trade has a buyer and a seller, so the quantity bought = quantity sold at the price at which trades happen. That is just the equilibrium condition (Qd = Qs at the market price).

But the curves can shift. If good news about a company increases the demand for its shares (or bad news reduces supply), there is a shortage at the old price, buyers bid the price up, and a new equilibrium price is reached where, again, quantity bought equals quantity sold. So prices change because demand and/or supply shift, while the quantities traded are always equal at the equilibrium price.

Recap and key terms

What to remember for class and the midterm

  • A market brings buyers and sellers together. This chapter studies highly competitive markets: many independent buyers and sellers, standardized product, price "discovered".
  • Demand = amounts buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, per period, other things equal. Law of demand: P ↑ → Qd ↓ (inverse). Why: price is an obstacle, diminishing marginal utility, income and substitution effects.
  • Market demand / supply = horizontal sum of individual quantities at each price (Joe + Jen + Jay at $3: 35 + 39 + 26 = 100; 200 buyers: D1 = 2,000 / 4,000 / 7,000 / 11,000 / 16,000).
  • 5 determinants of demand (T-N-I-P-E): tastes, number of buyers, income (normal vs inferior), prices of related goods (substitutes: same direction; complements: opposite), expectations.
  • Supply: law of supply P ↑ → Qs ↑ (direct), because price is an incentive and marginal cost rises. S1 = 12,000 / 10,000 / 7,000 / 4,000 / 1,000.
  • 6 determinants of supply: resource prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, prices of other goods, producer expectations, number of sellers. Anything that changes costs shifts supply.
  • Own price → movement along the curve (change in quantity demanded/supplied). Any determinant → shift of the curve (change in demand/supply). Right = increase, left = decrease.
  • Equilibrium: Qd = Qs ($3 and 7,000 bushels). Above it: surplus (Qs − Qd) → price falls. Below it: shortage (Qd − Qs) → price rises. Prices ration goods; equilibrium gives productive and allocative efficiency (MB = MC).
  • Single shifts: D ↑ → P ↑ Q ↑; D ↓ → P ↓ Q ↓; S ↑ → P ↓ Q ↑; S ↓ → P ↑ Q ↓. Double shifts: one result certain, the other indeterminate.
  • Price ceiling (maximum, below equilibrium) → shortage, rationing problem, black markets (gasoline, rent controls). Price floor (minimum, above equilibrium) → surplus, government purchases, higher consumer prices, misallocation (wheat, minimum wage).
  • Algebra: set Qd = Qs (or the two price equations equal). Q16: 10 − 0.2Q = 2 + 0.2Q → Q = 20, P = 6.

Glossary

Market
An institution or mechanism that brings buyers (demanders) and sellers (suppliers) together (marché).
Demand
A schedule or curve showing the amounts consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price during a period, other things equal (demande).
Demand schedule
A table of prices and the quantity demanded at each price (barème de demande).
Quantity demanded
The amount buyers want to buy at one specific price (quantité demandée).
Law of demand
Other things equal, price and quantity demanded are inversely related (loi de la demande).
Diminishing marginal utility
Each successive unit of a product gives less extra satisfaction (utilité marginale décroissante).
Income effect
A lower price raises the purchasing power of a given income, so buyers can buy more (effet revenu).
Substitution effect
A lower price makes the product cheaper relative to similar products, so buyers substitute it for them (effet de substitution).
Demand curve
The graph of a demand schedule; price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal; slopes downward (courbe de demande).
Market demand
The horizontal sum of all buyers' individual demands (demande du marché).
Determinants of demand
Tastes, number of buyers, income, prices of related goods, expectations; they shift the demand curve (déterminants de la demande).
Normal good
A good whose demand varies directly with income (bien normal).
Inferior good
A good whose demand varies inversely with income (bien inférieur).
Substitute good
A good used in place of another; price of one ↑ → demand for the other ↑ (bien substituable).
Complementary good
A good used together with another; price of one ↑ → demand for the other ↓ (bien complémentaire).
Independent goods
Goods with no relation; the price of one does not affect demand for the other (biens indépendants).
Change in demand
A shift of the whole demand curve caused by a determinant (variation de la demande).
Change in quantity demanded
A movement along a fixed demand curve caused by the product's own price (variation de la quantité demandée).
Supply
A schedule or curve showing the amounts producers are willing and able to sell at each possible price during a period, other things equal (offre).
Supply schedule
A table of prices and the quantity supplied at each price (barème d'offre).
Law of supply
Other things equal, price and quantity supplied are directly related (loi de l'offre).
Marginal cost
The extra cost of producing one more unit; it rises beyond some output, which explains the upsloping supply curve (coût marginal).
Supply curve
The graph of a supply schedule; slopes upward (courbe d'offre).
Determinants of supply
Resource prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, prices of other goods, producer expectations, number of sellers (déterminants de l'offre).
Subsidy
A government payment to producers; a "tax in reverse" that increases supply (subvention).
Change in supply
A shift of the whole supply curve caused by a determinant (variation de l'offre).
Change in quantity supplied
A movement along a fixed supply curve caused by the product's own price (variation de la quantité offerte).
Equilibrium price
The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; the market-clearing price (prix d'équilibre).
Equilibrium quantity
The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price (quantité d'équilibre).
Surplus
Quantity supplied minus quantity demanded at a price above equilibrium (excédent, excès d'offre).
Shortage
Quantity demanded minus quantity supplied at a price below equilibrium (pénurie, excès de demande).
Rationing function of prices
The ability of supply and demand to set a price at which buying and selling decisions are consistent (fonction de rationnement des prix).
Productive efficiency
Producing any good in the least costly way (efficacité productive).
Allocative efficiency
Producing the mix of goods most highly valued by society, where MB = MC (efficacité allocative).
Indeterminate
When two curves shift at once and push a variable in opposite directions, its change depends on which shift is larger (indéterminé).
Price ceiling
A maximum legal price; binding only below equilibrium; causes a shortage (prix plafond).
Price floor
A minimum legal price; binding only above equilibrium; causes a surplus (prix plancher).
Black market
Illegal trading at prices above a legal price ceiling (marché noir).
Rent control
A price ceiling on apartment rents (contrôle des loyers).
