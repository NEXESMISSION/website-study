01

What is economics?

Before you studied any economics, you already made dozens of economic decisions today. What to eat for breakfast. Whether to take the bus or a taxi to ISB. Whether to buy a coffee or a juice. Each time, you used something limited (your money, your time, your attention) and chose between things you wanted. That is economics.

DefinitionEconomics (l'économie)

The science that studies how individuals, businesses, and societies make choices to use limited resources in order to satisfy their needs and wants.

Look at the three key words in that definition:

  • Choices: economics is about deciding, not about money itself.
  • Limited resources: money, time, land, water, workers, machines. There is never enough of them.
  • Needs and wants: what people would like to have. This list never ends.
Economics is about making choices, because resources are limited.
Example · your morning

You have 10 TND and 40 minutes before class. A taxi costs 6 TND and takes 15 minutes. The bus costs 0.5 TND and takes 35 minutes. If you take the bus, you have 9.5 TND left for lunch (5.5 TND more than with the taxi), but you lose 20 minutes you could have used to review your notes. If you take the taxi, you gain time but have only 4 TND left for lunch.

Two limited resources (money and time), several wants (lunch, arriving relaxed, reviewing). You must choose. That's an economic decision.

Common mistake

"Economics only studies money and banks." False. Economics studies choices about any scarce resource: your time, a country's water, a hospital's beds. Money is just one of the resources.

Exactly the definition. Scarcity is a comparison: wants are bigger than the resources available.

02

Scarcity and choice

Scarcity is the starting point of all economic thinking. If everything were unlimited, nobody would need to choose, and there would be no economics.

DefinitionScarcity (la rareté)

A situation in which human wants for goods, services, and resources are greater than what is actually available to satisfy them.

Scarcity = Wants > Available resources

Because resources are scarce, everyone is forced to make choices: individuals, businesses, and governments.

Common mistake · scarcity ≠ poverty

Scarcity does not mean "being poor". Even the richest person in the world has only 24 hours a day and can't be in two places at once. Even rich countries have limited land, water, workers, and factories compared to what their people want. Everyone faces scarcity.

Example from the lecture · a student's time

A student has limited time. Between studying, working a part-time job, and spending time with friends, they cannot do all three fully. Every hour given to one activity is an hour taken from another. The way they divide their time shows their priorities, and the trade-offs scarcity forces on them.

Case study from class: water scarcity in Tunisia

Tunisia's water resources are under growing pressure, especially in dry summers. Many users compete for the same limited water:

Competing userWhat they want the water for
AgricultureIrrigating olive trees, citrus, vegetables. This is the biggest user.
HouseholdsDrinking, cooking, washing.
IndustryFactories, phosphate processing, food processing.
TourismHotels, swimming pools, golf courses in summer.

The authorities must make trade-offs: if more water goes to farms, less is left for households and hotels. In recent dry years, some regions have had night-time water cuts, and the use of drinking water for things like irrigation or washing cars has been restricted. Each of these decisions gives water to one use by taking it from another.

How to analyse any scarcity case (3 questions)
  1. What is the scarce resource? (water)
  2. Who are the competing users? (farms, homes, industry, tourism)
  3. What trade-off must be made? (more for one = less for another)

Then connect it to the definition: wants > available resources.

Check yourself

Q5Multiple choice

Which statement best describes scarcity?

Scarcity is the gap between unlimited wants and limited resources. It is not about money only (a), not about high prices (c), and it affects everyone, rich or poor (d).
Q6True or false

A very rich country like Switzerland does not face scarcity.

Every country has limited land, workers, time and production capacity compared to what its people want. Rich countries face scarcity too; they just face it at a higher level of wants.
P1Short answer

ISB has one big amphitheatre. On Wednesday at 14:00, the LBA 1 students want it for a revision session, the student club wants it for a conference, and a teacher wants it for a make-up class. Use the 3 questions to analyse this situation.

Show a model answer

1. Scarce resource: the amphitheatre at that time slot (one room, one time).

2. Competing users: LBA 1 students, the student club, the teacher.

3. Trade-off: whoever gets the room, the other two lose it. The administration must choose, for example by giving priority to teaching, and the cost of that choice is the conference or the revision session that can't happen.

This is scarcity: the wants (3 uses) are greater than the available resource (1 room).

03

Opportunity cost

Because of scarcity, every choice means giving something up. Economists have a precise name for what you give up.

DefinitionOpportunity cost (le coût d'opportunité)

The value of the next best alternative that you give up when you make a choice.

Read the definition slowly. Two details matter a lot on quizzes:

  • "Next best": only the single best option you gave up counts. You don't add up all the options you didn't choose.
  • "Value": it is not always money. It can be time, sleep, fun, effort, or anything that has value to you.
Examples from the lecture
  • Student: studying all evening → the opportunity cost is the party you missed, or the sleep you lost.
  • City government: uses its budget to build a hospital → the opportunity cost is the new school it could have built with the same money.
  • Business: invests in a new product line → the opportunity cost is the extra marketing it could have done for an existing product.

Worked example · Sami's weekend (slide exercise)

Step by step

Sami can either work a weekend shift earning 60 dinars, or tutor a neighbour's child for 40 dinars. He decides to tutor.

Step 1, what did he choose? Tutoring.

Step 2, what was the next best alternative? The weekend shift.

Step 3, what is its value? 60 dinars.

➜ Opportunity cost of tutoring = 60 dinars.

In money terms tutoring was the more expensive choice: he gave up 60 to earn 40. So why might he still choose it? Because of non-money value: helping the neighbour's family, flexible hours, enjoying teaching, building experience. Rational people count all benefits, not only money.

Exam alert · don't add up the options

Yasmine has a free Saturday. Her options: a paid shift at a shop (50 TND), babysitting (35 TND), or going to the beach. She goes to the beach.

Opportunity cost = 50 TND (the best option given up). It is not 50 + 35 = 85 TND, because she could only have done one of them anyway.

"There is no such thing as a free lunch"

This famous sentence means: nothing is truly free for society. A "free" public road, a "free" school meal, or "free" healthcare still uses workers, materials and land that could have produced something else. Someone pays, and something else is given up. The opportunity cost of a new public highway is the other goods and services that must be sacrificed to build it, not just the money paid to the contractors.

Activity from class · the 20-dinar decision

You have 20 dinars. Food, a cinema ticket, or transport for the week? Whatever you pick, the opportunity cost is the best of the other two. If Netflix raises its price, do you keep your subscription? One iPhone, or two cheaper phones? Every answer involves a scarce budget, competing wants, and an opportunity cost. That is microeconomics in action.

The opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative he gave up: the 60-dinar shift. 40 is what he gets, not what he gives up.

P2Problem

Following question 10: the shop closes and the 50 TND shift disappears. Yasmine still goes to the beach. What is her opportunity cost now? What does this show about opportunity cost?

Show the solution

Now the best remaining alternative is babysitting, so the opportunity cost of the beach becomes 35 TND.

This shows that opportunity cost depends on the alternatives available. The same choice can have a different cost when the options change.

P3Short answer

The Tunisian government decides to spend 200 million TND on a new highway. Give the opportunity cost in words, with two concrete examples.

Show a model answer

The opportunity cost is the other goods and services that those 200 million TND worth of resources could have produced (the best alternative use). Examples: new hospitals in the interior regions, a desalination plant to fight water scarcity, renovating schools. The money paid to contractors is only the accounting cost; the economic cost is what society gives up.

04

Microeconomics vs. macroeconomics

Economics has two branches. They look at the economy from different distances, a bit like a microscope and a satellite, and they complement each other.

DefinitionMicroeconomics (la microéconomie)

The study of individual economic units: households, firms, and markets.

  • Households (consumers) decide what to buy, how much to save, and how to use their time and income to maximise their satisfaction, called utility.
  • Firms (producers) decide what and how to produce, how to set prices, and how much to supply, to maximise profit.
  • Markets are where supply and demand meet and determine prices and quantities.
DefinitionMacroeconomics (la macroéconomie)

The study of the economy as a whole: aggregate (national or global) outcomes rather than individual decisions.

Macro variableWhat it measures
GDP (Gross Domestic Product, PIB)Total production of the economy
Price levelInflation or deflation trends
EmploymentJobs and the unemployment rate
Economic growthThe increase in a country's production capacity over time
Interest and exchange ratesAffect investment, consumption and international trade
One household, one firm, or one market → micro. The whole national (or world) economy → macro.
Tunisian examples
StatementBranch
The price of olive oil at a market in Sfax rises after a poor harvest.Micro (one market)
Tunisia's inflation rate this year.Macro (whole economy)
A bakery in Tunis hires two more workers.Micro (one firm)
Tunisia's national unemployment rate falls.Macro
The exchange rate of the dinar against the euro.Macro
A family decides to save 200 TND a month for a car.Micro (one household)
Trap · a big company is still micro

"The profit of Intel" or "Tunisie Télécom's prices" are about one firm, so they are micro, even if the company is huge. "How the price of wine affects how much wine people buy" is about one market, so it is micro too. Macro is only when you talk about the total economy: total output, total employment, the general price level, or a nationwide policy's effect on the whole economy.

05

The economic problem

At the heart of economics there is one tension, called the economic problem:

Unlimited wants vs. Scarce resources

Needs vs. wants

Needs (besoins)Wants (désirs)
MeaningBasic requirements for survivalDesires that improve quality of life but are not essential
ExamplesFood, water, shelter, safetyLuxury items, vacations, gadgets, a new phone

Human desires have no end: once a need is satisfied, new wants appear. You buy your first phone, then you want a better camera, then a newer model, then wireless earphones. This constant arrival of new wants, combined with limited resources, is what drives economic activity.

The four factors of production

To produce anything, an economy uses four types of scarce resources, called factors of production (facteurs de production). Each one is paid in its own way. Learn this table by heart; it comes up again in Chapter 2 (the circular flow).

FactorWhat it isExamplesPayment
Land (la terre)Natural resources, "gifts of nature"Farmland, forests, minerals, oil, waterRent
Labor (le travail)Human effort and skills, physical and mentalA barista, an engineer, a teacherWages
Capital (le capital)Man-made goods used to produce other goodsMachines, tools, factories, delivery vans, computersInterest
Entrepreneurship (l'esprit d'entreprise; textbook name: entrepreneurial ability)Organises the other three, takes risks, innovatesThe person who starts and runs the businessProfit
Memory trick

Read the payments in order: R-W-I-P. "Rent the land, Work for wages, Interest on machines, Profit for the boss."

Common mistake · money is NOT capital

In economics, capital means physical tools and machines that help produce. The money a firm borrows from a bank is financial capital, not a factor of production. Money by itself produces nothing; it's what you buy with it (an oven, a truck) that is capital.

Also: the same object can be capital or not depending on its use. A car used by a family is a consumer good; the same car used by a taxi driver to earn money is capital.

In economics, investment (l'investissement) means spending on the production and accumulation of capital goods (machines, tools, buildings). Buying shares or putting money in a bank is a financial investment, not investment in the economic sense.

Case study from class · a new café in Sfax

Amira wants to open a small café. She rents a shop downtown, hires two baristas, buys an espresso machine, and designs a unique concept to stand out from competitors. Identify the four factors:

FactorIn Amira's caféPaid as
LandThe site of the shop downtown (the location / ground it stands on)Rent to the landowner
LaborThe two baristasWages
CapitalThe espresso machineInterest (on the money tied up in it)
EntrepreneurshipAmira's concept and her risk-takingProfit (if the café succeeds)

Careful: the shop building is man-made, so strictly it is capital, like a factory or a warehouse. Only the natural site under it is land. If a quiz asks "a factory building / warehouse / office is which resource?", answer capital. (In everyday language we "rent" a shop; in economics, rent is the payment for land.)

The three fundamental questions

Because resources are scarce, every society, whatever its economic system, must answer three questions:

QuestionMeaningExample
What to produce?Which goods and services, and how much of eachConsumer goods or capital goods? Healthcare or defence? More olive oil for export or for local consumption?
How to produce?Which methods and technologyLabor-intensive (hand-picking olives with many workers) or capital-intensive (harvesting machines with few workers)?
For whom to produce?How output is shared among peopleBased on income (who can pay), need (public hospitals), effort, or other criteria

06

Goods and services

Economies produce goods (physical things) and services (activities) to satisfy wants. Classifying them helps analyse how people behave and how markets work. There are four classic pairs:

PairFirst typeSecond type
Free vs. economicFree goods: unlimited, no effort needed to get them (air, sunlight)Economic goods: scarce, need effort or cost to produce (food, clothes, cars)
Consumer vs. capitalConsumer goods: used directly by households (food, clothes)Capital goods: used to produce other goods (machinery, tools)
Durable vs. non-durableDurable: last a long time (cars, furniture, a fridge)Non-durable: used up quickly (food, drinks, fuel)
Private vs. publicPrivate goods: rival and excludable (a pizza, a car)Public goods: non-rival and non-excludable (streetlights, national defence)

Rival and excludable, explained

  • Rival: if I use it, you can't use the same unit. If I eat this pizza, you can't eat it.
  • Excludable: the owner can stop people who don't pay. A pizzeria won't give you the pizza without payment.
  • A streetlight is non-rival (my using the light doesn't reduce yours) and non-excludable (you can't stop someone from walking under it). That's why governments usually provide public goods: no private firm can easily charge for them.

Services

DefinitionServices (les services)

Intangible activities performed for others. They cannot be stored, and they are usually produced and consumed at the same time.

Examples: haircuts, medical care, education, transport, banking. A haircut is "produced" by the barber at the exact moment you "consume" it, and the barber can't make 50 haircuts in advance and store them.

Exam alert · the same good can change category

Classification depends on use. Flour bought by a family = consumer good; flour bought by a bakery to make bread is an input. A laptop for watching films = consumer good; the same laptop used by a graphic designer to earn money = capital good. Always ask: who uses it, and for what?

07

Rationality and marginal analysis

Economics assumes people and firms are rational: they make decisions to maximise their own well-being.

  • Consumers try to maximise their satisfaction (utility).
  • Firms try to maximise their profit.

How do they decide how much to do something? Not with "all or nothing" choices, but step by step, one more unit at a time. In economics, "marginal" means "one more" / "the extra unit".

DefinitionMarginal benefit (MB) and marginal cost (MC)

Marginal benefit (bénéfice marginal): the extra gain from one more unit of an activity.

Marginal cost (coût marginal): the extra cost of that same additional unit.

MB > MC → do it  ·  MB < MC → don't  ·  Best point: MB = MC
Example from the lecture · the bakery

Should the bakery make one more cookie?

✓ The cookie earns 2 dinars (MB) and costs 1 dinar (MC) to make → MB > MC → yes, make it (+1 dinar).

✗ The next cookie would cost 2 dinars (MC) and earn only 1 dinar (MB) → MC > MB → no, it would lose 1 dinar.

Worked example · should the food truck sell more? (slide exercise)

A food truck owner tracks the benefit and cost of each extra sandwich:

Sandwich #MB (dinars)MC (dinars)MB − MCMake it?
1st83+5Yes, MB > MC
2nd64+2Yes, MB > MC
3rd550Best stopping point (MB = MC)
4th36−3No, MC > MB

Answer: the best quantity is 3 sandwiches, where MB = MC. Sandwiches 1 and 2 add net benefit (MB > MC). The 3rd adds exactly 0, so the total is the same with 2 or 3, and the rule names the quantity where MB = MC. Don't make the 4th (MC > MB). Notice why: the total net benefit after 2 sandwiches is 5 + 2 = 7 dinars, after 3 it's still 7, and after 4 it falls to 4. Going past MB = MC reduces the total.

Your own life

You've studied 3 hours tonight. Should you do a 4th? Compare the extra marks the 4th hour will bring (MB) with what it extra costs you (MC: sleep, tiredness tomorrow). Not "is studying good?" but "is one more hour worth it?". That's thinking at the margin.

Worked example · Janice and the potatoes (end-of-chapter Q20)

Step by step

Potatoes cost Janice $1 per pound. She values the 1st pound at $1.50, the 2nd at $1.14, the 3rd at $1.05, and every pound after that at $0.30.

PoundMB (value)MC (price)Buy?
1st$1.50$1.00Yes
2nd$1.14$1.00Yes
3rd$1.05$1.00Yes
4th$0.30$1.00No

With $5: she buys 3 pounds (cost $3). The 4th is worth only $0.30, less than its $1 cost.

With only $2: she buys 2 pounds. She'd like the 3rd (worth $1.05 > $1), but her income has run out. The budget is a second limit on top of MB vs MC.

P5Problem

A student café sells crêpes. MB of each extra crêpe: 1st 7 TND, 2nd 6, 3rd 5, 4th 4, 5th 3. MC: 1st 2 TND, 2nd 3, 3rd 4, 4th 4, 5th 6. How many crêpes should it make? What is the total net benefit at that point?

Show the solution

MB − MC for each crêpe: 1st +5, 2nd +3, 3rd +1, 4th 0, 5th −3.

Make crêpes 1 to 3 (MB > MC). The 4th has MB = MC, so it's the stopping point (making it changes nothing). Don't make the 5th (MC > MB).

08

Positive vs. normative economics

Economic statements come in two kinds. Telling them apart is a skill that appears on almost every quiz.

Positive economicsNormative economics
Question"What is""What should be"
Based onData, evidence, testable factsValue judgements and opinions
Can be checked?Yes, it can be proven true or false with evidenceNo. Reasonable people can disagree.
Think ofA news reporter giving measurable factsA politician arguing for a policy
Examples"The unemployment rate is 8%." "Raising the price of coffee led to fewer people buying it." "Last year, company profits increased by 10%.""The government should raise the minimum wage to help the poor." "Taxes on the wealthy ought to be higher to fund public schools."
Quick test

Ask: "Could I check this with data?" If yes → positive. If it depends on someone's values → normative.

Warning words for normative: should, ought to, must, too (high / hot / much), fair, better, good, bad, harm, needs to be.

Exam alert · two classic traps

1. A false statement can still be positive. "Tunisia's unemployment rate is 50%" is false, but it's positive, because you can check it with data and prove it wrong.

2. Hidden judgements. "A trade deficit of 20 billion dollars will harm the economy" contains a numerical fact, but "harm" is a value judgement, so the whole statement is normative. Likewise "It is too hot to jog today" is normative, while "The temperature is 33°C" is positive.

How economists build theories (textbook Ch 1)

Positive economics uses the scientific method: observe facts, form a hypothesis, test it against data, then accept, reject or modify it. Well-tested generalisations are called theories, principles, laws or models. They are simplifications of reality that describe typical (average) behaviour, not every single person.

DefinitionCeteris paribus / other-things-equal assumption (toutes choses égales par ailleurs)

When economists study how one variable affects another, they assume that all other factors stay constant. Example: "If the price of coffee rises, people buy less coffee", ceteris paribus, meaning income, the price of tea and tastes do not change at the same time.

Pitfalls to sound reasoning (textbook Ch 1)

Fallacy of composition (sophisme de composition): assuming that what is true for one person or part is necessarily true for the whole group. Example: if one spectator stands up at a football match, he sees better; if everyone stands up, nobody sees better.

Post hoc, ergo propter hoc ("after this, therefore because of this"): assuming that because event A happened before event B, A caused B. Example: a new minister took office in January and unemployment fell in March, so the minister caused the fall. Not necessarily.

Correlation is not causation: two variables can move together without one causing the other. A third factor may cause both, or it may be a coincidence.

The budget line and the production possibilities curve

Why this section matters

The teacher's lecture notes stop at positive vs normative, but the end-of-chapter exercise sheet on Moodle (questions 13–17) uses a production possibilities table. This tool is in the textbook's Chapter 1 (p. 5–13). Learn it, because it can appear on the quiz.

The individual: the budget line

An individual's scarce resource is their income. The budget line (droite de budget) shows all the combinations of two goods a person can buy with a given income and given prices.

Example · Rania's monthly entertainment budget

Rania has 120 TND. A cinema ticket costs 20 TND; a paperback book costs 10 TND.

CombinationCinema tickets (20 TND)Books (10 TND)Total spent
A60120
B52120
C44120
D36120
E28120
F110120
G012120
  • Attainable: any combination on or inside the line (e.g. 2 tickets + 3 books = 70 TND).
  • Unattainable: outside the line (e.g. 5 tickets + 5 books = 150 TND > 120).
  • Opportunity cost: 1 cinema ticket = 2 books (20 ÷ 10). 1 book = ½ ticket. Moving along the line, every extra ticket costs 2 books.
  • If her income rises to 160 TND, the line shifts outward (more combinations become attainable).

Society: the production possibilities curve (PPC)

Society's scarce resources are its land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship. The production possibilities curve (frontière des possibilités de production) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce. It rests on four assumptions:

  1. Full employment: all resources are used.
  2. Fixed resources: their quantity and quality don't change.
  3. Fixed technology: no new methods during the analysis.
  4. Two goods: usually a consumer good and a capital good.

Reading the curve: on, inside, outside

PointMeaning
On the curve (A–F)Efficient: full use of resources. To get more of one good, you must give up some of the other.
Inside (U)Inefficient: unemployment or wasted resources. You could have more of both goods.
Outside (W)Unattainable: cannot be produced with current resources and technology. Producing it needs economic growth. (Through trade, a country can still consume beyond its PPC: see below.)

The law of increasing opportunity costs

Move from F toward A, gaining one capital good at a time, and count how many consumer goods you lose each time:

MoveCapital goods gainedConsumer goods given up
F → E1st unit15 − 14 = 1
E → D2nd unit14 − 12 = 2
D → C3rd unit12 − 9 = 3
C → B4th unit9 − 5 = 4
B → A5th unit5 − 0 = 5

Each extra capital good costs more consumer goods than the one before: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. This is the law of increasing opportunity costs. It's why the curve is bowed outward (concave to the origin).

Why? Because resources are not perfectly adaptable to all uses. The first workers moved to building machines are the ones best suited to it. The last ones moved are, say, excellent bakers who are bad at building machines, so you lose a lot of bread for very few machines.

Growth and the future

  • The PPC shifts outward (economic growth) when the economy gets more resources (more workers, new land, more capital) or better technology.
  • Present choices shape future possibilities. An economy that produces more capital goods today (like point C rather than D) is investing in machines and factories. Tomorrow it can produce more, so its PPC shifts out faster. It sacrifices some consumption today for faster growth.
  • Optimal point: society should choose the combination where the MB = MC of each good, the same rule as in section 07.
  • Qualification · international trade. A country can consume a combination outside its own PPC by specialising and trading with other countries. Trade does not shift the PPC (what the country can produce stays the same), but it lets the nation get more of the goods it wants for a smaller sacrifice of other goods.

P6Problem

Using the A–F table: (a) What is the opportunity cost of moving from E to C? (b) What is the opportunity cost of one more consumer good when moving from C to D? (c) Is the point (2 capital, 12 consumer) efficient, inefficient, or unattainable? And (1 capital, 15 consumer)?

Show the solution

(a) E (1, 14) → C (3, 9): gain 2 capital goods, lose 14 − 9 = 5 consumer goods.

(b) C (3, 9) → D (2, 12): gain 3 consumer goods, lose 1 capital good. So 1 consumer good costs 1/3 of a capital good.

(c) (2, 12) is point D, on the curve, so it's efficient. (1, 15): with 1 capital good the maximum is 14 consumer goods (E), so 15 is unattainable (outside).

P7Problem

Rania's income rises from 120 TND to 160 TND (ticket 20 TND, book 10 TND). (a) What is the maximum number of tickets now? Of books? (b) Does the opportunity cost of a ticket change?

Show the solution

(a) Tickets: 160 ÷ 20 = 8. Books: 160 ÷ 10 = 16. The budget line shifts outward.

(b) No. The opportunity cost depends on relative prices: 20 ÷ 10 = still 2 books per ticket. Only a change in relative prices (the ratio of the two prices) changes the slope. If both prices doubled, the slope would stay the same and the line would shift inward.

Recap and key terms

What to remember for Tuesday

  • Economics = how we use limited resources to satisfy unlimited wants. It's the science of choice.
  • Scarcity (wants > resources) forces choices. Every choice has an opportunity cost: the value of the single next best alternative.
  • Micro = households, firms, markets. Macro = GDP, inflation, unemployment, growth, interest and exchange rates.
  • The economic problem: unlimited wants vs scarce resources. Needs vs wants.
  • Factors of production: Land → rent, Labor → wages, Capital → interest, Entrepreneurship → profit (R-W-I-P). Money is not capital.
  • 3 fundamental questions: what, how, for whom (textbook Ch 2 adds 2 more).
  • Goods: free/economic, consumer/capital, durable/non-durable, private (rival, excludable)/public (non-rival, non-excludable). Services are intangible and can't be stored.
  • Marginal analysis: MB > MC → do more. MB < MC → do less. Stop at MB = MC.
  • Positive = testable fact ("is"). Normative = opinion ("should").
  • PPC: on = efficient, inside = unemployment, outside = can't be produced without growth (trade can let a country consume beyond it). It's bowed out because of increasing opportunity costs. More capital goods today → faster growth.
Economics
The study of how individuals, businesses and societies choose to use limited resources to satisfy needs and wants.
Scarcity
Human wants exceed the limited resources available to satisfy them.
Opportunity cost
The value of the next best alternative given up when making a choice.
Microeconomics
The study of individual households, firms and markets.
Macroeconomics
The study of the economy as a whole (GDP, inflation, employment, growth…).
Utility
The satisfaction a consumer gets from consuming goods and services.
Needs / wants
Needs: basic requirements for survival. Wants: desires that improve life but aren't essential.
Factors of production
Land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship, the resources used to produce goods and services.
Capital (economic)
Man-made goods used to produce other goods (machines, tools, buildings). Not money.
Public good
A good that is non-rival and non-excludable (streetlights, national defence).
Service
An intangible activity performed for others that cannot be stored.
Marginal benefit / cost
The extra benefit / extra cost of one additional unit of an activity.
Positive economics
Statements about what is, which can be tested with evidence.
Normative economics
Statements about what should be, based on value judgements.
Ceteris paribus
"Other things equal": the assumption that all factors except the one being studied stay constant.
Fallacy of composition
Wrongly assuming that what is true for one part is true for the whole group.
Post hoc, ergo propter hoc
Wrongly assuming that because A happened before B, A caused B.
Investment (economic)
Spending on the production and accumulation of capital goods. Buying shares is not investment in this sense.
Budget line
All combinations of two goods a consumer can buy with a given income and prices.
Production possibilities curve
The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with full employment of fixed resources and technology.
Law of increasing opportunity costs
As production of one good increases, the opportunity cost of each extra unit rises, because resources are not perfectly adaptable.
Economic growth
An outward shift of the PPC, caused by more resources or better technology.
