01

What a market is (2 minutes)

Welcome. You do not need to know anything before you start. We go in tiny steps.

Read a few lines. Answer a quick question. You see right away if you are right, and why.

How to use this page

Click an answer. If it is wrong, read the reason and press Try again. Do every question: they are the real lesson. When a part is clear, click I understand this part.

Think of the souk on Friday morning. Some people come to buy tomatoes. Some farmers come to sell tomatoes.

That meeting of buyers and sellers is a market.

DefinitionMarket (le marché)

Any place or system that brings buyers and sellers together. It does not need a building: Tayara.tn and Facebook Marketplace are markets too.

Every market has two sides.

The buyers are the demand side. The sellers are the supply side.

Buyers make DEMAND. Sellers make SUPPLY. Together they decide the PRICE.

In this chapter the market has many buyers and many sellers of the same product. One kilo of tomatoes is like another kilo.

So nobody alone can choose the price. If one farmer asks too much, buyers go to the next farmer. Economists call this a competitive market.

One more thing for the graphs. P means price. Q means quantity (how many units).

Memory trick

“P goes uP.” On every graph in this chapter, the price is on the vertical axis (going up) and the quantity is on the horizontal axis.

In French: market = le marché · demand = la demande · supply = l'offre · price = le prix · quantity = la quantité · buyer = l'acheteur · seller = le vendeur.

Quick check

Q1Multiple choice

At the louage station in Bab Alioua, the travellers who want a seat to Sousse are…

Travellers want to buy a seat, so they are buyers. Buyers are the demand side. The louage drivers sell the seats: they are the supply side.
Q2Multiple choice

Which one is a market?

A market is any place or system where buyers and sellers meet. It can be online. In the other cases nothing is bought or sold.
Q3True or false

A market must be a building with shops.

No. A market can be a website, a phone app or a Facebook group. What matters is that buyers and sellers meet.
Q4Multiple choice

On a demand and supply graph, the price goes on…

“P goes uP”: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis. This is a rule for every graph in this chapter.
Q5Multiple choice

In French, “supply” is…

Supply = l'offre (what sellers offer). Demand = la demande (what buyers want).
Q6True or false

In a competitive market, there are many buyers and many sellers of the same product.

Yes. Because there are many of them and the product is the same, no single buyer or seller can control the price.
02

Demand and the law of demand

Demand is about the buyers. It answers one question: at each price, how much do buyers want to buy?

DefinitionDemand (la demande)

A table (schedule) or a curve that shows how much of a product buyers are willing and able to buy at each possible price, during a period of time, other things equal.

“Willing and able” means: you want it and you have the money. Wanting a car with no money is not demand.

“During a period” means: per day, per week or per month.

Here is a small demand table. It shows how many litres of olive oil Fatma's family buys each month.

Price (DT/litre)Litres per month
10 DT6
12 DT5
14 DT4
16 DT3
18 DT2
Worked example · read the table

Step 1. Find the price in the left column. At 14 DT…

Step 2. Read across: the family buys 4 litres.

Step 3. Now the price goes up from 14 DT to 16 DT. The quantity goes down from 4 to 3 litres.

Look at the whole table. When the price goes up, the quantity goes down. When the price goes down, the quantity goes up.

This is the most famous rule in economics.

DefinitionLaw of demand (la loi de la demande)

Other things equal, when the price rises, the quantity demanded falls. When the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions (an inverse, or negative, relationship).

Memory trick

“Demand goes Down.” The demand curve slopes down. Price and quantity demanded are like a see-saw (une balançoire): one goes up, the other goes down.

Why do people buy more when the price is lower? Three simple reasons:

  1. Common sense. A high price is an obstacle. That is why shops make “soldes”: lower prices sell more.
  2. Diminishing marginal utility. Your 1st sandwich gives you a lot of pleasure. Your 3rd sandwich gives less. So you buy more only if it is cheaper.
  3. Income effect and substitution effect. Chicken gets cheaper. Your money now buys more (income effect). And you buy chicken instead of lamb or fish (substitution effect).

In French: utilité marginale décroissante · effet revenu · effet de substitution · quantité demandée.

Q11Multiple choice

“Willing and able to buy” means the buyer…

Both are needed. Wanting something without money to pay for it is not demand.

A market has many buyers. To get the market demand, add what all buyers want at the same price.

Worked example · market demand

A sandwich costs 3 DT. Amine buys 4 per week, Sami buys 2, Ines buys 3.

Market demand at 3 DT = 4 + 2 + 3 = 9 sandwiches. The price stays 3 DT; we only add the quantities. (The textbook calls this “horizontal summation”.)

Each row becomes one dot on a graph. Join the dots: you get the demand curve D.

Worked example · read the graph

Go up the vertical axis to 2. Go right until you touch D. Go down to the horizontal axis: 80 kg. So at 2 DT, buyers want 80 kg per day.

Q16True or false

03

What shifts demand

Until now, only the price changed. But other things change too: your income, fashion, the number of people in town…

When one of these “other things” changes, buyers want more (or less) at every price. Then the whole demand curve moves.

More demand → the whole curve moves RIGHT. Less demand → it moves LEFT.
Memory trick · TRIBE

The 5 things that shift demand spell TRIBE:

Tastes · Related goods' prices · Income · Buyers (how many) · Expectations.

The textbook calls them the determinants of demand.

T · Tastes

People like the product more → demand up. A TV doctor says olive oil is very healthy → demand for olive oil moves right.

A fashion ends → demand down.

R · Related goods

Substitutes are used instead of each other: tea and coffee, Ooredoo and Orange, louage and bus.

If coffee becomes more expensive, people drink more tea. So the demand for tea goes up.

Complements are used together: a phone and a phone cover, coffee and sugar, a car and petrol.

If petrol becomes more expensive, people want fewer big cars. So the demand for cars goes down.

TypeExamplePrice of the OTHER good goes up…
Substitutestea / coffeedemand for this good goes up (same direction)
Complementscar / petroldemand for this good goes down (opposite direction)

Quick check

Q17Multiple choice

In TRIBE, the letter R means…

TRIBE = Tastes, Related goods' prices, Income, Buyers, Expectations. (Resource prices are in the supply list, later.)
Q18Multiple choice

Tea and coffee are…

You drink one instead of the other. Goods used instead of each other are substitutes.
Q19Multiple choice

A phone and a phone cover are…

You use them together. Goods used together are complements (biens complémentaires).
Q20Multiple choice

The price of petrol rises a lot. The demand for big cars…

Cars and petrol are complements. The complement is more expensive → people want fewer big cars → demand moves left.
Q21Multiple choice

The price of Ooredoo internet packs rises. What happens to the demand for Orange internet packs?

Ooredoo and Orange are substitutes. One becomes more expensive → people switch to the other → demand for Orange goes up.
Q22Multiple choice

The price of phones falls a lot. What happens to the demand for phone covers?

Phones and covers are complements. Cheaper phones → more phones bought → more covers wanted → demand for covers moves right.

I · Income

For most goods, more income → more demand. These are normal goods: restaurant meals, new phones, meat.

For some goods, more income → less demand. These are inferior goods: used clothes from the fripe, an old used car, the cheapest brand. With more money, people switch to better things.

Common mistake

“Inferior” does not mean “bad quality”. It only means: when income rises, demand falls.

B · Buyers (number of buyers)

More buyers → more demand. In summer, tourists come to Hammamet and Djerba → demand for hotel rooms and ice cream moves right.

E · Expectations

People think the price will rise soon → they buy now → today's demand goes up.

Example: before Ramadan, families expect dates to get more expensive, so they buy dates early.

Common mistake

The good's own price is NOT in TRIBE. A change in the price of tomatoes does not shift the demand for tomatoes. (Next section explains this.)

Worked example 1 · step by step

Event: the price of coffee rises. What happens to the demand for tea?

Step 1. Which TRIBE letter? R (price of a related good).

Step 2. Tea and coffee are substitutes.

Step 3. Coffee is more expensive → people switch to tea → demand for tea increases → the tea curve moves right.

Worked example 2 · step by step

Event: Sami gets a pay rise. He buys fewer clothes at the fripe.

Step 1. Which letter? I (income).

Step 2. Income up and demand down → used clothes are an inferior good for Sami.

Step 3. His demand for used clothes decreases → the curve moves left.

Exam alert · what increases demand

Demand increases (moves right) when: tastes become more favourable · there are more buyers · income rises (normal good) · income falls (inferior good) · the price of a substitute rises · the price of a complement falls · people expect higher prices or higher income later.

In French: biens substituts (substituables) · biens complémentaires · bien normal · bien inférieur · les goûts · le revenu · les anticipations · déplacement de la courbe de demande.

04

Shift of the curve vs move along it

This is the number 1 trap in exams. There are two different changes, with two different names.

What changes?NameOn the graph
The good's own pricechange in quantity demandeda movement along the same curve (A → B)
A TRIBE thing (tastes, income…)change in demandthe whole curve shifts (D1 → D2)
Own price → move ALONG the curve. Anything else (TRIBE) → SHIFT the curve.
Memory trick

“The price moves you ON the road. TRIBE moves the ROAD.” The curve is the road. A price change makes you walk on it. A TRIBE change builds a new road.

Quick check

Q30Multiple choice

The price of olive oil rises. On the demand graph for olive oil we see…

The own price changed, so we slide along the same curve (to a smaller quantity). The curve itself does not move.
Q31Multiple choice

Incomes rise and olive oil is a normal good. On the demand graph for olive oil we see…

Income is a TRIBE factor (I). Normal good + more income → more demand at every price → shift right.
Q32Multiple choice

Which event causes a change in the quantity demanded of bread?

Only the good's own price causes a movement along its curve (change in quantity demanded). The other three are TRIBE factors: they shift the curve.
Q33Multiple choice

Which event causes a change in demand for phones (a shift)?

Income is a TRIBE factor → shift. The three other answers are all changes in the phone's own price → movement along.
Q34True or false

A movement along a demand curve can only be caused by a change in the good's own price.

Yes. Own price → movement along. Everything else → shift.
Worked example · tomatoes

Case 1. The price of tomatoes falls from 3 DT to 2 DT. Buyers now want 80 kg instead of 60 kg. Only the own price changed → increase in quantity demanded → movement along D (A to B).

Case 2. Ramadan starts. At the same price of 3 DT, buyers now want 100 kg instead of 60 kg. Something other than price changed → increase in demand → the curve shifts right (A to C).

Common mistake · the wording

WRONG: “The price rose, so the demand fell.”

RIGHT: “The price rose, so the quantity demanded fell.”

The same rule works for supply: own price → change in quantity supplied; ROTTEN factors (next sections) → change in supply.

Exam alert

A typical test question: “Which of the following causes a movement along the demand curve for olive oil?” The answer is always: a change in the price of olive oil.

In French: variation de la quantité demandée = mouvement le long de la courbe · variation de la demande = déplacement de la courbe.

05

Supply and the law of supply

Now the sellers. Supply answers: at each price, how much do sellers want to sell?

DefinitionSupply (l'offre)

A table (schedule) or a curve that shows how much of a product producers are willing and able to sell at each possible price, during a period of time, other things equal.

Hamza grows dates near Tozeur. Here is how many kilos he offers each week:

Price (DT/kg)Kilos per week
4 DT100
6 DT200
8 DT300
10 DT400
12 DT500
Worked example · read the table

Step 1. At 8 DT, Hamza offers 300 kg.

Step 2. The price goes up to 10 DT. He now offers 400 kg.

Step 3. Price up → quantity up. Same direction!

DefinitionLaw of supply (la loi de l'offre)

Other things equal, when the price rises, the quantity supplied rises. When the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. Price and quantity supplied move in the same direction (a direct, or positive, relationship).

Memory trick

“Sellers love high prices.” Supply goes up like a staircase: the higher the price, the more they sell. (Demand goes Down, Supply goes up.)

Why? Two simple reasons:

  1. More money. A higher price means more revenue and more profit. So producers want to sell more.
  2. Extra units cost more. To produce more, a farmer needs more water, workers and fertilizer, and each extra kilo costs more (rising marginal cost). He produces those extra kilos only if the price is higher.

Market supply works like market demand: add what all sellers offer at the same price.

Back to the tomato market. Here is its supply:

Price (DT/kg)Supplied (kg)
1 DT20
2 DT40
3 DT60
4 DT80
5 DT100

In French: l'offre · la quantité offerte · la loi de l'offre · le coût marginal · courbe d'offre.

Market supply = add all sellers at the same price: 200 + 150 + 50 = 400 kg.

Q46True or false

Yes, that is the definition of supply (l'offre).
06

What shifts supply

Same idea as demand. The own price → movement along the supply curve.

Other things → the whole supply curve shifts.

Anything that changes the COST of producing shifts supply. Cost down → supply right. Cost up → supply left.
Memory trick · ROTTEN

The 6 things that shift supply spell ROTTEN:

Resource prices · Other goods' prices · Technology · Taxes and subsidies · Expectations · Number of sellers.

Think: when costs go up, supply goes “rotten” (it moves left).

Common mistake · right is not up

An increase in supply = the curve moves RIGHT. Because S goes up, a curve moved right also looks lower.

Never say “supply increased, so the curve went up”. A curve that moved up (left) is a decrease in supply.

The six letters, one by one

  • R · Resource prices. Fertilizer, wages or electricity cost more → producing costs more → supply decreases.
  • O · Other goods' prices. A farmer can grow tomatoes or peppers. Peppers now sell at a high price → he grows more peppers and fewer tomatoes → supply of tomatoes decreases.
  • T · Technology. Drip irrigation gives more tomatoes for the same cost → supply increases.
  • T · Taxes and subsidies. A new tax on producers → supply decreases. A subsidy (money from the state to producers) → supply increases. A subsidy is a tax in reverse.
  • E · Expectations. Olive oil producers expect a higher price next month → they keep oil in stock → supply today decreases. They expect a lower price later → they sell now → supply today increases.
  • N · Number of sellers. Five new phone shops open in Sfax → supply of phones increases.

Q47Multiple choice

In ROTTEN, the letter N means…

ROTTEN: Resource prices, Other goods' prices, Technology, Taxes and subsidies, Expectations, Number of sellers. (Number of buyers is a demand factor.)
Q48Multiple choice

R = resource price. Higher cost → farmers offer less at every price → supply moves left.
Q49Multiple choice

T = technology. Lower cost per kilo → more offered at every price → supply moves right.
Q50Multiple choice

A tax is an extra cost for producers → they offer less at every price → supply moves left.
Q51Multiple choice

O = other goods' prices. Peppers are now more profitable, so he uses his land for peppers → fewer tomatoes at every price.
Q52Multiple choice

N = number of sellers. More sellers → more bread offered at every price → supply moves right.
Worked example 1 · step by step

Event: the price of fertilizer rises. What happens to the supply of tomatoes?

Step 1. Which ROTTEN letter? R (resource price).

Step 2. Fertilizer costs more → producing tomatoes costs more.

Step 3. Supply decreases → the curve moves left (S1 → S3). At 3 DT, farmers now offer only 20 kg instead of 60 kg.

Worked example 2 · step by step

Event: the state gives a subsidy to olive oil producers.

Step 1. Which letter? T (taxes and subsidies).

Step 2. A subsidy lowers the cost for producers.

Step 3. Supply increases → the curve moves right.

Common mistake · wrong list

Income, tastes and number of buyers are demand things (TRIBE). They do not shift supply. And the good's own price does not shift supply either: it moves us along S.

In French: déterminants de l'offre · le coût des facteurs de production · la technologie · une taxe · une subvention · le nombre de vendeurs · déplacement de la courbe d'offre.

07

Equilibrium: where they meet

Now we put buyers and sellers together in one table. Same tomato market:

Price (DT/kg)Demanded (kg)Supplied (kg)
1 DT10020
2 DT8040
3 DT6060
4 DT4080
5 DT20100

Look for the row where the two numbers are equal. At 3 DT, buyers want 60 kg and sellers offer 60 kg.

This is the equilibrium.

DefinitionEquilibrium price (le prix d'équilibre)

The price where quantity demanded = quantity supplied. It is also called the market-clearing price. The quantity at that price is the equilibrium quantity.

Here: equilibrium price = 3 DT, equilibrium quantity = 60 kg.

Memory trick

“Equilibrium = Equal.” In a table: find the row where Qd = Qs. On a graph: find the X where D and S cross.

At this price the market is calm. Everybody who wants to buy at 3 DT can buy. Every seller who wants to sell at 3 DT can sell. Nothing pushes the price up or down.

Worked example 1 · from a table

Seats in a louage from Tunis to Sousse, per day (imagine there is no official fare):

PriceDemandedSupplied
8 DT500180
10 DT400240
12 DT300300
14 DT200360
16 DT100420

Step 1. Go down the rows and compare the two quantities.

Step 2. At 12 DT: 300 = 300. Equal!

Step 3. Equilibrium price = 12 DT, equilibrium quantity = 300 seats.

Quick check

Q58Multiple choice

The equilibrium price is the price where…

Equilibrium = Equal: Qd = Qs. On the graph, it is where D and S cross.
Q59True or false

On a graph, the equilibrium is the point where the demand curve and the supply curve cross.

Yes: the crossing point gives the equilibrium price (read on the vertical axis) and quantity (read on the horizontal axis).
Q60True or false

At the equilibrium price there is always a small shortage.

At equilibrium, Qd = Qs exactly: no shortage and no surplus. That is why the price stays there.
Q61Multiple choice

Use the tomato graph. Where do D and S cross?

Point E: price 3 DT, quantity 60 kg. At this price both Qd and Qs are 60 kg.
Q62Multiple choice

The “market-clearing price” is another name for…

At the equilibrium price everything offered is bought: the market “clears”. No goods are left, no buyer is left waiting.
Q63Multiple choice

Use the louage table. The equilibrium price is…

At 12 DT, seats demanded (300) = seats supplied (300).
Q64Multiple choice

Use the louage table. The equilibrium quantity is…

At the equilibrium price of 12 DT, both quantities are 300 seats.

Sometimes the exam gives equations instead of a table. Don't panic: it is the same idea.

Qd = Qs → solve for P → put P back to find Q
Worked example 2 · from equations

Tomatoes: Qd = 120 − 20P and Qs = 20P.

Step 1. Write Qd = Qs: 120 − 20P = 20P

Step 2. Put the P terms together: 120 = 40P

Step 3. Divide: P = 120 ÷ 40 = 3. So P = 3 DT.

Step 4. Put P = 3 back: Qd = 120 − 20×3 = 60. Check: Qs = 20×3 = 60. Same number, so Q = 60 kg. ✔

Common mistake

Don't stop after finding P. Most questions ask for P and Q. And always check Q with both equations: if you get two different numbers, there is a mistake.

In French: l'équilibre · le prix d'équilibre · la quantité d'équilibre · le marché est équilibré.

P2Problem

(Test-bank style.) The demand equation is P = 10 − 0.2Q and the supply equation is P = 2 + 0.2Q. Find the equilibrium quantity and price.

Show the full solution

Here the equations give P, not Q. Same idea: at equilibrium the buyers' price = the sellers' price.

Step 1. 10 − 0.2Q = 2 + 0.2Q

Step 2. 10 − 2 = 0.2Q + 0.2Q → 8 = 0.4Q

Step 3. Q = 8 ÷ 0.4 = 20

Step 4. P = 10 − 0.2 × 20 = 10 − 4 = 6. Check: 2 + 0.2 × 20 = 6. ✔

Shortage and surplus

What if the price is not at equilibrium? Then the two quantities are different.

DefinitionShortage (la pénurie)

When the price is below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer. Shortage = Qd − Qs. Also called excess demand.

DefinitionSurplus (l'excédent)

When the price is above equilibrium, sellers offer more than buyers want. Surplus = Qs − Qd. Also called excess supply.

In the table, Qd = quantity demanded and Qs = quantity supplied (kg). P ↑ means “the price will go up”; P ↓ means “the price will go down”.

PriceQdQsResult
1 DT10020Shortage 80 → P ↑
2 DT8040Shortage 40 → P ↑
3 DT6060Equilibrium
4 DT4080Surplus 40 → P ↓
5 DT20100Surplus 80 → P ↓

What happens next? With a shortage, buyers queue and compete for the few tomatoes. Sellers see this and raise the price. The price goes up toward 3 DT.

With a surplus, sellers have tomatoes left at the end of the day. They cut the price to sell them. The price goes down toward 3 DT.

Shortages push prices UP. Surpluses push prices DOWN. The market moves back to equilibrium.
Memory trick

LOW price → Lack (shortage: not enough for everyone).
HIGH price → Heap (surplus: a heap of unsold goods).
To calculate: always big number − small number.

Quick check

When the price is below the equilibrium price, there is…

Low price → buyers want a lot, sellers offer little → Qd > Qs → shortage. (LOW → Lack.)

At 1 DT: Qd = 100, Qs = 20. Shortage = 100 − 20 = 80 kg.
Q71Multiple choice

At 5 DT: Qs = 100, Qd = 20. Sellers offer more → surplus = 100 − 20 = 80 kg.
Q72True or false

A surplus pushes the price down.

Yes. Sellers have unsold goods, so they cut the price until the surplus disappears.
Q73True or false

“Shortage” means the quantity supplied is bigger than the quantity demanded.

The opposite. Shortage: Qd > Qs (not enough goods). Surplus: Qs > Qd.
Q74Multiple choice

In French, “shortage” is…

Shortage = pénurie. Surplus = excédent.
Worked example 1 · from the louage table

At 10 DT (below 12 DT): demanded 400, supplied 240. Shortage = 400 − 240 = 160 seats. The price will go up.

At 16 DT (above 12 DT): demanded 100, supplied 420. Surplus = 420 − 100 = 320 seats. The price will go down.

Worked example 2 · from equations

Qd = 50 − 2P, Qs = 10 + 2P (equilibrium P = 10).

At P = 6: Qd = 50 − 12 = 38; Qs = 10 + 12 = 22. 38 > 22 → shortage = 38 − 22 = 16.

At P = 14: Qd = 50 − 28 = 22; Qs = 10 + 28 = 38. 38 > 22 → surplus = 38 − 22 = 16.

Common mistake

The sentence “Surpluses drive prices up; shortages drive them down” is wrong. It is the opposite. A surplus means unsold goods → the price falls.

In French: la pénurie (excès de demande) · l'excédent (excès d'offre) · le prix monte · le prix baisse.

When curves shift: new price and quantity

When a curve shifts, the crossing point moves. We get a new equilibrium: a new price and a new quantity.

Use this 3-step method every time:

  1. Which curve? A TRIBE event → demand. A ROTTEN event → supply.
  2. Which way? Increase → right. Decrease → left.
  3. Read the new crossing: is the price higher or lower? Is the quantity bigger or smaller?
What happensPriceQuantity
Demand increases (right)↑ up↑ up
Demand decreases (left)↓ down↓ down
Supply increases (right)↓ down↑ up
Supply decreases (left)↑ up↓ down
Memory trick

Demand moves P and Q in the SAME direction. (D up → both up.)
Supply moves P and Q in OPPOSITE directions. (S up → P down, Q up.)

Worked example 1 · demand increases (with numbers)

Event: Ramadan. At every price, buyers want 40 kg more tomatoes. New demand: Qd = 160 − 20P. Supply stays Qs = 20P.

Step 1. At the old price 3 DT: Qd = 160 − 60 = 100, Qs = 60 → shortage of 40 → the price must rise.

Step 2. New equilibrium: 160 − 20P = 20P → 160 = 40P → P = 4.

Step 3. Q = 20 × 4 = 80. Result: price 3 → 4 DT (up), quantity 60 → 80 kg (up).

Worked example 2 · supply increases (with numbers)

Event: a very good harvest. At every price, farmers offer 40 kg more. New supply: Qs = 40 + 20P. Demand stays Qd = 120 − 20P.

Step 1. New equilibrium: 120 − 20P = 40 + 20P → 80 = 40P → P = 2.

Step 2. Q = 120 − 40 = 80. Result: price 3 → 2 DT (down), quantity 60 → 80 kg (up).

Common mistake

An increase in supply LOWERS the price. Many students think “more supply → higher price”. No: more tomatoes on the market → sellers must cut the price to sell them all.

Quick check

Q79Multiple choice

Demand increases and supply does not change. The new equilibrium has…

Demand moves P and Q in the same direction: demand up → P up and Q up.
Q80Multiple choice

Supply decreases and demand does not change. The new equilibrium has…

Supply moves P and Q in opposite directions: supply down → P up, Q down. Fewer goods → they get more expensive.
Q81Multiple choice

Supply increases and demand does not change. Then…

Supply right → the crossing point slides down along D: lower price, bigger quantity (like the good harvest example: 3 → 2 DT, 60 → 80 kg).
Q82Multiple choice

Demand decreases and supply does not change. Then…

Demand down → both price and quantity go down (same direction).
Q83Multiple choice

Tomatoes: Qd = 120 − 20P. Supply decreases from Qs = 20P to Qs = −20 + 20P. The new equilibrium price is…

120 − 20P = −20 + 20P → 140 = 40P → P = 3.5 DT (and Q = 120 − 70 = 50 kg). Supply down → price up from 3 DT.
Q84True or false

An increase in supply raises the equilibrium price.

The opposite: more supply → the price falls (and the quantity rises).

Double shifts: both curves move

Sometimes demand and supply change at the same time. Then one result is sure, and the other is indeterminate (we cannot know without numbers).

Method: do each shift alone, then put the arrows together.

SupplyDemandPriceQuantity
↑↓↓ sure?
↓↑↑ sure?
↑↑?↑ sure
↓↓?↓ sure

? = indeterminate: it depends on which shift is bigger.

Memory trick · the arrows

Write the arrows for each shift. Demand up gives P↑ Q↑. Supply up gives P↓ Q↑.

Q: ↑ and ↑ agree → Q surely goes up.
P: ↑ and ↓ fight → P is “?” (it depends on which shift is bigger).

Worked example 3 · why the price is “?”

Start: Qd = 120 − 20P, Qs = 20P (3 DT, 60 kg). Both curves move right, by different amounts. “D +40” means Qd = 160 − 20P; “S +80” means Qs = 80 + 20P, and so on.

ShiftsNew PNew QP vs 3 DT
D +40, S +403100same
D +40, S +802120lower
D +80, S +404120higher

Quantity goes up in every case (60 → 100 or 120). The price can go down, stay the same or go up: it is indeterminate.

Common mistake

Prices and quantities of tomatoes both went up this summer. Does this break the law of demand? No. The demand curve shifted right (more tourists, the wedding season…). The law of demand is about moving along one curve.

In French: nouvel équilibre · hausse / baisse de la demande · hausse / baisse de l'offre · indéterminé.

P6Problem

Tomatoes: Qd = 120 − 20P, Qs = 20P (equilibrium 3 DT, 60 kg). (a) Supply rises to Qs = 40 + 20P: new P and Q? (b) Instead, demand falls to Qd = 80 − 20P: new P and Q? (c) Both changes happen together: new P and Q? What does (c) show?

Show the full solution

(a) 120 − 20P = 40 + 20P → 80 = 40P → P = 2; Q = 120 − 40 = 80. Price ↓, quantity ↑.

(b) 80 − 20P = 20P → 80 = 40P → P = 2; Q = 20 × 2 = 40. Price ↓, quantity ↓.

(c) 80 − 20P = 40 + 20P → 40 = 40P → P = 1; Q = 80 − 20 = 60.

It shows the rule: supply ↑ + demand ↓ → the price surely falls (3 → 1 DT), but the quantity is indeterminate. Here the two effects cancel exactly, so Q stays at 60 kg.

P7Problem

Bad weather destroys many olive trees, so the olive harvest is very poor. (a) What happens in the olive oil market? (b) Sunflower oil is a substitute for olive oil. What happens in the sunflower oil market?

Show the full solution

(a) Fewer olives → less olive oil offered at every price → supply of olive oil decreases (left) → price ↑, quantity ↓.

(b) Olive oil is now more expensive. Sunflower oil is a substitute (R in TRIBE) → people switch → demand for sunflower oil increases (right) → its price ↑ and quantity ↑.

Note: one event can change two markets. In the first market it is a supply change, in the second a demand change.

Price ceilings and floors

Sometimes the government fixes a price by law. There are two kinds.

DefinitionPrice ceiling (le prix plafond)

A maximum legal price. Sellers cannot charge more. Goal: help buyers get essential goods. Examples: rent control; fixed low prices for bread and other subsidized basic products; a maximum price for some vegetables.

DefinitionPrice floor (le prix plancher)

A minimum legal price. Buyers cannot pay less. Goal: help sellers. Examples: a minimum price for wheat paid to farmers; the minimum wage (SMIG), which is a floor on the price of labour.

A ceiling matters only if it is BELOW equilibrium → shortage. A floor matters only if it is ABOVE equilibrium → surplus.
Memory trick

A ceiling is a roof. A high roof does not touch you. A roof only hurts when it is low (below equilibrium) → shortage.

A floor only matters when it is high (above equilibrium): it lifts the price up → surplus.

Worked example 1 · ceilings on tomatoes (equilibrium 3 DT)

Ceiling at 2 DT (below 3 DT): Qd = 80, Qs = 40 → shortage = 80 − 40 = 40 kg. The ceiling is binding (it has an effect).

Ceiling at 4 DT (above 3 DT): sellers may charge up to 4 DT, but the market price is 3 DT anyway. No effect: price 3 DT, quantity 60 kg.

Worked example 2 · floors on tomatoes

Floor at 4 DT (above 3 DT): Qs = 80, Qd = 40 → surplus = 80 − 40 = 40 kg.

Floor at 2 DT (below 3 DT): the price may not go below 2 DT, but it is 3 DT anyway. No effect.

Quick check

Q93Multiple choice

Ceiling = plafond = the highest price allowed by law.

If the maximum is above the market price, nobody is stopped. It only bites when it is below equilibrium.
Q95Multiple choice

At 2 DT: Qd = 80, Qs = 40 → shortage = 80 − 40 = 40 kg.
Q96Multiple choice

Tomatoes: equilibrium 3 DT. The government sets a ceiling of 4 DT. Result?

The ceiling is above equilibrium. The market price of 3 DT is already below the maximum, so nothing changes.
Q97Multiple choice

Tomatoes: the government sets a price floor of 5 DT. Result?

5 DT is above equilibrium. Qs = 100, Qd = 20 → surplus = 100 − 20 = 80 kg.
Q98True or false

A price floor set below the equilibrium price causes a surplus.

A floor below equilibrium has no effect: the market price is already higher. A floor causes a surplus only when it is above equilibrium.

Problems caused by a binding ceiling

  • Queues and rationing: “first come, first served”, or favouritism (the seller keeps goods for friends).
  • Black markets (le marché noir): some goods are sold secretly above the legal price.
  • Less is produced: with rent control, owners stop renting or stop repairing their buildings. In Tunisia, shortages of some subsidized products in shops have happened.

Problems caused by a binding floor

  • A surplus that nobody buys. The government often must buy the surplus and store it (paid by taxpayers), or limit production.
  • Consumers pay more for the product.
  • A minimum wage above equilibrium: more people want jobs than there are jobs (a surplus of labour = unemployment).
Common mistake

A ceiling is a maximum, but to matter it must be below the equilibrium price. Don't think “ceiling = high price”. And don't mix them up: ceiling → shortage; floor → surplus.

In French: prix plafond · prix plancher · le marché noir · le rationnement · le salaire minimum (SMIG) · pénurie · excédent.

What to remember

  • Demand (la demande): price ↑ → quantity demanded ↓. The curve goes down.
  • Supply (l'offre): price ↑ → quantity supplied ↑. The curve goes up.
  • Own price → move along the curve. TRIBE shifts demand; ROTTEN shifts supply. Increase = right, decrease = left.
  • Substitutes: same direction. Complements: opposite direction. Inferior good: income ↑ → demand ↓.
  • Equilibrium (l'équilibre): Qd = Qs. With equations: set Qd = Qs, find P, then Q.
  • Price below equilibrium → shortage (pénurie) → price rises. Price above → surplus (excédent) → price falls.
  • D ↑: P ↑ Q ↑. D ↓: P ↓ Q ↓. S ↑: P ↓ Q ↑. S ↓: P ↑ Q ↓. Two shifts at once: one result is indeterminate.
  • Ceiling (prix plafond) below equilibrium → shortage. Floor (prix plancher) above equilibrium → surplus.
