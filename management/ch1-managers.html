01

What is an organization?

How to use this page

Read the sections in order. After each part, answer the "Check yourself" questions before you move on. Click an answer to see if you are right and why. For open questions, write your own answer on paper first, then open the model answer. Section 07 is a big drill on Mintzberg's roles (the slide exercise is there), and section 08 is a 19-question practice test. Key words have their French term in parentheses the first time, to help you.

Think about your normal week. You come to ISB for classes. Your parents may have an account at a bank like BIAT. You buy a Délice yogurt at the corner shop. On TV you see volunteers of the Tunisian Red Crescent helping families after a flood. ISB, BIAT, Délice Danone and the Red Crescent are very different. But they are all organizations. And every one of them needs managers. That is why this course starts with organizations.

DefinitionOrganization (organisation)

A group of people who work together in an organized way and use resources of various kinds to achieve common goals.

Let us take the definition apart. It has four key ideas:

  1. A group of people. One person alone is not an organization.
  2. Who work together in an organized way. The work is not random. Each person has a job, and the jobs fit together.
  3. Who use resources of various kinds. Money, buildings, machines, information, raw materials.
  4. To achieve common goals. Everybody works toward the same purpose.
Example · ISB as an organization

People: students, teachers, the director, the administrative staff. Organized way: there is a timetable, there are departments and groups (like your group Sigma), and each person knows his or her job. Resources: classrooms, computers, Moodle, a budget. Common goal: to teach business students and give them a recognized degree.

Counter-example · a crowd is not an organization

Fifty people wait for the metro at Place Barcelone in Tunis. They are a group of people in the same place. But they have no common goal (each one goes somewhere different), nobody has a role, and nobody coordinates them. So they are not an organization. Now imagine twenty of them are volunteers who meet there every Saturday to clean the station, with a leader who gives each person a zone. That group is an organization.

Two kinds of organizations: for-profit and non-profit

Organizations exist for different reasons. The slides divide them into two big families, according to their main goal and the way they measure success.

DefinitionFor-profit organization (organisation à but lucratif)

An organization, usually called a firm or business (entreprise), whose main goal is to make a profit. Its success is measured by financial performance: revenues (chiffre d'affaires), profit margins (marges bénéficiaires), shareholder value (valeur pour l'actionnaire) and similar numbers.

DefinitionNon-profit organization (organisation à but non lucratif)

An organization whose main goals are charitable, social or educational, not profit. Its success is measured by the quality of the services it delivers and its impact on the target community (the people it wants to help). Examples from the slides: governments, armies, and NGOs (non-governmental organizations, ONG) such as the Red Cross (humanitarian aid), Greenpeace (environment) and UNICEF (child welfare).

Example · two success reports

At the end of the year, BIAT's managers look at numbers like: "Our revenues grew, our profit margin improved, the bank's shares are worth more." That is a for-profit success report. The managers of the Tunisian Red Crescent ask different questions: "How many families did we help after the floods? How fast did the help arrive? Were the families satisfied with the quality of the help?" That is a non-profit success report: service quality and impact.

Common mistake

"A non-profit organization never has money left over." Not true. A non-profit can receive donations or sell services, and it can end the year with extra money. The difference is that profit is not its goal: the extra money goes back into the mission (more help, more services), not into the pockets of owners.

Three characteristics that every organization shares

Big or small, for-profit or non-profit, every organization has the same three ingredients. The slide shows them as three overlapping circles.

  1. Goals (objectifs). Every organization has a goal or a set of goals. This is its reason to exist. BIAT wants profit and growth. The Red Crescent wants to help people in emergencies.
  2. Structure (structure). Every organization has a structure that defines the roles and responsibilities of each member: who does what, and who reports to whom. You often see the structure drawn as an organization chart (organigramme).
  3. People. Every organization is made of people who make decisions and do the activities. Buildings and machines alone are not an organization.
Example · the three circles in two Tunisian organizations

Délice Danone (for-profit). Goals: produce and sell dairy products and make a profit. Structure: departments such as production, quality, marketing, sales, finance, each with a head who reports to the general management. People: managers, lab technicians, machine operators, truck drivers, sales staff.

The Tunisian Red Crescent (non-profit). Goals: humanitarian help, first aid, support in disasters. Structure: a national headquarters, regional committees and local teams, each with a person in charge. People: paid staff and many volunteers.

Common mistake

"An organization is a company." Too narrow. A ministry, an army, a football club, an NGO and even a well-run student club are organizations too, because they have goals, a structure and people.

Exam alert

"Define an organization and give its three common characteristics" is a classic short question. Write the definition, then Goals, Structure, People, with one sentence each and one example. A second classic: "Compare for-profit and non-profit organizations." Use the two criteria: main goal and how success is measured.

02

Management: effectiveness, efficiency and resources

An organization has goals, a structure and people. But somebody must make all of this work together: decide where to go, share the work, keep people motivated and check that results arrive. This job is called management, and the people who do it are managers.

DefinitionManagement (management, gestion)

The process of achieving organizational goals through the effective and efficient utilization of resources.

Again, take the definition word by word:

  • "The process": management is not one action. It is a continuous series of activities. You will meet them in section 05: planning, organizing, directing and controlling.
  • "Of achieving organizational goals": this is the purpose. A manager is judged on the results of the group, not on personal results.
  • "Effective and efficient": two quality tests. Good management passes both. They are explained just below.
  • "Utilization of resources": the manager works with people, information, materials, equipment and money.
DefinitionManager (manager, gestionnaire)

A person who directs and coordinates the work of other people and the use of resources so that the organization (or one of its units) reaches its goals. A manager gets results through other people.

Effectiveness vs efficiency

These two words look alike, but they mean different things. Your teacher's slide puts them on two axes: effectiveness is about output (did we reach the goal?), efficiency is about input (how much did we use, how much did we waste?).

DefinitionEffectiveness (efficacité)

Attaining goals: making good decisions and carrying them out successfully. It relates to outputs and the results achieved. In short: "doing the right things."

DefinitionEfficiency (efficience)

Using resources wisely, with low waste, to achieve goals. It relates to inputs and resource usage. In short: "doing things right."

Watch out · false friend

For a French speaker this is the most dangerous trap of the chapter. Effectiveness = efficacité (you reach the goal). Efficiency = efficience (you do not waste resources). Many students translate "efficacité" as "efficiency". That is wrong, and on an exam it changes the meaning of your whole answer.

Memory trick

Efficiency and efficience share the letters "ci": they go together. Effectiveness is about the effect, the result: did you hit the target? Efficiency is about the cost of hitting it.

Worked example · three delivery teams

A delivery company in Tunis gives three teams the same goal: deliver 500 parcels today.

Team A is effective (500 delivered) but not very efficient (10 vans, only 50 parcels per van).

Team B is effective and efficient: same result with only 6 vans (500 ÷ 6 ≈ 83.3 parcels per van). This is good management.

Team C used few vans and has a good ratio (300 ÷ 4 = 75 parcels per van), but 200 customers did not get their parcel. It saved resources and missed the goal: it is not effective. Saving resources is useless if the job is not done.

The effectiveness × efficiency matrix

If you combine the two tests, you get four possible situations. Good management is in the top-right box: high goal attainment with low waste.

The four mini-cases in detail

1. Effective and efficient · a bakery in Sfax. Every evening the owner looks at the day's sales and plans tomorrow's production. She bakes 1,000 baguettes and sells 980: customers are served (goal reached) and only 20 baguettes, 2%, are left (little waste). Right things, done right.

2. Effective but inefficient · an ISB club conference. The club wanted 300 students at its conference, and 300 came. Effective. But the club printed 1,000 flyers and ordered food for 600 people, so half of the food (300 of 600 portions, 50%) was thrown away. The goal was reached at a far higher cost than necessary.

3. Efficient but not effective · a textile factory in Monastir. The factory makes 5,000 winter jackets with the lowest cost per jacket in its sector and almost no wasted fabric. Very efficient. But buyers wanted light summer jackets, and only 400 jackets sold (400 ÷ 5,000 = 8%). The factory did things right, but it did not do the right thing.

4. Neither · a restaurant in La Marsa. The manager buys too much fish and 45% of it is thrown away (waste). The kitchen is so slow that customers leave, and the month ends at 26,000 TND of sales instead of the 40,000 TND target: only 65% of the goal. Wrong on both tests.

Effectiveness asks "Did we reach the goal?" Efficiency asks "How much did it cost us to get there?" Good management says yes to the first and "very little" to the second.

Which matters more?

Your teacher's view is clear: effectiveness is more important than efficiency. First you must be doing the right work. Only then does it matter whether you do it cheaply. Look at case 3: the Monastir factory was a champion of efficiency, and it still lost money, because it made the wrong product. The management writer Peter Drucker made the same point: nothing is more useless than doing very efficiently something that should not be done at all.

Exam alert

Classic question: "Distinguish between effectiveness and efficiency. Which is more important? Give an example." Your answer: effectiveness = attaining goals (output, "doing the right things"); efficiency = using resources with low waste (input, "doing things right"); effectiveness comes first; then one example like the Monastir factory.

The slide also asks a discussion question: "What are the most important resources?" There is no single official answer, but two resources are usually argued to be the most important:

  • People (human resources), because people are the ones who use every other resource. A modern machine is useless without a trained operator; money is wasted without good decisions. People also create new ideas, and competitors cannot copy a motivated team easily.
  • Information, because every decision depends on it. A manager who does not know what customers want, what competitors do or what the new rules are will make bad decisions, even with a lot of money.

03

Levels of management: who manages what?

In a small café, the owner does everything: buys the coffee, serves customers, pays the bills. In a big organization like BIAT or Poulina Group, with thousands of employees, one person cannot manage everything. So the managerial work is divided into levels.

DefinitionManagement levels (niveaux hiérarchiques)

The lines of separation between managerial positions in an organization. They are created so that activities are managed smoothly. Traditional organizations have three levels of managers (top, middle, first-line), which differ in their duties, responsibilities and authority. Below them are the operatives, who are not managers.

Two words in this definition need a clear meaning:

DefinitionAuthority (autorité) and responsibility (responsabilité)

Authority is the right to make decisions, give instructions and use the organization's resources. Responsibility is the duty to carry out tasks and to answer for the results. The higher a manager is in the hierarchy, the more authority and the more responsibility he or she has.

Why a pyramid? Because the number of managers is small at the top and grows at each lower level. A company has one CEO, a few vice presidents, more department heads, many supervisors and a large number of operatives.

The four layers, one by one

Level 1Top managers (cadres dirigeants, direction générale)

Also called executives. They are responsible for the organization as a whole. They make the important decisions and set the strategies (stratégies) and long-term goals. Typical titles: CEO (chief executive officer, PDG or directeur général), chairman (président du conseil), president, vice president. Their decisions affect everybody, often for years.

Level 2Middle managers (cadres intermédiaires)

They report to top managers and are in charge of relatively large divisions or departments. Their key job is to translate the general goals of top management into specific details that lower-level managers can carry out. Typical titles: sales manager, branch manager (chef d'agence), department head (chef de département).

Level 3First-line managers (managers de premier niveau, agents de maîtrise)

They supervise the daily activities of small work units. They are the managers closest to the people who do the work. Typical titles: group leader, unit leader, supervisor (superviseur, chef d'équipe), office manager (chef de bureau).

The workforceOperatives (personnel d'exécution)

Employees who have no responsibility for overseeing others. They do the work directly. Typical jobs: cashiers, specialists, technicians. They are not managers.

Worked example · how one goal travels down the BIAT pyramid

Top management decides: "In 2027, we want many more customers to use our mobile banking app." This is a strategy for the whole bank.

A middle manager (the branch manager in Sfax) translates it into specific details for her branch: "My branch will open 300 new mobile-banking accounts in 2027. That is 300 ÷ 12 = 25 per month. With 5 customer advisers, each adviser needs 25 ÷ 5 = 5 new accounts per month."

A first-line manager (the team leader) organizes the daily work: "This morning, Amel and Youssef will help customers install the app at the tablet desk. I will check the numbers every Friday."

Operatives (the advisers) do the work: they explain the app to customers and open the accounts.

Common mistake

"A specialist with a high salary is a manager." Not necessarily. The test is simple: does this person oversee the work of other people? A senior IT engineer or an experienced accountant who supervises nobody is an operative (a specialist), whatever the salary. Also, do not forget that operatives are not a management level: there are three managerial levels, and the operatives are below them.

Memory trick

Top thinks about the whole. Middle translates. First-line supervises every day. Operatives do.

Use the slide's titles: CEO / chairman / president / vice president; sales manager / branch manager / department head; group leader / unit leader / supervisor / office manager; and for operatives: cashiers, specialists, technicians.

04

Managerial skills: Katz's technical, human and conceptual skills

We know who managers are. Now: what must a manager be able to do? In a famous article in the Harvard Business Review (1955), Robert Katz said that every manager needs three essential skills (compétences). The mix changes with the level, but all three are always needed.

Skill 1Technical skills (compétences techniques)

The ability to use a special talent or expertise to perform particular tasks. It is hands-on knowledge of the work itself: machines, processes, methods, tools, procedures. Example from the slide: the supervisor of a team of mechanics must be able to teach them how to use the tools.

Skill 2Human skills (compétences humaines, relationnelles)

The ability to work with and through people. It means understanding and empathizing with employees' feelings; predicting, explaining and controlling their behavior (in the sense of guiding it); helping solve their problems; and being accepted by them. Some people are born with these skills; others improve them through experience, training and practice.

Skill 3Conceptual skills (compétences conceptuelles)

The ability to analyze and interpret information, diagnose complex situations and predict how things fit together. It is critical thinking: seeing the organization as a whole, breaking a problem into parts and seeing the hidden relationships between them.

Memory trick · Hands, Heart, Head

Hands = technical (doing the work). Heart = human (understanding people). Head = conceptual (thinking about the whole). As you climb the pyramid, you use your hands less and your head more, but you always need your heart.

Which level needs which skill most? (the slide's question)

The slide asks: "Are all three skills equally important at every level?" The answer has four parts. Learn them in this order:

  1. All three skills are essential for every manager, but their relative importance varies with the level.
  2. Technical skills matter most for first-line managers, because they supervise the employees' work directly. They must understand it, teach it and check it.
  3. Human skills are equally important at all levels, because managerial work is interpersonal at every level: every manager gets results through people.
  4. Conceptual skills are especially important for top managers, because they make long-range plans for the organization as a whole. Middle managers need a balance: human skills plus growing conceptual skills, and still some technical skills.
Example · Sami's career at Tunisie Télécom

Year 1, operative: Sami is a network technician. His job is almost 100% technical: he installs and repairs fibre connections.

Year 5, first-line manager: he becomes leader of a team of eight technicians. He still needs strong technical skills (to train them and check their work), but now he also needs human skills: he plans their days, settles conflicts, motivates them.

Year 12, middle manager: he heads the network department of a whole region. He rarely touches a cable. He uses human skills every day (with his team leaders) and more and more conceptual skills: he must see how the region's network, budget and customers fit together.

Year 20, top manager: if he becomes a director, conceptual skills dominate: he decides on strategy for the whole company. His human skills are still as important as before.

Common mistakes

(1) "Human skills are mostly needed at the top" or "mostly at the bottom". Both are wrong: human skills are equally important at all levels. (2) "Top managers do not need technical skills at all." Too strong: they need less, but they still must understand their industry. (3) "The best technician automatically makes the best manager." No: when a good technician is promoted, he or she must develop human and conceptual skills.

Exam alert

"Which managerial skill is most important for top managers? For first-line managers? Explain." Answer: conceptual for top (long-range plans, the whole organization); technical for first-line (they supervise the employees' work); and add that human skills are equally important at all levels.

P5Exam-style question

"All managers need technical, human and conceptual skills, but not in the same proportions." Explain this statement for each level of management (6–8 sentences).

Show a model answer

According to Robert Katz, every manager needs three skills: technical (expertise to perform specific tasks), human (working with and through people) and conceptual (analyzing complex situations and seeing the organization as a whole). All three are essential, but their relative importance depends on the level.

First-line managers need technical skills most, because they supervise the employees' work directly and must be able to teach and check it.

Middle managers need a balance: strong human skills, some technical skills, and growing conceptual skills, because they translate top-level goals into plans for their departments.

Top managers need conceptual skills most, because they make long-range plans for the whole organization.

Human skills are equally important at all levels, because all managerial work is interpersonal.

So as a manager moves up, technical skills become relatively less important and conceptual skills more important, while human skills stay constant.

The four functions of management: planning, organizing, directing, controlling

The definition of management said it is a process. This process has four parts, called the functions of management (fonctions du management). The idea comes from the French engineer Henri Fayol, whom you will meet in Chapter 2. Today we use four functions, easy to remember with the letters POLC: Planning, Organizing, Leading (also called directing), Controlling.

To make the functions concrete, we follow two managers through the whole section:

  • Ines, president of the ISB Business Club, organizes the ISB Career Day on Wednesday 2 December 2026, with 15 companies and 300 students expected, and a budget of 2,000 TND.
  • Karim, a regional manager at Délice Danone, must open a new distribution centre in Sfax that will deliver to shops in the south of Tunisia. (This is an imagined case, to help you picture the functions in a firm.)

1. Planning

Function 1Planning (planification)

Determining in advance what should be accomplished, when, by whom, how, and at what cost. In other words: setting goals and deciding how best to achieve them.

Planning happens at every scale: from a five-year strategy for the whole company to a two-hour meeting. Your teacher's slide calls planning "the major contributor to success". Why? Because every other function depends on it: you organize to carry out the plan, you lead people toward the plan's goals, and you control by comparing results with the plan. No plan, no direction.

Running example · planning

Ines: What? A career day with 15 companies and 300 students. When? Wednesday 2 December, 9:00–16:00. By whom? The club's 20 members. How? Invite companies from October, book the main hall, advertise on Instagram. At what cost? 2,000 TND at most.

Karim: What? A centre that serves 400 shops. When? Open on 1 March 2027. How? Rent a warehouse in the Sfax industrial zone, buy refrigerated trucks, hire staff. At what cost? A budget approved by the head office. By whom? A warehouse supervisor, a transport supervisor and their teams.

2. Organizing

Function 2Organizing (organisation)

Arranging tasks, people and other resources to accomplish the work according to the plan. Its two important elements are assigning work (répartir le travail) and granting authority (déléguer l'autorité).

Running example · organizing

Ines splits the club into four teams: companies (contact the 15 firms), logistics (hall, chairs, coffee), communication (posters, Instagram) and welcome (badges, guiding visitors). She names a leader for each team: that is assigning work. She allows each team leader to spend up to 300 TND without asking her: that is granting authority. (4 × 300 = 1,200 TND, so 800 TND of the 2,000 TND budget stay under her direct control.)

Karim designs the centre's structure: a warehouse supervisor with stock clerks, a transport supervisor with 12 drivers. He hires the staff, buys forklifts, and gives the warehouse supervisor the authority to hire temporary workers in busy weeks.

3. Directing (leading)

Function 3Directing, or leading (direction, diriger)

Motivating, communicating, guiding and encouraging employees; coaching them, assisting them and solving problems with them. This function focuses on people more than on tasks.

Running example · directing

Ines holds a short meeting every Monday, thanks volunteers in front of the club, helps the communication team when they are stuck on the poster design, and settles a disagreement between two members about who presents the opening speech.

Karim explains to the drivers why fresh yogurt must arrive on time, trains new stock clerks, and listens to their problems with the night shifts.

4. Controlling

Function 4Controlling (contrôle)

Continuously checking results against goals and taking corrective action (action corrective) when needed, so that the plans stay on track.

A simple way to see controlling is a loop: (1) take the goal from the plan, (2) measure what really happened, (3) compare, (4) correct if there is a gap.

Running example · controlling

Ines: two weeks before the event, only 9 companies have confirmed, against a goal of 15. That is 9 ÷ 15 = 60% of the goal. Corrective action: she asks ISB alumni who work in companies to help, and extends the registration deadline by one week.

Karim: his goal is 95% of deliveries on time. In the first month, only 88% arrive on time. He finds that one truck route is too long, so he splits it into two shorter routes.

The functions are not a straight line

The numbers 1-2-3-4 suggest a fixed order. Be careful: the functions are not linear steps that a manager does once, one after the other. Managers perform them simultaneously and keep going back and forth.

Example · one afternoon in Ines's week

At 14:00, Ines checks the confirmations: only 9 companies of 15 (controlling). At 14:20 she decides to contact alumni and to extend the deadline (planning again). At 14:40 she asks two members to call the alumni and gives them the list (organizing). At 15:00 she encourages them, because they are tired after exams (directing). Four functions in one hour, and not in the textbook order.

How much time on each function? It depends on the level

All managers perform all four functions. A CEO controls, and a supervisor plans. But the time spent on each function depends on the level (Exhibit 1-6 on your slides):

  • Top managers spend relatively more time on planning and organizing: long-term strategy and the structure of the whole organization.
  • Lower-level managers implement policies at the department level and play the supervisory role: they are in direct contact with employees and directly guide and control day-to-day performance. Your teacher's slide stresses that they are more concerned with the control function (and directing) than with planning and organizing.
FunctionTop manager (e.g. Délice's CEO)First-line manager (e.g. a shift supervisor)
PlanningA lot of time: 3- to 5-year strategy, new products, new marketsLess time: short plans, like next week's shift schedule
OrganizingA lot of time: structure of the whole company, which divisions, who heads themLess time: who works on which machine today

Common mistakes

(1) Organizing (the function: arranging tasks and people) is not the same as an organization (the group of people). (2) Controlling is not spying on or punishing people: it is comparing results with goals and correcting the course. (3) "Only top managers plan." Wrong: every manager plans; top managers just spend more of their time on it. (4) Do not confuse the directing (leading) function with Mintzberg's leader role in the next section. They are related, but one is a function in POLC and the other is one of ten roles.

Exam alert

Typical questions: "List and define the four functions of management." "Are the functions performed in a fixed order?" (No: simultaneously, not linear.) "Do all managers perform all four functions?" (Yes, but the time on each depends on the level: top → more planning and organizing; lower levels → more directing and controlling, per your slide.)

Mintzberg's ten managerial roles

The four functions describe what managers should do. In the late 1960s, the Canadian professor Henry Mintzberg asked a different question: what do managers actually do all day? He closely observed five chief executives at work and published the results in The Nature of Managerial Work (1973). His answer: managers play ten roles, grouped into three categories.

DefinitionRole (rôle)

A set of expectations of how a manager will behave in a given situation. Example: when a factory machine breaks down, everybody expects the manager to react and find a solution. That expected behavior is a role.

Memory trick · 3 + 3 + 4

Categories: "People, Information, Decisions" (interpersonal, informational, decisional). Interpersonal: "Friendly Leaders Liaise" = Figurehead, Leader, Liaison. Informational: "My Dog Speaks" = Monitor, Disseminator, Spokesperson (collect → share inside → speak outside). Decisional: "Every Day Requires Negotiation" = Entrepreneur, Disturbance handler, Resource allocator, Negotiator.

A. Interpersonal roles (rôles interpersonnels)

These roles come from the manager's formal authority and status. They are about dealing with people, inside and outside the organization.

RoleWhat the manager doesExample
Figurehead (symbole)Represents the organization legally and socially in formal matters. Ceremonial and symbolic duties: signing documents, greeting official visitors, cutting ribbons.Tunisie Télécom's CEO cuts the ribbon at a new shop in Sfax. ISB's director hands out diplomas at graduation.
Leader (leader)Communicates with, trains, motivates and disciplines employees.A BIAT branch manager trains a new adviser, praises the team, and warns an employee who is always late.
Liaison (agent de liaison)Builds and maintains relationships with individuals and groups inside and outside the immediate work group (slide wording): colleagues, other departments, partners, suppliers, officials. The focus is the network of contacts; directing your own team's work is the leader role.Délice's purchasing manager keeps good contacts with dairy farmers' cooperatives and with the company's logistics department.

B. Informational roles (rôles informationnels)

The manager is the centre of a network of information: he or she collects it, shares it inside, and communicates it outside.

RoleWhat the manager doesExample
Monitor (observateur actif)Collects information (scanning the media, talking with experts…) to predict changes in customer tastes, competitors' strategies and new regulations.A Poulina marketing manager reads market reports and visits a food fair to see competitors' new products.
Disseminator (diffuseur)Transmits information to members of the organization through memos, emails, reports, phone calls.A supervisor emails the team the new safety rules.
Spokesperson (porte-parole)Transmits information to outsiders about the organization's plans, policies, actions and results, through reports, speeches, press conferences.After floods, the president of the Tunisian Red Crescent explains on the radio how many families were helped.

C. Decisional roles (rôles décisionnels)

Here the manager uses the information to make choices and act.

RoleWhat the manager doesExample
Entrepreneur (entrepreneur)Initiates change: starts new, innovative projects, identifies new ideas and opportunities.The owner of a Sfax olive-oil mill launches organic oil in small glass bottles for export.
Disturbance handler (régulateur)Deals with unexpected problems and crises that disrupt operations: a strike, a machine breakdown, a supplier that fails to deliver.A power cut stops a yogurt line; the plant manager brings in generators and reschedules deliveries.
Resource allocator (répartiteur de ressources)Decides where, when and how people and projects get resources; manages schedules and budgets; sets priorities.Ines divides the 2,000 TND Career Day budget between the hall, coffee and posters.
Negotiator (négociateur)Represents the organization in negotiating contracts and agreements with unions, customers and suppliers.Poulina's purchasing director bargains with a grain supplier over the price and delivery dates.

The pairs that students confuse

If you hesitate between……ask yourself
Disseminator / SpokespersonIs the information going to people inside (disseminator) or outside (spokesperson: media, shareholders, the public)?
Monitor / DisseminatorIs the manager collecting information (monitor) or passing it on (disseminator)?
Leader / LiaisonIs the manager directing or motivating the team's work (leader), or building and keeping relationships, especially with other departments, partners and outsiders (liaison)?
Figurehead / SpokespersonIs it a symbolic or legal act, like a ceremony or a signature (figurehead), or giving information to outsiders (spokesperson)?
Entrepreneur / Disturbance handlerIs the change planned and voluntary, an opportunity (entrepreneur), or a reaction to an unexpected problem (disturbance handler)?
Resource allocator / NegotiatorIs the manager deciding internally who gets what (resource allocator), or bargaining with another party (negotiator)?
Roles overlap

In real life, one action often involves several roles at once. Example: a manager hears many customer complaints about a new yogurt (monitor), tells the product team in a meeting (disseminator, and leader when guiding them), and then launches a new recipe (entrepreneur). That is why your teacher sometimes accepts two roles. In a multiple-choice question, pick the role that matches the main action; in an open question, name the main role and mention the second one.

Which role is it? Identification drills

Identifying the role in a short situation is the most common Mintzberg question. It becomes easy with a method and a lot of practice. This section starts with the exact exercise from your slides, then gives you eleven more situations from Tunisian organizations.

A 3-step method

Step 1 · Category. Is the manager mainly dealing with people, handling information, or making a decision? Step 2 · Direction. Inside or outside? Own team or other groups? Collecting or sharing? Planned or unexpected? Deciding alone or bargaining with another party? Step 3 · Role. Pick the role. If two roles fit, choose the one that matches the main action.

The slide exercise · "Which roles?"

These are the seven situations from your teacher's slide. The answers follow the slide. Where the teacher also accepts a second role, the explanation tells you.

Q45Which role? · slide situation 1

Holding a team meeting to communicate new company policies.

The information (new policies) goes to people inside the organization (the team), so the manager is a disseminator. If the audience were journalists or shareholders, it would be spokesperson. The teacher also accepts leader, because talking to and guiding your own team is part of leading.
Q46Which role? · slide situation 2

Presenting quarterly performance to shareholders.

Shareholders own the company but do not work in it: for this role they count as outsiders. Giving outsiders information about results (reports, speeches, press conferences) is the spokesperson role. It is not figurehead, because the point is the information, not a ceremony.
Q47Which role? · slide situation 3

Assigning team members to tasks based on their skills and availability.

People's time and skills are resources. Deciding who works on what is resource allocator. The leader role is also involved (guiding the team). Notice the link with POLC: as a function, assigning work is part of organizing. Functions and roles are two ways to describe the same work.

Deciding how the budget is shared and setting priorities between departments is the purest example of resource allocator.

Analyzing, diagnosing complex situations and seeing how the parts fit together is the definition of conceptual skill. "Figurehead" is a Mintzberg role, not one of Katz's skills.
T8Multiple choice

When a manager is promoted from first-line to top management, which change is correct according to Katz?

Going up the pyramid, technical skills matter less, conceptual skills matter more, and human skills stay equally important. (d) is too extreme: all three skills remain essential.
T9Multiple choice

Which function does the slide call "the major contributor to success"?

Planning: it sets the goals and the way to reach them, and all the other functions depend on it (you organize, lead and control according to the plan).
T10True or false

"Controlling means continuously checking results against goals and taking corrective action so that plans stay on track."

That is the slide's definition. Note the word continuously: controlling is not only done at the end of a project.
T11True or false

"Middle managers are mainly responsible for setting the long-term strategy of the whole organization."

Setting strategy for the whole organization is the job of top managers. Middle managers translate those goals into specific details for their divisions.
T12Multiple choice

Which function focuses on people more than on tasks?

Directing means motivating, communicating, guiding, encouraging and coaching: its focus is on people.
T13Multiple choice

A manager learns that her main packaging supplier has gone bankrupt and stocks will run out in three days. She immediately looks for another supplier to keep the line running. Her main role is:

A supplier failure is one of the slide's examples of an unexpected problem that disrupts operations: disturbance handler. Bargaining with the new supplier afterward would add the negotiator role, but the main action is handling the crisis.
T14Multiple choice

Which list contains only DECISIONAL roles?

The four decisional roles are entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator and negotiator. (b) is all interpersonal; (c) and (d) mix in informational roles (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson).
T15Multiple choice

Plant A makes 1,000 units a day with 50 workers. Plant B makes 1,000 units a day with 40 workers. Both had a goal of 1,000 units a day. Which statement is correct?

Both reach the goal of 1,000 units, so both are effective. Plant B uses fewer inputs for the same output: 1,000 ÷ 40 = 25 units per worker, against 1,000 ÷ 50 = 20 for plant A. So plant B is more efficient.
T16Multiple choice

A CEO meets journalists at a press conference to explain why the company is closing one of its factories. Which role is the CEO playing?

Explaining the organization's decisions to outsiders (journalists, the public) through a press conference is spokesperson. It is not figurehead, because the purpose is to give information, not to perform a ceremony.
T17Open question

Distinguish between effectiveness and efficiency. Give an example of an organization that is efficient but not effective. According to your course, which of the two is more important, and why?

Show the model answer

Effectiveness (efficacité) is attaining goals: making good decisions and carrying them out successfully. It is about outputs and results ("doing the right things"). Efficiency (efficience) is using resources wisely, with little waste, to reach goals. It is about inputs ("doing things right").

Example: a factory produces 5,000 winter jackets at the lowest cost in its sector, with almost no waste, but customers want summer jackets and only 8% of the jackets are sold. The factory is efficient (low waste) but not effective (it misses the real goal of selling products customers want).

More important: effectiveness. A manager must first make sure the organization is doing the right work; doing the wrong thing cheaply is useless. Good management, however, aims for both: high goal attainment with low waste.

T18Case study

Mr Hatem is plant director of a pasta factory in Sfax. He reports to the company's CEO and manages three shift supervisors, who each supervise about twenty operators. Last week: (1) he divided the new packaging work between the three shifts and allowed each supervisor to call in two temporary workers; (2) he compared the week's output (180 tonnes) with the target (200 tonnes) and found that one machine stopped too often; (3) he signed a maintenance contract after discussing the price with the machine maker; (4) he welcomed a delegation from head office and cut the ribbon of the new packaging line.
(a) What is Mr Hatem's management level? (b) Which Katz skill should be relatively more important for him than for his supervisors? (c) Which function is shown in action (1), and which in action (2)? (d) Which roles are shown in action (3)?

Show the model answer

(a) Middle manager. He reports to top management (the CEO) and manages other managers (the shift supervisors, who are first-line managers). He runs a relatively large unit, the plant.

(b) Conceptual skills. As a middle manager, he needs more conceptual skill than his first-line supervisors: he must see how the whole plant (machines, shifts, maintenance, targets) fits together. His supervisors need relatively more technical skill. Human skills are equally important for both.

(c) Action (1) is organizing: assigning work (dividing the packaging work) and granting authority (the right to call in temporary workers). Action (2) is controlling: output was 180 ÷ 200 = 90% of the target, and he looked for the cause in order to correct it.

(d) Discussing the price with the machine maker is negotiator; signing the contract on behalf of the organization is figurehead (formal, legal representation). Both roles overlap in one action.

Bonus, action (4): welcoming visitors and cutting a ribbon is a ceremony, so it is the figurehead role again.

T19Open question

"All managers perform the four functions of management, but not in the same way." Explain this sentence. Include (a) why the functions are not linear and (b) how the time spent on each function differs between top managers and lower-level managers.

Show the model answer

The four functions are planning (setting goals and deciding how to reach them), organizing (assigning work and granting authority), directing (motivating, communicating and guiding people) and controlling (checking results against goals and correcting). Every manager, at every level, performs all four.

(a) Not linear: the functions are not steps done once in a fixed order. Managers perform them simultaneously and move back and forth. For example, when controlling shows that a target will be missed, the manager plans a correction, reorganizes tasks and motivates the team, all in the same day.

(b) Time by level: top managers spend relatively more time on planning and organizing, because they decide the strategy and the structure of the whole organization. Lower-level managers implement policies at the department level and play the supervisory role, in direct contact with employees; they are more concerned with directing and controlling day-to-day performance than with planning and organizing.

09

Recap and key terms

What to remember from Chapter 1

  • An organization is a group of people working together in an organized way, using resources to achieve common goals. Every organization has goals, a structure and people.
  • For-profit organizations (firms) aim at profit and measure success financially (revenues, margins, shareholder value). Non-profit organizations (governments, armies, NGOs like the Red Cross, Greenpeace, UNICEF) have charitable, social or educational goals and measure success by service quality and impact on the community.
  • Management = the process of achieving organizational goals through the effective and efficient use of resources (human beings, information, materials, equipment, money).
  • Effectiveness (efficacité) = attaining goals, outputs, "doing the right things". Efficiency (efficience) = low waste of resources, inputs, "doing things right". Good management = both. Your teacher: effectiveness comes first.
  • Levels: top managers (whole organization, strategy: CEO, chairman, president, VP) → middle managers (translate goals: sales manager, branch manager, department head) → first-line managers (daily supervision: supervisor, group leader, office manager) → operatives (no one to oversee: cashiers, specialists, technicians). Authority and responsibility grow toward the top; the number of people grows toward the bottom.
  • Katz's skills: technical (expertise), human (working with and through people), conceptual (seeing the whole). Technical matters most for first-line managers, conceptual for top managers, human equally at all levels.
  • POLC: planning (what, when, who, how, at what cost; the major contributor to success), organizing (assigning work + granting authority), directing/leading (motivating, communicating, guiding; focus on people), controlling (checking results against goals + corrective action). Not linear; all managers do all four. Top: more planning and organizing; lower levels: more directing and controlling.
  • Mintzberg's 10 roles: interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator). Roles overlap.

Glossary

Organization
A group of people who work together in an organized way and use resources of various kinds to achieve common goals (organisation).
For-profit organization
A firm whose main goal is profit; success measured by financial performance (organisation à but lucratif).
Non-profit organization
An organization with charitable, social or educational goals; success measured by service quality and impact on the community (organisation à but non lucratif).
Goals
What the organization wants to achieve; its reason to exist (objectifs).
Structure
The arrangement that defines the roles and responsibilities of each member: who does what, who reports to whom (structure; drawn as an organigramme).
Management
The process of achieving organizational goals through the effective and efficient utilization of resources (management, gestion).
Manager
A person who directs and coordinates the work of others and the use of resources to reach the organization's goals (manager, gestionnaire).
Effectiveness
Attaining goals; about outputs and results; "doing the right things" (efficacité).
Efficiency
Using resources wisely with low waste; about inputs; "doing things right" (efficience).
Resources
Anything a manager uses to create value: human beings, information, materials, equipment, money (ressources).
Management levels
Lines of separation between managerial positions; three in traditional organizations: top, middle, first-line (niveaux hiérarchiques).
Authority
The right to make decisions, give instructions and use resources (autorité).
Responsibility
The duty to carry out tasks and answer for the results (responsabilité).
Top managers
Executives responsible for the whole organization; set strategies. CEO, chairman, president, vice president (cadres dirigeants).
Middle managers
Report to top managers, run large divisions, translate goals into specific details. Sales manager, branch manager, department head (cadres intermédiaires).
First-line managers
Supervise the daily activities of small work units. Group leader, unit leader, supervisor, office manager (managers de premier niveau, agents de maîtrise).
Operatives
Employees with no responsibility for overseeing others. Cashiers, specialists, technicians (personnel d'exécution).
Technical skills
Ability to use special expertise to perform particular tasks (compétences techniques).
Human skills
Ability to work with and through people: understand, motivate, solve their problems, be accepted (compétences humaines).
Conceptual skills
Ability to analyze complex situations and see the organization as a whole (compétences conceptuelles).
Planning
Determining in advance what should be accomplished, when, by whom, how and at what cost (planification).
Organizing
Arranging tasks, people and resources to carry out the plan; assigning work and granting authority (organisation).
Directing (leading)
Motivating, communicating, guiding, encouraging and coaching employees; focus on people (direction, diriger).
Controlling
Continuously checking results against goals and taking corrective action (contrôle).
Corrective action
A change made when results differ from the goal, to bring the plan back on track (action corrective).
Role
A set of expectations of how a manager will behave in a given situation (rôle).
Figurehead
Interpersonal role: represents the organization in formal, ceremonial and legal matters (symbole).
Leader
Interpersonal role: communicates with, trains, motivates and disciplines employees (leader).
Liaison
Interpersonal role: builds and maintains relationships with individuals and groups inside and outside the immediate work group; a network of contacts (agent de liaison).
Monitor
Informational role: collects information about the environment (observateur actif).
Disseminator
Informational role: transmits information to members of the organization (diffuseur).
Spokesperson
Informational role: transmits information to outsiders (porte-parole).
Entrepreneur
Decisional role: initiates change and new projects (entrepreneur).
Disturbance handler
Decisional role: deals with unexpected problems and crises (régulateur).
Resource allocator
Decisional role: decides who gets which resources; budgets, schedules, priorities (répartiteur de ressources).
Negotiator
Decisional role: negotiates contracts and agreements with unions, customers and suppliers (négociateur).
