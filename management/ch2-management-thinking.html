The Evolution of Management Thinking

Why does a fast-food kitchen time every step? Why does a bank have so many rules? Why do good bosses listen to their teams? Each answer comes from a theory of management. This chapter tells the story of these ideas, from the factories of the early 1900s to modern quality management. Why management theory appeared

In Chapter 1 you learned what managers do: they plan, organize, direct and control. But where do these ideas come from? Who decided that a manager should "plan", or that a company should have "rules"? This chapter is the history of management ideas. Each idea was born to solve a real problem of its time. When you understand the problem, the idea becomes easy to remember.

Management is very old

People managed big projects thousands of years before anyone wrote a theory of management. Look at the early civilizations:

  • Egypt: building the pyramids needed thousands of workers, huge blocks of stone, food for everybody and a plan that lasted many years.
  • China: the Great Wall needed workers, materials and supervisors spread over thousands of kilometres.
  • Mesopotamia: temples and merchants kept written records of goods, debts and payments on clay tablets.
  • Greece and Rome: the Roman army had a clear chain of command, standard training and standard equipment. Rome also built roads, bridges and cities across a huge empire.
Tunisian example · El Jem and Carthage

The amphitheatre of El Jem was built about 1,800 years ago. Someone had to plan the building, organize teams of stone cutters and carriers, direct the workers and control the quality of the work. Even earlier, Carthage ran a large trading network across the Mediterranean, with ships, ports and warehouses to manage. So the four functions of Chapter 1 were already in use — but nobody had written them down as a theory.

So what was missing? Managers learned only by experience and by copying others. Each manager had his own habits. There were no tested methods and no books that explained how to manage. People worked by rule of thumb.

DefinitionRule of thumb (règle empirique)

A way of doing something that comes from habit and experience, not from careful study or measurement. "We have always done it like this" is a rule of thumb.

The Industrial Revolution changed everything

The Industrial Revolution (révolution industrielle) started in Britain at the end of the 1700s and spread to Europe and the United States in the 1800s. Machines and steam power replaced hand work. Small workshops became big factories with hundreds or thousands of workers. Factories started mass production (production de masse): making very large quantities of the same product.

This created new problems that nobody knew how to solve:

  • How do you organize the work of 1,000 people in one building?
  • How do you train workers who came from farms and had never used a machine?
  • How long should a job take? How much should one worker produce in a day?
  • How do you keep costs low and quality stable?

Factory owners had no "science of management" to help them. They used rule of thumb, and the results were often poor: waste, slow work, conflicts and accidents.

From the late 1800s, engineers started to study work in a systematic way. They wanted to improve work processes, raise productivity and reduce costs. In the early 20th century (the 1900s and 1910s), the formal study of management began. The first school of thought is called the classical approach. Later came the humanistic approach and then the modern approaches.

DefinitionProductivity (productivité)

How much output a worker or an organization produces with a given amount of input. Example: a worker who sews 25 pockets per hour is more productive than one who sews 20 pockets per hour with the same machine. Higher productivity = more output from the same resources.

DefinitionManagement perspective (courant de pensée en management)

A group of ideas about how to manage organizations, created at a certain time to answer the problems of that time. In this chapter we study three big perspectives: classical, humanistic and modern.

Why do new ideas keep appearing? The textbook (Daft) explains that management thinking changes because society changes. Social forces change what people expect from their job. Political forces change laws and workers' rights. Economic forces change resources, technology and competition. A method that worked in a factory in 1910 may not work in a start-up in 2026.

Each management theory answered the biggest problem of its time: first efficiency, then people, then complexity and quality.

In 1972, Tunisia passed a law that attracted foreign clothing companies to produce for export. Big sewing factories opened in the Sahel (Monastir, Ksar Hellal, Sousse). Suddenly, managers had to organize hundreds of operators, train young workers who had never used an industrial machine, set daily targets and keep the quality required by European clients. These are exactly the problems that the first management theories were created to solve.

The classical perspective: an overview

The classical perspective was the first serious answer to the factory problems of the Industrial Revolution. It appeared when managers did not know how to raise productivity. Its central image: an organization should work like a well-designed machine. If we design the tasks, the structure and the management correctly, everything will run smoothly and efficiently.

DefinitionClassical perspective (approche classique)

The first school of management thought (late 1800s to the 1920s). It emphasized rationality and making organizations and workers as efficient as possible.

DefinitionRationality (rationalité)

Deciding by logic, facts and measurement — not by habit, feelings or personal relationships. A rational manager asks "What do the numbers show?", not "What did my father do?"

DefinitionEfficiency (efficience)

You met it in Chapter 1: using resources wisely, with low waste — "doing things right". The classical writers wanted the maximum output for the minimum time, money and effort.

The classical perspective has three subfields. Each one looks at a different part of the organization:

SubfieldKey personWhat it looks atIts central question
Scientific managementFrederick Taylor (USA)The task of each worker on the shop floorWhat is the one best way to do this job?
Bureaucratic organizationsMax Weber (Germany)The structure of the whole organizationWhich rules and hierarchy make the organization rational and fair?
Administrative principlesHenri Fayol (France)The job of the top managerWhich functions and principles help to direct and coordinate the whole organization?

How did classical writers see workers? Mostly as rational economic beings: people who work for money and who will do what pays them most. If you pay a worker more for more output, he will produce more. This view is simple and partly true. Later, the humanistic perspective will show that it is incomplete.

Today the classical perspective is less common as a complete way to manage, but its ideas are still used every day: standard procedures, job descriptions, organization charts, performance targets and the management functions all come from it. It remains useful for some organizations, especially those that do repetitive, high-volume work.

Example · one bank branch, three classical viewpoints

Imagine a BIAT branch in Tunis with long queues. How would each classical thinker help?

  • Taylor would take a stopwatch, time each step of serving a customer at the counter, remove useless movements and teach every teller the fastest method.
  • Weber would write clear procedures (which documents to ask for, who signs what), define each job precisely and make sure every customer is treated by the same rules.
  • Fayol would look at the branch manager's job: is the work planned, organized, directed, coordinated and controlled? Does each employee have only one boss?
Memory trick · T-T, W-W, F-F

Taylor → the Task. Weber → Written rules. Fayol → the Functions of the manager.

Common mistake

"Classical = old = useless." Wrong. The classical perspective is less common today as a complete system, but many of its tools are everywhere: every fast-food kitchen, bank procedure and organization chart uses classical ideas.

Exam alert

Be able to name the three subfields with their authors: scientific management (Taylor), bureaucratic organizations (Weber), administrative principles (Fayol). Also learn the key phrase: the classical perspective emphasized rationality and making organizations and workers as efficient as possible.

Scientific management (Frederick Taylor)

Frederick Winslow Taylor (1856–1915) was an American engineer. He started as a simple worker in a steel company (Midvale Steel) and became its chief engineer. He watched workers every day and saw three problems:

  • Each worker did the job his own way, by rule of thumb. Some ways were fast; many were slow and tiring.
  • Managers did not know how long a job should take, so they could not set fair targets.
  • Workers often worked slowly on purpose. Taylor called this "soldiering" (freinage). Why did they do it? They feared that if they worked faster, the boss would cut the pay per piece or need fewer workers.

Taylor's answer was simple and revolutionary: replace rule of thumb with science. Study each job carefully, measure it, find the best method, and teach it to everyone. For this reason he is called the "father of scientific management". His famous book, The Principles of Scientific Management, appeared in 1911.

DefinitionScientific management (organisation scientifique du travail, OST)

A classical approach that uses the scientific study of work — observation, measurement and standards — instead of rule of thumb, to improve labor efficiency (the productivity of each worker). In French it is often called OST or taylorisme.

The five tools of scientific management

1. Time and motion study. Break the job into its simplest movements (pick up, turn, place, walk…). Watch good workers and measure each movement with a stopwatch. Remove the useless movements. Keep only the fastest and least tiring ones.

DefinitionTime and motion study (étude des temps et des mouvements, chronométrage)

Breaking a job into simple movements, then analyzing and timing them so the job can be done in the minimum time.

2. The "one best way". After the study, there is one standard method: the same sequence of steps, the same tools, the same speed, for every worker. Nobody improvises anymore.

DefinitionOne best way (la meilleure façon de faire)

The single most efficient method to do a task, found by scientific study. All workers must follow it in the same standardized sequence.

3. Division of labor. Instead of one worker making a whole product, the job is cut into small tasks. Each worker repeats one or two movements all day and masters them. Taylor also separated thinking from doing: managers and engineers plan the work; workers execute it.

DefinitionDivision of labor (division du travail)

Splitting work into small, simple tasks, so that each worker specializes in one or two movements and repeats them.

4. Scientific selection and training. Choose the worker whose abilities fit the job, then train him in the standard method. Before Taylor, new workers learned by watching others, including their bad habits.

5. Piece-rate pay. Pay workers for each unit they produce. The most productive workers earn more. Taylor even proposed a differential piece rate: a low rate per piece below the standard, and a higher rate per piece for workers who reach the standard.

DefinitionPiece-rate pay (salaire aux pièces, salaire au rendement)

A pay system in which a worker earns a fixed amount for each unit produced, so higher output means higher pay.

Taylor summarized his method in four principles (in our own words):

  1. Develop a science for each part of a job, to replace rule of thumb.
  2. Select workers scientifically, then train and develop them in the standard method.
  3. Cooperate with the workers to make sure the work is done according to the science.
  4. Divide work and responsibility between managers (who plan) and workers (who execute).
Taylor's question: "What is the one best way to do this task, and how do we make every worker use it?"

Textbook example · loading pig iron at Bethlehem Steel

Taylor's most famous study took place around 1899 at Bethlehem Steel. Workers carried heavy bars of iron ("pig iron") onto railway wagons. Taylor studied the job: how to lift, how to walk, and — importantly — when to rest. He then trained a worker to follow the method exactly, and paid him more for reaching the new standard.

Textbook example · Henry Ford and mass production

Scientific management shaped mass production. Henry Ford was the first to apply it in the car industry. In 1913, his factory introduced the moving assembly line for the Model T: the car moved on a conveyor and each worker repeated one small task as it passed. According to the slides, the average time to produce a car fell from 12 hours to 93 minutes.

Worked example · how big was Ford's gain?

Step 1. Put both times in the same unit: 12 hours = 12 × 60 = 720 minutes.

Step 2. Compare: 720 ÷ 93 ≈ 7.7. A car was built about 7.7 times faster.

Step 3. Time saved: 720 − 93 = 627 minutes. 627 ÷ 720 ≈ 0.87, so the time fell by about 87%.

(Some books say 12.5 hours. Then 750 ÷ 93 ≈ 8 times faster. Either way, the gain is huge.) Cheaper production made the Model T affordable for ordinary families. But the work was so boring that very many workers quit, and in 1914 Ford had to roughly double pay to about $5 a day to keep them — an early sign of the human cost of the method.

Tunisian worked example · time study and piece rate in a jeans factory

Worked example · a jeans factory in Monastir

The production manager wants a fair daily target for the task "sew a back pocket". She times a trained operator five times: 1.9, 2.1, 2.0, 2.2 and 1.8 minutes.

Step 1 — average time. (1.9 + 2.1 + 2.0 + 2.2 + 1.8) ÷ 5 = 10.0 ÷ 5 = 2.0 minutes.

Step 2 — add an allowance. People need short rests and must change thread. The factory adds 10%: 2.0 × 1.10 = 2.2 minutes. This is the standard time (temps standard).

Step 3 — daily standard. An 8-hour day = 480 minutes. 480 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 218.2, so the standard is 218 pockets per day.

Step 4 — differential piece rate. Below the standard: 0.12 TND per pocket. At or above the standard: 0.15 TND per pocket.

Going further (not on the slides) · the Gilbreths

Frank and Lillian Gilbreth were famous partners of the scientific management movement. Frank filmed bricklayers and reduced the movements needed to lay one brick from about 18 to about 5. Lillian studied fatigue and the human side of work. Another Taylor follower, Henry Gantt, created the Gantt chart, still used today to plan projects.

DefinitionDeskilling (déqualification)

When a job is cut into tiny tasks, the worker no longer needs — or learns — broad skills. A tailor who once made a whole suit becomes an operator who only sews one seam.

Where scientific management is still alive

The slides say scientific management is still relevant in high-volume production: the car industry, computer manufacturing plants and fast-food restaurants. Look around you:

  • Fast food: each burger is made in the same steps, with timers, standard portions and a fixed layout of the kitchen.
  • Call centres in Tunis and Sousse: agents follow a script, and managers measure the average handling time of each call and the number of calls per hour.
  • Tunisian textile factories: sewing lines where each operator does one operation (collar, sleeve, hem), with a standard time per piece and an output bonus.
  • Car-parts factories in Tunisia (for example, factories that make wiring harnesses for European carmakers): each station has a precise, timed sequence of steps.
  • Delivery and warehouses: drivers and pickers follow optimized routes and are measured on time per stop or items per hour.
Common mistakes

1. "Ford invented scientific management." No — Taylor created it; Ford was the first to apply it in the car industry. 2. "Scientific management means making people work harder." Not exactly — it means working with a studied, standard method; in Taylor's pig-iron study, rest periods were part of the method. 3. The criticisms are not about productivity (which rose a lot) but about the human cost and the long term.

Show the model answer

Scientific management, created by Frederick Taylor, replaces rule of thumb with time and motion study, a standard "one best way", division of labor and piece-rate pay.

Bureaucratic organizations (Max Weber)

Taylor looked at one worker and one task. Max Weber (1864–1920), a German sociologist, looked at the whole organization. In his time, many European organizations were run like a family. The owner gave jobs to relatives and friends. Decisions depended on who you knew. People were loyal to a person, not to rules. Weber saw that this was unfair and inefficient: the best people were not always chosen, and decisions changed with the boss's mood.

His proposal: manage organizations impersonally. Regulate employees' behaviour with clear rules, regulations and procedures. Give authority to the position, not to the person. Weber called this form bureaucracy. He believed it was the most rational and efficient form of organization — the "ideal" type — better and more consistent than personal or family structures.

DefinitionBureaucracy (bureaucratie)

In Weber's sense: an organization managed impersonally, in which employees' behaviour is regulated by clear rules, regulations and procedures, with a clear hierarchy and specialized jobs. Weber saw it as the most rational and efficient form of organization.

Careful with the word

Today "bureaucracy" often has a negative meaning: slow offices, queues and too much paper ("red tape", paperasse). Weber meant something positive: fairness, logic and efficiency. The negative meaning describes what happens when bureaucracy goes wrong (see the limitations below).

The national recruitment exam for the public service, the concours, is a pure example of Weber's thinking: the same written test for every candidate.

Going further · Weber's three types of authority

Weber asked why people obey. He saw three answers: traditional authority (we obey because it has always been so — a king, a tribal chief, the head of a family), charismatic authority (we obey because of a leader's exceptional personality) and rational-legal authority (we obey because of rules and the official position). Bureaucracy is built on rational-legal authority: you obey the branch manager because of his position, not because he is your uncle.

Why bureaucracy can work well

  • Fairness: everyone is treated by the same rules.
  • Predictability: you know in advance what will happen and how long it takes.
  • Efficiency for routine, large-scale work: a bank or a social-security fund (like the CNSS) can process millions of identical files.
  • Continuity: when a person leaves, the rules and the files stay; the new employee can continue.
  • Competence: merit-based hiring brings qualified people.

Limitations of bureaucracy

The slides list several limitations. Each has a simple cause: rules and layers that were designed for order can become obstacles.

  • Slowness: a decision must pass through many levels and signatures. Example: a small purchase at a public school needs four signatures and takes weeks.
  • Rigidity: rules cannot foresee every case. When a customer's situation does not fit the form, nobody is allowed to decide.
  • Weak communication: with many layers, information travels slowly and gets distorted, like the "telephone game".
  • Barriers to innovation: hierarchies form barriers; a new idea from the bottom rarely reaches the top, or arrives too late.
  • Demotivation: employees cannot express their opinions or influence decisions, so they become annoyed and demotivated. Some start to follow rules blindly, even when the rule no longer makes sense.
Tunisian example · when rules become the goal

Youssef, a fresh graduate, needs a certified copy of his diploma for a job abroad. The office accepts requests only on Tuesday mornings, needs a stamped form bought at another office, and the signing official is on leave this week. Every rule has a reason, but together they make a simple task take three weeks. This is the "red tape" side of bureaucracy: the rules have become more important than the service to the citizen.

Common mistakes

1. Writing "Weber said bureaucracy is slow and bad." No — Weber presented it as the ideal, most efficient form; slowness and rigidity are its limitations. 2. Confusing "impersonal" with "unfriendly". Impersonal means that rules do not depend on who you are. An employee can be polite and still apply the rules equally to everybody.

Administrative principles (Henri Fayol)

Henri Fayol (1841–1925) was a French mining engineer. From 1888 he ran a large French mining and steel company for about 30 years. When he took over, the company was close to bankruptcy; when he retired, it was strong. Taylor studied the worker at the bottom. Fayol looked at the organization from the top: what does a general manager actually do, and which principles help him direct and coordinate the whole organization? He published his ideas in 1916 in a book with a French title you can read easily: Administration industrielle et générale.

Fayol's big message was new at the time: management is a separate skill that can be taught and learned, like engineering or accounting. Before him, people thought you were simply "born" a manager.

DefinitionAdministrative principles (principes d'administration, école administrative)

A classical approach that focuses on the total organization rather than the individual worker. It defines the functions of management and gives general principles to direct and coordinate the organization — the top manager's domain.

Fayol gave us three things: (1) six groups of activities found in every organization, (2) the functions of the manager, and (3) fourteen principles of management.

1. The six groups of organizational activities

Fayol said every company, big or small, performs six groups of activities. Example: an olive-oil company in Sfax.

Common mistake · unity of command vs unity of direction

Unity of command is about people: one employee → one boss. Unity of direction is about activities: one objective → one manager and one plan. Test: if the sentence talks about "receiving orders", it is unity of command; if it talks about "a group of activities with the same goal", it is unity of direction.

The humanistic perspective and the Hawthorne studies

By the 1920s, the classical approach had raised productivity a lot. But it could not explain some things. Why do some well-paid workers still work slowly? Why do groups of workers create their own rules? Why does a worker who feels respected work harder? Taylor treated workers mainly as economic actors who respond to money and orders. This view failed to explain employee behaviour and motivation.

The humanistic perspective grew from these criticisms. It appeared in the early 20th century as a counterweight to Taylor. It moved the attention from tasks and structures to people: their behaviour, their needs and their social relations at work. Its main idea: productivity and satisfaction improve when managers understand human behaviour at work — motivation, expectations, conflict and group dynamics.

DefinitionHumanistic perspective (approche humaniste, école des relations humaines)

A management perspective that emphasizes understanding human behaviour, needs and attitudes in the workplace, as well as social interactions and group processes. It is also called the human relations or behavioural approach.

DefinitionGroup dynamics (dynamique de groupe)

The way people in a group influence each other: how they cooperate, compete, create rules and resolve conflicts.

The Hawthorne studies (1924–1932)

The most important research of this perspective took place at the Hawthorne Works of the Western Electric company, a large factory near Chicago that made telephone equipment. It was a series of studies between 1924 and 1932. It started as a simple engineering question — "Does better lighting increase productivity?" — and ended by changing the way managers think about workers and motivation. The studies are linked to Elton Mayo, a professor at Harvard University, and his team.

PhaseWhat the researchers didWhat they found
1. Illumination experiments
1924–1927		Changed the lighting for a test group (brighter, then dimmer). A control group kept the same lighting.Output rose in both groups. It even stayed high when the light was made very dim. No consistent link between light and output.
2. Relay assembly test room
1927–1932		Six women assembling telephone parts worked in a separate room. Researchers changed rest breaks, the length of the working day and the week, and talked with them about each change.Output rose almost period after period — and stayed high even when the old conditions (no breaks, long days) came back.
3. Interview programme
1928–1930		More than 20,000 employees were interviewed about their work and their feelings.Workers valued being listened to. Feelings and attitudes strongly affect behaviour at work.
4. Bank wiring observation room

DefinitionTest group and control group (groupe expérimental, groupe témoin)

In an experiment, the test group receives the change (more light); the control group does not, so we can compare. At Hawthorne, both groups produced more — proof that the light was not the real cause.

What the Hawthorne studies discovered

  1. Physical conditions are not the main driver. Lighting, breaks and working hours had no consistent relationship with productivity.
  2. Social and psychological factors matter. Workers responded to attention, respect and their relationships — not only to pay and physical conditions.
  3. Informal groups have real power. Groups create unwritten rules about how much to produce and how to behave, sometimes stronger than formal rules.
  4. Attention raises motivation. Being observed, being listened to and feeling that one's work matters increased motivation. This is the Hawthorne effect.
  5. The supervisor's style matters. A friendly, respectful supervisor raised morale and output; a harsh one lowered them.

The conclusion: human relations and workers' social needs are crucial for productivity. A worker is a social being, not just a pair of hands.

Key ideas: the Hawthorne effect and informal groups

DefinitionHawthorne effect (effet Hawthorne)

People change their behaviour — usually they work harder — when they know they are being observed or when they receive special attention, being listened to, or feeling that their work matters.

DefinitionInformal group (groupe informel)

A group that forms naturally among employees through friendship, shared interests or working side by side. It does not appear on the organization chart, but it influences behaviour.

DefinitionGroup norm (norme de groupe)

An unwritten rule that a group expects its members to follow, for example "nobody produces more than 60 pieces an hour" or "we always help a colleague who is late".

DefinitionFormal vs informal organization (organisation formelle / informelle)

The formal organization is the official one: organization chart, job descriptions, written rules. The informal organization is the real network of relationships, friendships and unwritten rules that grows inside it.

Textbook example · the bank wiring room

The 14 men in the bank wiring room had their own idea of a "fair day's work". A worker who produced much more than the norm was called a "rate-buster"; one who produced much less was a "chiseler"; one who told the boss about the group was a "squealer". Members who broke the norm were teased or given a light hit on the arm. Result: output stayed at the group's level, even though the company paid more for more output. Lesson: the informal group can be stronger than the official incentive.

Tunisian example 1 · a call centre in Tunis

A team's results are falling. The new team leader, Mariem, changes nothing in the pay or the computers. She simply sits with each agent once a week, listens to their difficulties, explains why their work matters for the client, and thanks good work in front of the team. Within a month, the number of solved calls rises and absences fall. This is the Hawthorne lesson: attention, listening and the supervisor's style affect morale and output.

Tunisian example 2 · informal norms in a sewing workshop

In a workshop in Monastir, a new operator, Nour, sews much faster than the others. Her colleagues tell her to slow down: they fear the manager will raise the target for everyone. Nour slows down to stay accepted by the group. A manager who only looks at the piece rate will not understand why output stays flat. A humanistic manager will talk with the group, involve them in setting fair targets and reward the team, not only individuals.

Did you know?

The first lighting tests were run by Western Electric's own engineers; Mayo's Harvard team joined later (from 1928) and interpreted the results. The name "Hawthorne effect" was only given in the 1950s. Later researchers re-examined the data and argued that other factors — such as the new group payment and the fear of losing one's job during the Great Depression — also played a role. The studies were not perfect science, but they changed management thinking for ever.

Common mistakes

1. "The Hawthorne studies proved that better lighting raises productivity." No — they found no consistent link between lighting and output. 2. "The humanistic perspective says pay does not matter." No — it says pay and physical conditions are not enough; social and psychological factors matter too. 3. Confusing the Hawthorne effect (attention changes behaviour) with the Hawthorne studies (the whole research programme).

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

The Hawthorne studies showed that workers are not only "economic". But then, what do people really want? Abraham Maslow (1908–1970), an American psychologist, gave a famous answer in 1943. His starting point: human behaviour is purposeful. People act to satisfy their needs.

DefinitionNeed (besoin)

A physiological or psychological deficiency (manque) that a person feels. Hunger is a physiological need; feeling alone or not respected is a psychological need. A need creates tension, and the person acts to reduce it.

DefinitionHierarchy of needs (pyramide / hiérarchie des besoins de Maslow)

Maslow's theory that people have five types of needs, arranged in a hierarchy from basic (physiological) to highest (self-actualization). Lower needs must be satisfied before higher needs start to motivate.

Two rules you must remember

Rule 1 — the needs are hierarchical. A higher need does not motivate a person until the lower needs are (mostly) satisfied. A worker who cannot pay his rent does not think about "self-actualization" yet.

Rule 2 — a satisfied need is not a motivator. Once a need is satisfied, offering more of the same thing does not motivate much. The person's attention moves to the next level up.

A satisfied need is not a motivator. To motivate someone, find the need that is NOT yet satisfied.

Pay versus non-monetary motivators

Maslow shows that motivation is more complex than scientific management assumed. Taylor thought pay was enough. Maslow's view:

  • Pay (salaire) mainly satisfies the lower-level needs: food and housing (physiological), savings and security (safety).
  • Once these needs are satisfied, more money has a weaker effect. Then non-monetary motivators (motivations non financières) become important: praise, recognition, the characteristics of the job (interesting, varied, responsible), friendships, training and growth.
DefinitionNon-monetary motivator (facteur de motivation non financier)

Anything that motivates people at work without being money: praise, recognition, an interesting job, responsibility, a good team, training, the chance to grow.

Example · why well-paid people still leave

A software engineer in Tunis earns a good salary, has a CDI and likes his colleagues. His physiological, safety and social needs are satisfied. Yet he resigns. Why? His work is always the same and his ideas are never used: his esteem and self-actualization needs are not met. A pay rise of 50 TND would not keep him; a challenging project and recognition might.

Did you know?

Later research did not fully confirm Maslow's strict order. People can pursue several needs at the same time, and the importance of each level varies between cultures and individuals. But the two practical lessons — needs differ between people, and a satisfied need stops motivating — are still very useful for managers.

Common mistakes

1. "Maslow says money never motivates." Wrong — pay motivates the lower needs. 2. "A satisfied need motivates even more." Wrong — it is the opposite. 3. Mixing up levels: job security is safety (level 2), not physiological; recognition is esteem (level 4), not social (level 3). Social = acceptance and friendship; esteem = respect and status.

Modern theories (1): systems theory

By the 1960s, managers had many theories: Taylor's tasks, Weber's rules, Fayol's principles, Hawthorne's groups, Maslow's needs. But each theory studied one element separately — tasks, motions, functions, needs or attitudes. This way of thinking is called reductionism. It gives simplistic answers, because in a real organization everything is connected.

DefinitionReductionism (réductionnisme)

Studying a complex thing by breaking it into parts and analyzing each part alone. In management, it leads to simplistic prescriptions, because it ignores how the parts affect each other.

The systems view says: look at the whole organization and at the links between its parts. Managers must see the distinct elements and the complexities of how they interact. And above all: an organization depends on its environment for its resources. It is an open system that must adapt to a changing external environment.

DefinitionSystem (système)

A set of interrelated parts that function as a whole to achieve a common purpose. Your body is a system; so is a car, a university or a company.

DefinitionOpen system vs closed system (système ouvert / fermé)

An open system interacts with its environment: it takes resources in and gives outputs back, and it must adapt to changes outside. A closed system does not depend on its environment. Early management theories looked at organizations almost as closed systems.

The parts of an open system

  • Inputs (intrants, ressources): the resources the organization takes from the environment — human, material, financial and information resources.
  • Transformation process (processus de transformation): the production and management activities that turn inputs into outputs.
  • Outputs (extrants, résultats): what the organization gives back to the environment — products, services, profits, and also employee satisfaction.
  • Feedback (rétroaction, feedback): information about the results that comes back and helps the organization adjust (sales figures, customer complaints, market share).
  • Environment (environnement): everything outside the organization that affects it — customers, suppliers, competitors, government, the economy, technology.

PartIn a Tunisian dairy company (like Délice Danone)

Subsystems are interdependent

DefinitionSubsystem (sous-système)

A part of a larger system — for example, the production, sales, finance or HR department of a company. Subsystems are highly interdependent: what happens in one affects the others.

Because the parts are connected, a minor event in one part can grow and cause big effects elsewhere. Example: one packaging machine breaks down in the dairy → yogurts wait → cold rooms are full → trucks leave late → shops have empty shelves → customers buy a competitor's brand → sales fall. The sales manager sees "falling sales", but the cause was a machine.

Going further · two more systems words (Daft)

Synergy (synergie): the whole is greater than the sum of its parts ("2 + 2 = 5") — departments working together achieve more than they would alone. Entropy (entropie): the tendency of a system to run down and die if it stops receiving new inputs and adapting. Managers fight entropy by renewing resources and adapting to the environment.

Two benefits of systems thinking

Benefit 1 — better diagnosis of problems. A systems thinker asks: where in the system does the problem start? The slides' example: the sales team keeps missing delivery deadlines. The easy reaction is to blame the salespeople. But the real cause may be in production scheduling or in late suppliers. Fixing the wrong unit (training the sales team) wastes time and money, and the problem continues.

Benefit 2 — anticipating side effects. Before deciding, a systems thinker asks: who else will be affected? The slides' example: cutting the maintenance budget saves money this year, but leads to breakdowns and higher costs later.

Worked example · the maintenance "saving"

A factory in Bizerte cuts its maintenance budget by 20,000 TND this year. Next year, two machines break down. Each breakdown costs 18,000 TND in repairs and lost production.

Step 1. Total cost of the breakdowns: 2 × 18,000 = 36,000 TND.

Step 2. Net result of the decision: 20,000 − 36,000 = −16,000 TND.

The "saving" became a loss of 16,000 TND — and that does not even count angry customers who received late orders. A manager who thought only about the maintenance department (reductionism) missed the side effects on production, sales and customers.

Common mistakes

1. "Systems theory is about computers." No — "system" here means any set of connected parts, like an organization. 2. "The problem is where it appears." Often not: the symptom (late deliveries in sales) can appear far from the cause (production or suppliers). 3. Forgetting the environment and the feedback arrow when you draw the diagram.

Modern theories (2): contingency theory

The earlier approaches assumed that there are universal principles: one set of rules that works in every organization. Taylor's "one best way" is the clearest example. Contingency theory says: the one best way may suit simple, repetitive tasks, but it does not work for most managerial problems. Organizations are different, and situations are different. So the right way of managing depends on the circumstances.

DefinitionContingency theory (théorie de la contingence, approche contingente)

A modern view that says there is no single best way to manage. Managers should use a flexible approach, draw on many theories, evaluate their options, and choose the style or structure that fits the situation. Its short slogan: "It depends."

DefinitionContingency (contingence, facteur de contingence)

A factor in the situation that changes which management approach works best — for example the type of task, the people, the size of the organization, time pressure or the environment.

Three ways to see management

ViewWhat it saysProblem
Universalist viewThe same principles work everywhere ("one best way").Too rigid: ignores differences between situations.
Case viewEvery situation is unique; nothing can be generalized.Too vague: no lessons can be learned.
Contingency viewThere are useful theories and patterns, but which one to use depends on the key contingencies of the situation.Asks more work: managers must analyze the situation.

What does it depend on?

  • The task: routine and repetitive (scientific-management tools fit) or creative and new (freedom fits).
  • The people: young beginners need clear rules and close guidance; experienced experts need autonomy.
  • The size: a small organization can be informal; a large, complex one needs formal structures and rules.
  • Time pressure: in an emergency there is no time for long discussions.
  • The environment: a stable environment allows fixed procedures; a fast-changing one needs flexibility.

Modern theories (3): total quality management (TQM)

Total quality management started in the 1950s in the Japanese automotive industry. After the Second World War, Japanese products had a reputation for poor quality. Japanese managers invited American experts, especially W. Edwards Deming, and built a new way of managing in which quality is everyone's job. Thirty years later, Japanese cars (Toyota, Honda) were famous for their reliability, and TQM spread to the United States, Europe and the rest of the world.

DefinitionTotal quality management, TQM (management de la qualité totale)

A management approach in which the whole organization — not only a quality department — is responsible for improving quality. The organization is seen as a system of interdependent processes, and quality is improved continuously, with everyone involved.

DefinitionProcess (processus)

A sequence of activities that turns inputs into an output for a customer. Example: "taking an order → preparing it → delivering it → sending the invoice".

The four core principles of TQM

1. Customer focus. Quality is defined by the customer, not by the engineers. And there are two kinds of customers: external customers (the people who buy the product) and internal customers (the next department in the process, who receives your work).

DefinitionInternal customer (client interne)

The person or department inside the organization that receives your work as the next step of the process. In a yogurt factory, the packaging line is the internal customer of the filling line. At ISB, the exams office is the internal customer of the teachers who submit the marks.

2. Total employee involvement. Everyone, from top managers to frontline staff, owns quality. At Toyota, any worker on the line can pull a cord to stop production when he sees a problem. Many companies also use quality circles (cercles de qualité): small groups of employees who meet regularly to find and solve quality problems.

3. Process orientation. Most defects come from poorly designed processes, not from careless individuals. The idea associated with Deming: about 85% of problems are system problems that only management can fix; only about 15% are caused by individual workers. So instead of blaming people, fix the process.

4. Continuous improvement (kaizen). Make small improvements all the time, rather than waiting for occasional big changes. Kaizen is a Japanese word meaning "change for the better".

DefinitionKaizen (amélioration continue)

The Japanese practice of continuous improvement: many small, ongoing improvements made by everyone, every day, instead of rare large-scale changes.

Going further · the PDCA cycle

Deming popularized a simple four-step cycle for kaizen: Plan a small change → Do it on a small scale → Check the results → Act: keep the change if it works, then start again. Each turn of the cycle is one step of the staircase.

Tunisian worked example · kaizen in a wiring-harness factory

A factory near Sousse makes electrical wiring harnesses for European carmakers, who demand very high quality. A production line has 2,000 defective parts per million. Each quarter, small kaizen improvements (a better tool holder, a colour code, a checklist) reduce defects by 10%.

The 85% idea in numbers: if the factory records 200 quality problems, about 0.85 × 200 = 170 come from the system (process design, tools, training, materials) and only about 30 from individual mistakes. Blaming the operators would fix only a small part.

Tunisian example · TQM at ISB's registration office

Customer focus: ask students what "good service" means (short queues, clear information). Total involvement: clerks, teachers and the director all own the quality of registration. Process orientation: if every year students queue for hours, the problem is the process (one window, paper forms), not lazy clerks — so redesign it (online forms, appointment times). Kaizen: after each registration period, list three small things to improve next time.

Common mistakes

1. "Quality is the job of the quality department." Not in TQM — the whole organization is responsible. 2. "Kaizen = a big transformation project." No — kaizen is small, continuous steps. 3. "Defects are caused by careless workers." TQM says most defects (about 85%) come from the system, which management must fix.

His safety need is already satisfied, and a satisfied need is not a motivator. Higher needs (belongingness, esteem, self-actualization) are now more likely to motivate him.

In systems theory, which of these are OUTPUTS of an organization?

Outputs: products, services, profits and employee satisfaction. (a) are inputs, (c) is the transformation process, (d) belongs to the environment.
T11Multiple choice

Systems theorists criticized earlier approaches for "reductionism". This means:

Reductionism = breaking the organization into elements (tasks, motions, functions, needs, attitudes) and studying each alone. Systems theory looks at the whole and the links.
T12Multiple choice

Which sentence best summarizes contingency theory?

"It depends." Managers stay flexible, draw on many theories and choose what fits the circumstances. (a) and (d) are universalist ideas; (b) is one style that fits some situations only.
T13Multiple choice

In total quality management, who is responsible for quality?

Total employee involvement: the whole organization owns quality. Customers define quality (customer focus), but the organization is responsible for delivering it.
T14Multiple choice

According to the TQM idea associated with Deming, about what share of quality problems are system problems that management must fix?

About 85% are system problems (process design, tools, training); about 15% come from individuals. That is why TQM focuses on the process, not on blaming people.
T15Classical, humanistic or modern?

"Unity of command" and "esprit de corps" are ideas from which perspective?

Both are among Fayol's 14 principles, and Fayol belongs to the classical perspective. Don't be tricked by "esprit de corps": it sounds humanistic, but it is Fayol's.
T16Multiple choice

Which is a criticism of scientific management given in the slides?

T17Multiple choice

An employee skips his supervisor and the department head, and takes every small request directly to the CEO. Which of Fayol's principles is ignored?

The scalar chain is the line of authority from top to bottom; communication normally follows it. Jumping over two levels for small matters breaks it.
T18Multiple choice

Which approach insists that organizations depend on their environment for resources and must adapt to it?

Systems theory sees the organization as an open system that takes inputs from the environment and must adapt to its changes. The classical approaches looked mostly inside the organization.

P12Exam-style essay

Compare the classical and the humanistic perspectives: their view of the worker, their main authors, and one strength and one weakness of each. Then explain in 2–3 sentences how the modern perspectives try to go beyond both.

Show the model answer

Classical perspective (late 1800s–1920s; Taylor, Weber, Fayol). It sees the organization as a machine and the worker mainly as a rational economic being who responds to money and clear instructions. Strength: huge gains in efficiency and productivity (Ford: 12 hours → 93 minutes), plus tools still used today (standard methods, rules, POLC). Weakness: it ignores human needs — workers treated like robots, bored, stressed and deskilled; bureaucracies become slow and rigid.

Humanistic perspective (1920s–1950s; Mayo and the Hawthorne studies, Maslow). It sees the worker as a social and psychological being with needs, feelings and group relationships. Strength: it explains motivation better — attention, informal groups, the supervisor's style and higher needs (esteem, self-actualization) affect output. Weakness: it can neglect structure and efficiency, and its findings were not always scientifically solid (Hawthorne) or universal (Maslow's strict order).

Modern perspectives try to combine them. Systems theory looks at the whole organization — tasks, structure and people — as connected parts in an environment. Contingency theory says each older theory can be the right tool in the right situation. TQM combines efficient processes with the involvement of every employee.

13

Recap and key terms

What to remember

  • Management is very old (Egypt, China, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome), but its formal study began in the early 20th century, because the Industrial Revolution created big factories with no science of management.
  • Three perspectives: classical (late 1800s–1920s) → humanistic (1920s–1950s) → modern (1950s onward). Each answered the main problem of its time.
  • Classical = rationality and efficiency. Three subfields: Taylor → the Task; Weber → Written rules; Fayol → the Functions of the manager.
  • Taylor (father of scientific management): time and motion study, one best way, division of labor, piece rate. Ford: 12 h → 93 min (≈ 7.7 times faster, −87%). Criticisms: robots, stress, deskilling, fewer promotions. Still used in high-volume work.
  • Weber: bureaucracy = the most rational, efficient form. Five features: chain of command, specialization, written rules and procedures, impersonal relationships, separation of management and ownership. Limits: slow, rigid, weak communication, barriers to innovation, demotivation.
  • Fayol: the top manager's view. Six activities (technical, commercial, financial, security, accounting, managerial), the functions of management (→ POLC), 14 principles. Unity of command = one boss; unity of direction = one plan.
  • Humanistic: reaction to Taylor. Hawthorne (Western Electric, near Chicago, 1924–1932, Mayo): physical conditions → no consistent link with output; social factors, informal groups, attention (Hawthorne effect) and supervisor style matter.
  • Maslow: physiological → safety → belongingness → esteem → self-actualization. Lower needs first; a satisfied need is not a motivator; pay → lower needs, non-monetary factors → higher needs.
  • Systems theory: against reductionism. Open system: inputs → transformation → outputs → feedback, inside an environment. Two benefits: better diagnosis (sales vs production/suppliers) and anticipating side effects (maintenance cuts).
  • Contingency theory: "it depends" — no universal best way. Fast-food teenagers vs a research team; autocratic style in emergencies; small vs large organizations.
  • TQM (Japan, cars, 1950s, Deming): the whole organization is responsible for quality. Four principles: customer focus (internal and external), total employee involvement, process orientation (≈ 85% system problems), continuous improvement (kaizen).

Glossary

Rule of thumb
A method based on habit and experience, not on study or measurement (règle empirique).
Industrial Revolution
The move from hand work to machines and factories, from the late 1700s (révolution industrielle).
Mass production
Making very large quantities of the same standard product (production de masse).
Productivity
Output produced per unit of input, for example pieces per worker per hour (productivité).
Classical perspective
First school of management thought; emphasized rationality and making organizations and workers as efficient as possible (approche classique).
Rationality
Deciding by logic, facts and measurement rather than habit or personal ties (rationalité).
Scientific management
Taylor's approach: scientific study of work to improve labor efficiency (organisation scientifique du travail, OST, taylorisme).
Time and motion study
Breaking a job into simple movements and timing them to do it in minimum time (étude des temps et des mouvements).
One best way
The single most efficient, standardized method for a task (la meilleure façon de faire).
Division of labor
Splitting work into small tasks so each worker repeats one or two movements (division du travail).
Piece-rate pay
Pay per unit produced; more output, more pay (salaire aux pièces, au rendement).
Soldiering
Workers deliberately working slowly (freinage).
Deskilling
Loss of broad skills when jobs are cut into tiny tasks (déqualification).
Bureaucracy
Weber's impersonal organization based on rules, hierarchy and specialized jobs; the "ideal" rational form (bureaucratie).
Chain of command
The line of authority in which each person is supervised by someone one level above (hiérarchie, chaîne de commandement).
Impersonal relationships
Rules and sanctions applied equally to everyone, regardless of personal ties (relations impersonnelles).
Separation of ownership and management
The people who run the organization are not its owners (séparation propriété / gestion).
Red tape
Excessive rules, forms and signatures that slow things down (paperasse).
Administrative principles
Fayol's approach focusing on the whole organization and the manager's functions and principles (principes d'administration).
Unity of command
Each employee receives orders from one boss only (unité de commandement).
Unity of direction
Activities with the same objective are directed by one manager with one plan (unité de direction).
Scalar chain
The line of authority from top to bottom that communication normally follows (chaîne scalaire, voie hiérarchique).
Centralization
How much decision power stays at the top (centralisation).
Esprit de corps
Team spirit, harmony and unity (esprit d'équipe).
Humanistic perspective
Approach emphasizing human behaviour, needs, attitudes and social relations at work (approche humaniste, relations humaines).
Hawthorne studies
Research at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works near Chicago (1924–1932), linked to Elton Mayo, showing the power of social factors (études de Hawthorne).
Hawthorne effect
People change their behaviour (often work harder) when they are observed or receive attention (effet Hawthorne).
Informal group
A group that forms naturally among employees, outside the organization chart (groupe informel).
Group norm
An unwritten rule a group expects its members to follow (norme de groupe).
Need
A physiological or psychological deficiency a person feels (besoin).
Hierarchy of needs
Maslow's five levels: physiological, safety, belongingness, esteem, self-actualization (pyramide des besoins).
Self-actualization
The need to grow, create and reach one's full potential (accomplissement de soi).
Non-monetary motivator
A motivator that is not money: praise, recognition, interesting work, responsibility (motivation non financière).
Reductionism
Studying the parts of a system separately, which leads to simplistic answers (réductionnisme).
System
A set of interrelated parts that function as a whole to achieve a common purpose (système).
Open system
A system that interacts with and depends on its environment (système ouvert); the opposite is a closed system (système fermé).
Inputs
Resources taken from the environment: human, material, financial, information (intrants, ressources).
Transformation process
The production and management activities that turn inputs into outputs (processus de transformation).
Outputs
Products, services, profits and employee satisfaction given back to the environment (extrants, résultats).
Feedback
Information about results that returns to help the system adjust (rétroaction).
Environment
Everything outside the organization that affects it: customers, suppliers, competitors, government, economy, technology (environnement).
Subsystem
A part of a larger system, such as a department; subsystems are interdependent (sous-système).
Contingency theory
There is no universal best way; the right approach depends on the situation (théorie de la contingence).
Autocratic style
The manager decides alone and gives orders; fits emergencies and time shortage (style autocratique).
Total quality management (TQM)
The whole organization is responsible for continuously improving quality (management de la qualité totale).
Internal customer
The next person or department in the process, who receives your work (client interne).
Process orientation
Fixing processes rather than blaming individuals; about 85% of problems are system problems (orientation processus).
Kaizen
Continuous improvement through small, ongoing steps by everyone (amélioration continue).
Quality circle
A small group of employees who meet regularly to solve quality problems (cercle de qualité).
