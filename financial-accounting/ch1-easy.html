Accounting is the language of business. It tells the story of a business with numbers.

Accounting does three things. It identifies economic events. It records them. It communicates them to the people who need them.

An economic event is a money event of the business: a sale, a purchase, paying salaries.

Memory trick · Find it, write it, tell it

Identify = find the event. Record = write it in the books (in money, by date). Communicate = tell the users with reports called financial statements.

DefinitionAccounting (comptabilité)

An information system that identifies, records and communicates the economic events of an organization to interested users.

Recording only is called bookkeeping. Bookkeeping is one part of accounting, not all of it.

Accountants also analyze and interpret the reports. They explain what the numbers mean.

In French: accounting = comptabilité · bookkeeping = tenue des livres · financial statements = états financiers · users = utilisateurs.

Example · Café Jasmin in Sousse

On Monday the café sells 300 coffees for 600 TND.

  1. Identify: "A sale happened. It matters for the café."
  2. Record: the accountant writes "Sales 600 TND, Monday" in the books.
  3. Communicate: at the end of the month, the café gives a report to the owners and the bank: "This month we earned 18,000 TND of sales."
Common mistake · Bookkeeping ≠ accounting

Bookkeeping = only recording. Accounting = identify + record + communicate (and interpret). On the exam, "Bookkeeping covers all the steps" is false.

Accounting = Identify → Record → Communicate.

Who uses accounting information

Many people read accounting reports. We put them in two groups.

Internal users work inside the company. They are the managers: they plan, organize and run the business. Example: the marketing manager, the production supervisor, the finance director, the company officers (president, CEO, CFO).

External users are outside the company. The two most common are investors and creditors.

Ask: does this person run the company? Yes → internal (managers, officers). No → external. The two big external users are Investors and Creditors: "I C (I see) your reports from outside".

Managerial accounting makes reports for internal users. Financial accounting makes reports for external users.

Example · Sousse Mobile needs a loan

Sousse Mobile, a phone repair shop, asks a bank for a 10,000 TND loan.

  1. The bank reads the shop's financial statements. The bank is a creditor → external user.
  2. The bank's question: "Will this shop be able to pay us back?"
  3. The shop's manager also reads the reports to decide which repairs earn the most → internal user.
Common mistake · Company officers are internal

The president, the CEO and the finance director (CFO) are officers of the company. They run it, so they are internal. "The two most common external users are investors and company officers" is false.

03

Ethics, IFRS and measurement (historical cost, fair value)

1. Ethics

Accounting reports are useful only if they are honest.

Ethics = the standards that say if an action is right or wrong, honest or dishonest, fair or not fair.

When accountants lie, investors lose money. Famous scandals: Enron and AIG (USA), Parmalat (Italy), Satyam (India).

Memory trick · 3 steps for an ethics case: See → Study → Solve

1. See: recognize the ethical situation and the issue. 2. Study: identify the main elements and the stakeholders (people who can be harmed or helped). 3. Solve: list the alternatives, weigh their effect on each stakeholder, choose the most ethical one.

Example · "Cooking the books"

A finance director asks employees to make fake invoices so income looks bigger. This is unethical. The reports no longer show what really happened. Banks and investors will take wrong decisions.

2. Who writes the rules?

All companies must follow the same rules, so we can compare them. These rules are called GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles).

IASB (International Accounting Standards Board, in London) writes the IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards). More than 115 countries use IFRS. Your textbook uses IFRS.

FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) writes U.S. GAAP (USA).

Convergence = the work to reduce the differences between IFRS and U.S. GAAP.

Memory trick · I with I

IASB writes IFRS (International). FASB writes the American rules.

Good information has two qualities. Relevance: it can make a difference in a decision. Faithful representation: it shows what really happened (complete, neutral, free from error).

3. At what amount do we record an asset?

DefinitionHistorical cost principle (cost principle)

Record assets at their cost: the price paid when the company got them. Keep that cost while the company holds the asset, even if its value goes up.

DefinitionFair value principle

Report some assets and liabilities at fair value: the price you would receive today if you sold the asset. It is used mainly for investment securities (shares of other companies) because their market price is easy to find.

Example · The bakery's land
  1. In 2024, Boulangerie Yasmine buys land for 100,000 TND.
  2. In 2026, an expert says the land is worth 150,000 TND.
  3. Under the historical cost principle, the land stays at 100,000 TND in the books.

Now, the bakery also owns shares of a listed company, bought for 5,000 TND. Their market price today is 6,000 TND. These are investment securities → reported at fair value: 6,000 TND.

Memory trick · The receipt stays

Cost = the price on the receipt. The receipt does not change when prices move. Fair value = today's market price. Fair value is more relevant; cost is more faithful (easy to check).

In French: ethics = éthique · standards = normes · historical cost = Coût historique · fair value = Juste valeur · relevance = pertinence · faithful representation = représentation fidèle.

Monetary unit, economic entity, business forms

Accounting uses two basic assumptions. An assumption is a basic idea we accept as true.

DefinitionMonetary unit assumption (unité monétaire)

Record only the events that can be expressed in money. Employee morale, customer satisfaction or a good reputation are not recorded.

DefinitionEconomic entity assumption (entité économique)

Keep the activities of the business separate from the activities of its owner and of every other entity.

An economic entity can be any organization: a company, a school, a municipality, an association.

Memory trick · Two short rules

Monetary unit: "No price? Not in the books." Economic entity: "The owner's wallet is not the shop's wallet."

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Every business has resources: cash, machines, supplies. These are its assets.

Who gave the money for these assets? Two groups: the creditors (people the business owes money to) and the owners (shareholders).

The creditors' claim = liabilities. The owners' claim = equity.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
DefinitionAssets (Actif)

Resources the business owns or controls. They give future benefits. Examples: Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Inventory, Equipment, Land, Buildings.

DefinitionLiabilities (Passif / dettes)

Claims against assets: the debts the business must pay. Examples: Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Salaries and Wages Payable, Taxes Payable.

DefinitionEquity (Capitaux propres)

The owners' claim on the assets. It is what is left after paying all debts: Equity = Assets − Liabilities.

Why are liabilities written before equity? If the business closes, the creditors are paid first. The owners get what is left.

In French: assets = Actif · liabilities = Passif (dettes) · equity = Capitaux propres · creditor = créancier.

Memory trick · Owns = Owes + Owners

What the business owns = what it owes + what belongs to the owners.

Memory trick · The 3 forms of the equation

Need equity? E = A − L. Need liabilities? L = A − E. Need assets? A = L + E. It also works with changes: change in A = change in L + change in E.

Example 1 · Find the missing number

Café Jasmin has cash of 12,000 TND, equipment of 18,000 TND and supplies of 2,000 TND. It owes 9,000 TND to the bank.

Step 1. Assets = 12,000 + 18,000 + 2,000 = 32,000.

Step 2. Liabilities = 9,000.

Step 3. Equity = A − L = 32,000 − 9,000 = 23,000 TND.

Example 2 · Changes during a year

During 2026, a bakery's assets decrease by 50,000 TND and its liabilities decrease by 90,000 TND. What happened to equity?

Step 1. Write the equation with changes: ΔA = ΔL + ΔE.

Step 2. −50,000 = −90,000 + ΔE.

Step 3. ΔE = −50,000 + 90,000 = +40,000. Equity increased by 40,000 TND.

Common mistake · Signs in change questions

Keep the minus signs! Write ΔE = ΔA − ΔL = (−50,000) − (−90,000) = +40,000. Do not just add 50,000 and 90,000.

The equation is ALWAYS in balance: the left side always equals the right side.

Inside equity: share capital, revenues, expenses, dividends

In a corporation, equity has two parts.

Share Capital—Ordinary: the money shareholders put in when they buy shares.

Retained Earnings: the profits the business earned and kept (not paid out to shareholders).

Equity = Share Capital—Ordinary + Retained Earnings

Retained earnings change for three reasons. Revenues make it go up. Expenses make it go down. Dividends make it go down.

DefinitionRevenues (Produits)

Increases in equity from the business's activities to earn income: sales, fees, services, commissions, interest, rent, royalties.

DefinitionExpenses (Charges)

The cost of assets consumed or services used to earn revenue: rent, salaries and wages, utilities, advertising, property tax. They decrease equity.

DefinitionDividends (Dividendes)

Cash (or other assets) given to the shareholders. They decrease retained earnings, but they are NOT expenses: they are not used to earn revenue.

Net income = Revenues − Expenses

If expenses are bigger than revenues, the result is a net loss.

Assets = Liabilities + Share Capital + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends

This last line is the expanded accounting equation. Revenues − Expenses − Dividends are the parts of Retained Earnings.

Memory trick · RED for Retained Earnings

Revenues ↑ · Expenses ↓ · Dividends ↓. Only R makes retained earnings go up. And: Revenue = money earned by working; Expense = cost used to earn; Dividend = a gift back to owners (not used to earn → not an expense).

In French: share capital = Capital social · retained earnings = Bénéfices non distribués / Résultats reportés · revenues = Produits · expenses = Charges · dividends = Dividendes · net income = bénéfice net · net loss = perte nette.

Transaction analysis, step by step

A transaction is an economic event that the accountant records.

The test is one question: does it change the assets, the liabilities or the equity? Yes → record it. No → nothing to record.

Hiring someone, ordering goods, signing a contract, talking with a customer: nothing changed yet → no transaction.

DefinitionDual effect

Every transaction changes at least two things. If an asset goes up, then another asset goes down, or a liability goes up, or equity goes up. So the equation always stays in balance.

External transactions are with outside parties (buying from a supplier, paying rent, selling to a customer). Internal transactions happen inside the company (using up supplies).

Memory trick · The 4 steps

1. Did something change? No → no entry. 2. Which items? (Cash, Supplies, Accounts Payable...) 3. Up (+) or down (−)? 4. Check: change in assets = change in liabilities + change in equity.

Memory trick · Equity only moves for 4 reasons

Equity changes only with: shareholders invest (+), revenue (+), expense (−), dividends (−). Everything else (buying, paying debts, collecting, borrowing) does not touch equity.

The 6 classic traps
  • Paying a creditor (Accounts Payable) = Cash −, Liability −. Not an expense.
  • Collecting from a customer (Accounts Receivable) = Cash +, A/R −. Not revenue (the revenue was recorded when the work was done).
  • Revenue on account = revenue now, even with no cash: A/R +, Revenue +.
  • Borrowing = Cash +, Notes Payable +. Not revenue.
  • Dividends = Cash −, Dividends −. Not an expense.
  • Buying equipment for cash = one asset up, one asset down: total assets do not change.

In French: transaction = opération · on account / on credit = à crédit · to collect = encaisser · to pay a creditor = régler un fournisseur.

Yes. At least two items change, so the equation stays balanced.

The four financial statements

At the end of the period, accounting communicates with four statements. You prepare them in this order:

#StatementWhat it showsTime
1Income statementRevenues and expenses → net income or net lossA period ("For the Month Ended...")
2Retained earnings statementBeginning RE + net income − dividends = ending REA period
3Statement of financial positionAssets, equity and liabilitiesOne date ("September 30, 2026")
4Statement of cash flowsCash in and cash out: operating, investing, financingA period
Memory trick · I Really Should Count

Income statement → Retained earnings statement → Statement of financial position → Cash flows. And: the SFP is a photo (one date); the other three are a film (a period).

In French: income statement = État du résultat (compte de résultat) · retained earnings statement = état des résultats non distribués · statement of financial position = Bilan / État de la situation financière · statement of cash flows = Tableau des flux de trésorerie.

Common mistakes
  • Dividends are in the retained earnings statement, never in the income statement.
  • Shareholders' investments are not in the income statement either (they go to Share Capital on the SFP, and to financing in cash flows).
  • Revenues and expenses do not appear on the SFP: they are already inside retained earnings.
  • Net income ≠ cash. Revenue on account is income but not cash; buying equipment is cash out but not an expense.

There is also a fifth statement, the comprehensive income statement. It shows "other comprehensive income" items that are not part of net income. Just know its name for now.

Memory trick · O, I, F

Before you start: the 10 rules

  • Accounting = identify, record, communicate. Bookkeeping = recording only.
  • Internal users run the company; the two main external users are investors and creditors.
  • Historical cost: keep the cost. Fair value: market price, for investment securities.
  • Monetary unit: only money amounts. Economic entity: the owner is not the business.
  • Proprietorship (1 owner), partnership (2+), corporation (shares, limited liability).
  • Assets = Liabilities + Equity, after every transaction. E = A − L.
  • Equity = Share Capital + Retained Earnings. RED: Revenues ↑, Expenses ↓, Dividends ↓.
  • Dividends ≠ expense. Collecting a receivable ≠ revenue. Paying a payable ≠ expense. Borrowing ≠ revenue.
  • Hiring, ordering, signing a contract = no entry.
  • Order: Income statement → Retained earnings → SFP (one date) → Cash flows (operating, investing, financing).
