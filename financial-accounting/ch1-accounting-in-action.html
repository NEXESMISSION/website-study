What is accounting?

Every organization (a café, a bank, a hospital, a ministry) does things with money every day. It sells, buys, pays salaries, borrows. These are called economic events. Owners, managers, banks and the tax office all want answers to simple questions: How much did the business earn? What does it own? What does it owe? Accounting is the system that answers these questions.

DefinitionAccounting (comptabilité)

An information system that identifies, records and communicates the economic events of an organization to interested users.

The three activities of accounting

  1. Identification. The accountant selects the economic events that are relevant to the business. Examples: selling goods, providing a service, paying wages, buying a machine. A friendly chat with a customer is not an economic event, so it is not selected.
  2. Recording. The accountant writes each event in a chronological diary (in date order), measured in money. The events are also classified (grouped by type, for example all sales together) and summarized (added up into totals).
  3. Communication. The accountant prepares accounting reports, called financial statements (états financiers), and then analyses and interprets them for the users. The main ones are the income statement (compte de résultat), the retained earnings statement, the statement of financial position (bilan) and the statement of cash flows (tableau des flux de trésorerie). You will build all four in section 09.
Accounting = Identify → Record → Communicate. Bookkeeping is only the "Record" part.
DefinitionBookkeeping (tenue des comptes)

The recording of economic events. It is only one part of accounting. The accounting process includes bookkeeping, but it also includes identifying the events and communicating, analysing and interpreting the results.

Example · A café in Sfax

Café Jasmin in Sfax, one day in March:

  • It sells 150 coffees at 2 TND each = 300 TND in cash. → Identify: yes, this is a sale. Record: "12 March, coffee sales, 300 TND, cash".
  • It pays the waiter's daily wage of 40 TND. → Identify: yes. Record: "12 March, wages paid, 40 TND".
  • The owner talks with a regular customer about a possible new menu. → Identify: no. Nothing was bought, sold or paid, so nothing is recorded.
  • At the end of March, the accountant adds up all the sales and all the costs of the month and prepares an income statement. The owner sees a profit of 2,600 TND, and the accountant explains that profit rose because sales were higher during the school holidays. → Communicate (a report, plus analysis and interpretation).
Memory trick

Think of a journalist. First she decides what is news (identify), then she writes it down (record), then she publishes and explains it (communicate). An accountant does the same with money events.

Common mistake

(1) Saying that accounting and bookkeeping are the same thing. Bookkeeping is only recording. (2) Thinking accountants only prepare reports. They also analyse and interpret them. (3) In multiple-choice questions, words like "verification", "economic entity" or "auditing" are NOT steps of the accounting process.

Exam alert

"Which of the following is NOT a step in the accounting process?" appears both in the slides and on the in-class sheet. The right answer is always the option that is not identification, recording or communication.

02

Who uses accounting information?

Accounting information is produced for people who must make decisions. We divide these users into two groups: internal users (inside the company) and external users (outside the company). The same numbers serve both groups, but they ask different questions.

Internal users

DefinitionInternal users (utilisateurs internes)

The managers who plan, organize and run the business: marketing managers, production supervisors, finance directors and company officers (the top managers, such as the chief executive officer and the chief financial officer).

Managers need detailed reports: the cost of each product, a forecast of next year's cash, a comparison of this month with last month. The branch of accounting that prepares these internal reports is called managerial accounting (comptabilité de gestion).

DepartmentTypical question of an internal user
FinanceIs cash sufficient to pay dividends (dividendes: cash paid out to the shareholders, see section 06)?
MarketingWhat price for an iPhone will maximize the company's profit?
Human resourcesCan we afford to give employees pay raises this year?

External users

DefinitionExternal users (utilisateurs externes)

Individuals and organizations outside the company who want financial information about it. They cannot ask managers for internal reports; they use the published financial statements.

The two most common external users are:

  • Investors (investisseurs): the owners (shareholders) and possible future owners. They use accounting information to decide whether to buy, hold or sell ownership shares.
  • Creditors (créanciers): suppliers who sell on credit, and bankers who lend money. They use accounting information to evaluate the risk of granting credit or lending money.

Example · A repair shop in Tunis

Garage Ben Salah, a small car repair shop in Tunis, asks a bank for a loan of 20,000 TND to buy a new diagnostic machine.

  • The bank (external user, a creditor) asks for the financial statements of the last two years. Its question: "Can this garage repay 20,000 TND plus interest?"
  • Karim, the owner-manager (internal user) looks at a monthly internal report that shows which service earns the most (oil changes, brakes or electrical repairs). He uses it to decide if the new machine is worth buying.
  • The tax administration (external user) uses the profit declared by the garage to check the tax it owes.

Same business, three users, three different questions.

Memory trick

Ask one question: "Does this person work inside the company and help to run it?" Yes → internal. No → external. Managers and company officers are always internal. Investors, creditors, tax authorities, regulators, customers and unions are always external.

Common mistake

Thinking that regulatory or taxing authorities are internal users because they are powerful. They are outside the company, so they are external. And "company officers" are internal, even though they sound official.

03

Why ethics is a fundamental business concept

Financial statements are useful only if users can trust them. A bank lends money because it believes the numbers. An investor buys shares because she believes the profit is real. If the numbers are false, everybody makes bad decisions. This is why the textbook says that effective financial reporting depends on sound ethical behavior.

DefinitionEthics (éthique)

The standards of conduct by which one's actions are judged as right or wrong, honest or dishonest, fair or not fair.

When ethics fails: famous scandals

In the last 25 years, several large companies published false or misleading financial information. When the truth came out, shareholders lost their investments, employees lost their jobs, and people stopped trusting financial markets. The slides mention four of them:

  • Enron (USA, energy)
  • Parmalat (Italy, dairy and food)
  • Satyam Computer Services (India, information technology)
  • AIG (USA, insurance)

After scandals like these, governments made the rules stricter. One example is the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in the USA (see section 10).

Analysing an ethics case in three steps

The textbook gives a method (Illustration 1-4) to solve ethical dilemmas. Learn the steps in this order:

  1. Recognize an ethical situation and the ethical issues involved. Use your personal ethics or the company's code of ethics to see that there is a problem (lying, hiding information, unfair treatment).
  2. Identify and analyze the principal elements in the situation. Who are the stakeholders (parties prenantes), the persons or groups who may be harmed or benefited? What are their rights or claims, and what are the responsibilities of each party?
  3. Identify the alternatives, and weigh the impact of each alternative on the various stakeholders. Then select the most ethical alternative, considering all the consequences. Sometimes there is one right answer; sometimes there are several, and you choose the best one.
Ethics insight · Dewey & LeBoeuf ("I felt the pressure — would you?")

Dewey & LeBoeuf LLP was a large international law firm. Instructed by the chief financial officer (CFO), employees created fake invoices and gave the auditors incorrect information, so that the firm's results looked better than they really were. The people at the top described what they were doing with words like "accounting tricks", "cooking the books" and "fake income".

The employees said they felt pressure from their bosses. The textbook asks you: would you do it? The lesson: pressure from a boss does not make a dishonest act acceptable. The person who creates the fake invoice is responsible too.

Worked example · Applying the three steps (a furniture company in Sfax)

Amira is the accountant of a furniture company in Sfax. On 28 December, the manager asks her to record a 15,000 TND sale that will only be delivered in January. The company is asking a bank for a loan and wants this year's profit to look bigger.

Step 1, Recognize. Recording a sale that has not happened yet makes this year's revenue and profit false. The bank would be misled. This is an ethical issue of honesty.

Step 2, Stakeholders. The bank (it could lend money on the basis of false numbers and lose it); the shareholders; the employees (if the company cannot repay, jobs are at risk); the tax administration (tax would be calculated on a wrong profit); the manager; and Amira herself (her job, her professional reputation, and possible legal responsibility).

Step 3, Alternatives. (a) Record the sale in December as asked: it helps the manager in the short term, but it misleads the bank and it is dishonest. (b) Refuse, explain the rule (a sale is recorded when it actually happens), and record it in January. (c) Record correctly, and suggest giving the bank a separate note mentioning the signed January order, as honest extra information.

Most ethical choice: (b) together with (c). The statements stay true, and the bank still receives useful information.

Common mistake

Writing "it is ethical because the manager ordered it" or "it is fine because the amount is small". In an ethics case, always name the stakeholders and compare the alternatives. That is how you get the marks.

Exam alert

Know the definition of ethics by heart (right/wrong, honest/dishonest, fair/not fair), the three steps in order, and the four scandal names: Enron, Parmalat, Satyam, AIG.

Accounting standards and measurement principles

Imagine that every company could choose its own way to calculate profit. One café counts a sale when the coffee is served; another counts it when the customer pays at the end of the month. You could never compare them. To make financial statements comparable and trustworthy, all accountants follow common rules.

DefinitionGenerally Accepted Accounting Principles, GAAP (principes comptables généralement admis)

A common set of standards that tells companies how to report economic events.

Global insight · The "Korean discount"

For years, investors paid less for shares of South Korean companies than for similar companies in other countries, because they did not fully trust Korean accounting numbers. This was called the "Korean discount". To fix it, Korea adopted international standards. China, India, Japan and Hong Kong have also adopted IFRS or based their own standards on it.

Lesson: trustworthy, comparable accounting rules make a country's companies more valuable to investors.

Two qualities of useful information

DefinitionRelevance (pertinence)

Financial information is relevant if it would make a difference in a business decision.

DefinitionFaithful representation (représentation fidèle)

Information shows what really happened: it is factual, complete, neutral and free from error.

The price you could sell a building for today is very relevant to an investor, but this estimate may be uncertain. The price actually paid, written on the purchase invoice, is older information, but it faithfully represents what happened, and anyone can check (verify) it.

Measurement principles: at what amount do we record?

DefinitionHistorical cost principle (principe du coût historique)

Also called the cost principle. Companies record assets (actif: the resources the business owns; full definition in section 06) at their cost. This is true when the asset is acquired AND for the whole time the company holds it.

Example · Land at historical cost

Textbook case. A company buys land for €300,000. Later, an expert estimates it is worth €400,000. Under the historical cost principle, the land stays in the accounts at €300,000. The €100,000 increase is not recorded.

Tunisian case. Café Jasmin buys a plot of land next to the café for 120,000 TND, to use as a car park. Two years later, similar plots sell for 150,000 TND. The land is still reported at 120,000 TND, its cost.

DefinitionFair value principle (principe de la juste valeur)

Assets and liabilities (passif / dettes: what the business owes) should be reported at fair value, which is the price that would be received to sell an asset, or paid to settle a liability. Under IFRS this is used mainly for certain investment securities, because their market price is readily available (for example, shares listed on a stock exchange).

Example · Investment securities at fair value

Café Jasmin also owns 1,000 shares of a company listed on the Bourse de Tunis. It bought them at 12 TND each: 1,000 × 12 = 12,000 TND. At year-end, the share price is 15 TND.

These are investment securities with a readily available market price, so they are reported at fair value: 1,000 × 15 = 15,000 TND.

Compare: the land stays at its cost (120,000 TND), while the listed shares are shown at today's market value (15,000 TND).

Common mistake

Mixing up the two qualities. Relevance = it makes a difference to a decision. Faithful representation = it matches what really happened (factual).

Exam alert

A classic true/false: "In later periods, the fair value of the asset must be used if fair value is higher than its cost." This is FALSE. Under the historical cost principle, the asset stays at cost, even if its value goes up.

05

Two basic assumptions and the three forms of business

Before recording anything, accountants make two basic assumptions (hypothèses de base). They answer two questions: what can be recorded, and whose events are recorded?

The monetary unit assumption

DefinitionMonetary unit assumption (hypothèse de l'unité monétaire)

Include in the accounting records only transaction data that can be expressed in money terms (TND, €, £…).

So, things that are important but cannot be measured in money are not recorded: customer satisfaction, employee morale, the honesty of the managers, a great location. They matter for the business, but they are not in the accounts.

The economic entity assumption

DefinitionEconomic entity assumption (hypothèse de l'entité économique)

The activities of the entity must be kept separate and distinct from the activities of its owner and of all other economic entities.

An economic entity can be any organization or unit in society: a company, a government unit, a municipality (for example the Municipality of Sfax), a school district, or a church. Consequence: the owner's personal car, holidays or house rent are not in the business's records. And if one person owns two businesses, each business keeps its own separate records.

The three forms of business ownership

A business can be organized in three main forms. The form decides who the owners are and how much they risk.

DefinitionProprietorship (entreprise individuelle)

A business owned by one person. The owner is often also the manager. The owner receives all the profits, suffers all the losses, and is personally liable for all the debts of the business. Typical: small service businesses (plumbers, barbers), farms, small retail shops.

DefinitionPartnership (société de personnes)

A business owned by two or more persons associated as partners. Often retail and service businesses, and professional practices (lawyers, doctors, architects). Partners generally have unlimited personal liability. A partnership agreement (written or oral) sets the terms, for example how income and losses are divided.

DefinitionCorporation (société de capitaux, for example a société anonyme, "SA")

A business whose ownership is divided into transferable shares. It is a separate legal entity organized under corporation law. The owners, called shareholders (actionnaires), have limited liability: they are not personally liable for the corporation's debts. Shareholders can sell their shares easily, and the corporation continues even when owners change. Most large companies (Nestlé, Toyota, Unilever) are corporations.

Good to know

For accounting, even a proprietorship is a separate economic entity. Its records are kept apart from the owner's personal records, even though, legally, the owner and the business are the same person.

Common mistake

"Partnerships are not economic entities." False: every form of business (and every organization, even a municipality) is an economic entity for accounting. Also, don't confuse assumptions (monetary unit, economic entity) with principles (historical cost, fair value). They are different things.

The basic accounting equation

Everything in this course rests on one simple equation. A business has resources (assets). Somebody has a claim on those resources: either the people the business owes money to (creditors) or the owners. So the resources must always equal the claims on them.

Assets = Liabilities + Equity
The accounting equation must ALWAYS balance, after every single transaction.

It is the framework for recording and summarizing all economic events. Why are liabilities written before equity? Because if a business closes and sells everything (liquidation), the creditors are paid first. The owners get only what is left.

Assets

DefinitionAssets (actif)

Resources a business owns (controls) that are expected to provide future services or benefits.

  • Cash (trésorerie): money in the cash register and in the bank.
  • Accounts receivable (créances clients): money that customers owe the business for services already performed or goods already sold on credit.
  • Supplies (fournitures): paper, ink, small items that the business will use.
  • Inventory (stocks): goods held for sale.
  • Equipment (matériel): machines, computers, furniture, vehicles.
  • Land (terrains) and Buildings (constructions).

Liabilities

DefinitionLiabilities (passif / dettes)

Claims against assets: the existing debts and obligations of the business. The parties to whom money is owed are called creditors (créanciers).

  • Accounts payable (dettes fournisseurs): amounts owed to suppliers for goods or services bought on credit ("on account").
  • Notes payable (effets à payer / emprunts): written promises to repay, for example a bank loan.
  • Salaries and wages payable (dettes envers le personnel): wages already earned by employees but not yet paid.
  • Taxes payable (dettes fiscales): taxes owed to the government.

Equity

DefinitionEquity (capitaux propres)

The ownership claim on total assets. It is what remains after the liabilities are subtracted from the assets, so it is also called residual equity: Equity = Assets − Liabilities.

In a corporation, equity has two parts:

  • Share capital—ordinary (capital social): the amount the shareholders invested in exchange for ordinary shares.
  • Retained earnings (résultats non distribués / réserves): the net income kept (retained) in the business, meaning all net income earned so far minus all dividends paid.
Equity = Share Capital—Ordinary + Retained Earnings

What makes equity go up or down?

DefinitionRevenues (produits / chiffre d'affaires)

The gross increases in equity resulting from business activities entered into for the purpose of earning income. Sources: sales, fees, services, commissions, interest, dividends (received from shares in other companies), royalties and rent. Common accounts: Service Revenue, Sales Revenue, Rent Revenue, Interest Revenue.

DefinitionExpenses (charges)

The cost of assets consumed or services used in the process of earning revenue. They are decreases in equity. Examples: salaries and wages expense, rent expense, utilities expense (electricity, water, gas: think of the STEG and SONEDE bills), property tax expense, advertising expense.

DefinitionDividends (dividendes)

A distribution of cash or other assets to shareholders. Dividends reduce retained earnings, but they are NOT expenses. They are not a cost of earning revenue; they are a sharing of the profit with the owners.

DefinitionNet income (résultat net, bénéfice) and net loss (perte nette)

Net income = Revenues − Expenses, when revenues are greater than expenses. When expenses are greater than revenues, the difference is a net loss.

The biggest trap of the chapter

Dividends are NOT expenses. They decrease retained earnings, but you never subtract them when you compute net income. Net income = revenues − expenses, and nothing else. Also: dividends a company receives from shares it owns are revenue; dividends it pays to its own shareholders are dividends (not an expense).

Expect quick calculations like "assets €3,500, equity €2,000, find the liabilities" or "assets decreased €50,000 and liabilities decreased €90,000, what happened to equity?". Always write the equation first, then put the numbers in. Careful with signs: a decrease is a negative change.

Transactions and the expanded equation

Now we use the equation. Every time something happens that changes what the business owns, owes, or what belongs to the owners, the accountant records it. These events are called transactions.

DefinitionTransactions (opérations)

The economic events of a business that are recorded by accountants.

  • External transactions involve an outside party: buying equipment from a supplier, paying rent to a landlord, selling services to customers.
  • Internal transactions happen entirely inside the company: for example, using up supplies.

Is it a transaction? The test

Not every activity is a transaction. Ask one question: "Did this event change the financial position (the assets, liabilities or equity) of the company?" If yes, record it. If no, don't.

EventFinancial position changed?Decision

The dual effect

Each transaction has a dual effect on the equation: at least two items change, and the equation stays balanced. For example, if an asset increases, then one of these must also happen: another asset decreases, or a liability increases, or equity increases.

Some transactions change three or more items. Example: buying equipment for 6,000 TND, paying 2,000 TND in cash and owing the rest: Equipment +6,000, Cash −2,000, Accounts Payable +4,000. Left side: +6,000 − 2,000 = +4,000. Right side: +4,000. Still balanced.

The expanded accounting equation

We know that equity = share capital + retained earnings, and that retained earnings go up with revenues and down with expenses and dividends. So we can "open up" equity:

Assets = Liabilities + Share Capital—Ordinary + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends

We use the items of this expanded equation as column headings to analyse transactions. That is exactly what we do with Softbyte in the next section.

Common mistake

Thinking every transaction changes equity. Only transactions with revenues, expenses, dividends or shareholders' investments change equity. Buying equipment for cash, buying supplies on credit, paying a supplier or collecting from a customer do not change equity, and they do not change net income.

Transaction analysis step by step: Softbyte SA

This is the most important example of the chapter, and the professor's slides follow it closely. Ray and Barbara Neal start a smartphone app development company, Softbyte SA, on September 1, 2017. They are its shareholders. We analyse its first ten transactions one at a time, using the expanded equation as column headings:

Cash + Accounts Receivable + Supplies + Equipment = Accounts Payable + Share Capital—Ordinary + (Revenues − Expenses − Dividends)

For each transaction ask three questions: (1) Which items change? (2) Up or down, and by how much? (3) Is the equation still balanced? Expenses and dividends are written with a minus sign in the table, because they reduce equity.

Transaction 1 · Investment by shareholders

What happened

Ray and Barbara Neal invest €15,000 cash in the business in exchange for €15,000 of ordinary shares.

Analysis: the asset Cash increases €15,000, and equity, Share Capital—Ordinary, increases €15,000. Why share capital and not revenue? Because the money comes from the owners as an investment, not from selling a service. An investment by the owners is never revenue.

Cash +15,000 = Share Capital—Ordinary +15,000

Equation check: Assets €15,000 = Liabilities €0 + Equity €15,000. Balanced.

Transaction 2 · Purchase of equipment for cash

What happened

Softbyte buys computer equipment for €7,000 cash.

Analysis: Equipment increases €7,000 and Cash decreases €7,000. One asset is exchanged for another. Total assets do not change (still €15,000); only their composition changes. No liability and no equity are involved.

Cash −7,000 + Equipment +7,000 = (no change on the right side)

Equation check: Assets €15,000 (Cash 8,000 + Equipment 7,000) = Liabilities €0 + Equity €15,000. Balanced.

Transaction 3 · Purchase of supplies on credit

What happened

Softbyte buys headsets and other computer accessories, expected to last several months, for €1,600. The supplier allows Softbyte to pay this bill in October.

Analysis: Supplies increase €1,600 (an asset, because they will be used in future months). Accounts Payable increase €1,600 (a liability, because Softbyte owes the supplier). "On credit" or "on account" means the money will be paid later. When Softbyte buys on account, Softbyte owes the money: a liability (Accounts Payable). When Softbyte sells or performs services on account (see transaction 6), the customer owes Softbyte: an asset (Accounts Receivable), not a liability.

Supplies +1,600 = Accounts Payable +1,600

Equation check: Assets €16,600 (Cash 8,000 + Supplies 1,600 + Equipment 7,000) = Liabilities €1,600 + Equity €15,000 = €16,600. Balanced.

Transaction 4 · Services performed for cash

What happened

Softbyte receives €1,200 cash from customers for app development services it has performed.

Analysis: Cash increases €1,200, and Revenues increase €1,200 (Service Revenue), so equity increases. Revenue is recorded because Softbyte has done the work and earned the money.

Cash +1,200 = Revenues +1,200 (Service Revenue)

Equation check: Assets €17,800 (Cash 9,200 + Supplies 1,600 + Equipment 7,000) = Liabilities €1,600 + Share Capital €15,000 + Revenues €1,200 = €17,800. Balanced.

Transaction 5 · Purchase of advertising on credit

What happened

Softbyte receives a bill for €250 from Programming News for advertising its business. It decides to pay the bill later.

Analysis: Accounts Payable increase €250 (a liability), and Expenses increase €250 (Advertising Expense), so equity decreases €250. The advertising has already been used to help earn revenue, so it is an expense now, even though no cash has been paid. No asset changes.

0 = Accounts Payable +250 + Expenses −250 (Advertising Expense)

Equation check: Assets €17,800 (no change) = Liabilities €1,850 + Share Capital €15,000 + Revenues €1,200 − Expenses €250 = €17,800. Balanced.

Transaction 6 · Services performed for cash and credit

What happened

Softbyte performs €3,500 of app development services for customers. It receives €1,500 in cash and bills the €2,000 balance on account.

Analysis: Cash increases €1,500, Accounts Receivable increase €2,000, and Revenues increase €3,500. Revenue is the full €3,500, because all the work was done. It does not matter that part of the money will arrive later: the €2,000 becomes an asset, a right to receive cash.

Cash +1,500 + Accounts Receivable +2,000 = Revenues +3,500 (Service Revenue)

Equation check: Assets €21,300 (Cash 10,700 + A/R 2,000 + Supplies 1,600 + Equipment 7,000) = Liabilities €1,850 + Share Capital €15,000 + Revenues €4,700 − Expenses €250 = €21,300. Balanced.

Transaction 7 · Payment of expenses

What happened

Softbyte pays the following September expenses in cash: office rent €600, salaries and wages of employees €900, and utilities €200.

Analysis: Cash decreases €1,700 (600 + 900 + 200). Expenses increase: Rent Expense €600, Salaries and Wages Expense €900, Utilities Expense €200. Equity decreases €1,700. We keep the three expenses separate, because the income statement shows each type of expense.

Cash −1,700 = Expenses −600 (Rent) −900 (Salaries and Wages) −200 (Utilities)

Equation check: Assets €19,600 (Cash 9,000 + A/R 2,000 + Supplies 1,600 + Equipment 7,000) = Liabilities €1,850 + Share Capital €15,000 + Revenues €4,700 − Expenses €1,950 = €19,600. Balanced.

Transaction 8 · Payment of accounts payable

What happened

Softbyte pays its €250 Programming News bill in cash.

Analysis: Cash decreases €250 and Accounts Payable decrease €250. There is NO expense here: the advertising expense was already recorded in transaction 5, when the service was used. Today Softbyte only pays off a debt.

Cash −250 = Accounts Payable −250

Equation check: Assets €19,350 (Cash 8,750 + A/R 2,000 + Supplies 1,600 + Equipment 7,000) = Liabilities €1,600 + Share Capital €15,000 + Revenues €4,700 − Expenses €1,950 = €19,350. Balanced.

Transaction 9 · Receipt of cash on account

What happened

Softbyte receives €600 in cash from customers who were billed for services in transaction 6.

Analysis: Cash increases €600 and Accounts Receivable decrease €600. One asset replaces another, so total assets do not change. There is NO revenue here: it was already recorded in transaction 6, when the work was done.

What happened

The company pays a dividend of €1,300 in cash to Ray and Barbara Neal, its shareholders.

The financial statements

The table of transactions is useful for the accountant, but users want clear reports. From the tabular analysis, companies prepare financial statements (états financiers). The slides list five:

  1. Income statement (compte de résultat): revenues and expenses, and the resulting net income or net loss, for a period of time.
  2. Retained earnings statement (état des résultats non distribués): the changes in retained earnings for the same period.
  3. Statement of financial position (bilan / état de la situation financière): assets, liabilities and equity at a specific date.
  4. Statement of cash flows (tableau des flux de trésorerie): cash receipts and cash payments for a period of time.
  5. Comprehensive income statement (état du résultat global): other comprehensive income items, which are not part of net income (briefly explained below).

The order of preparation and how they link

The statements are prepared in a fixed order, because each one uses a number from the one before (Illustration 1-11):

  1. The income statement is prepared first. Its result, net income, goes into the retained earnings statement.
  2. The retained earnings statement gives ending retained earnings, which goes into the equity part of the statement of financial position.
  3. The statement of financial position is prepared next.
  4. The statement of cash flows uses information from the statement of financial position and the income statement. Its ending cash must equal the cash on the statement of financial position.

1. The income statement

The income statement reports the revenues and expenses, and the resulting net income or net loss, for a specific period of time (a month, a quarter, a year). Revenues are listed first, then expenses. It does NOT include investment and dividend transactions between the shareholders and the business, because these are not revenues or expenses.

4. The statement of cash flows

It reports the cash receipts and cash payments for a period. It answers three questions: Where did the cash come from? What was the cash used for? What was the change in the cash balance? Cash flows are grouped into three activities:

  • Operating activities: the day-to-day business: cash received from customers, cash paid for expenses.
  • Investing activities: investments made by the company, such as buying (or selling) equipment, land or buildings.
  • Financing activities: money from or to the owners and lenders: shares issued, dividends paid, loans received or repaid.

5. The comprehensive income statement (briefly)

Under IFRS, some gains and losses are not included in net income. They are called other comprehensive income (for example, certain changes in the value of some investments). They are reported either combined with the income statement (one statement of comprehensive income) or in a separate statement. You only need to know that it exists; the details come in later chapters.

A wise end · Not every year ends on 31 December

Companies may choose their year-end. Vodafone's year ends on 31 March, and Walt Disney's at the end of September. Many companies end their year when inventory and business activity are at their lowest, because it is easier to count and close the accounts.

The world needs one set of high-quality international standards because of:

  • Multinational corporations that operate in many countries;
  • Mergers and acquisitions across borders (Fiat/Chrysler, Vodafone/Mannesmann);
  • Information technology, which lets information travel instantly;
  • Financial markets that are global.

Transaction analysis is basically the same.

P12Calculation · Problem

On January 1, Medina Crafts SA had assets of 90,000 TND and liabilities of 40,000 TND. During the year, shareholders invested another 10,000 TND in exchange for ordinary shares, the company earned revenues of 70,000 TND, had expenses of 52,000 TND, and paid dividends of 6,000 TND. On December 31, its liabilities were 35,000 TND. Find: (a) equity on January 1; (b) net income for the year; (c) equity on December 31; (d) total assets on December 31.

Show the full solution

(a) Equity, January 1 = 90,000 − 40,000 = 50,000 TND.

(b) Net income = 70,000 − 52,000 = 18,000 TND.

(c) Equity, December 31 = opening equity + new share capital + net income − dividends = 50,000 + 10,000 + 18,000 − 6,000 = 72,000 TND.

(d) Total assets = liabilities + equity = 35,000 + 72,000 = 107,000 TND.

The idea: equity changes for only four reasons: shareholders' investments (+), revenues (+), expenses (−) and dividends (−).

Recap and key terms

What to remember

  • Accounting identifies, records and communicates economic events to users. Bookkeeping is only the recording part.
  • Internal users = managers (managerial accounting). External users = investors, creditors, tax authorities, regulators, customers, unions (financial accounting).
  • Ethics = standards of right or wrong, honest or dishonest, fair or not fair. Three steps: recognize → identify the elements and stakeholders → weigh the alternatives and choose the most ethical one.
  • IASB (London) issues IFRS (115+ countries); FASB issues U.S. GAAP; convergence reduces the differences.
  • Historical cost: assets at cost, at acquisition and later. Fair value: only for certain investment securities. Relevance (makes a difference) vs faithful representation (what really happened).
  • Monetary unit: only money data. Economic entity: the business is separate from its owner and from other entities.
  • Proprietorship (one owner, unlimited liability), partnership (2+ partners, generally unlimited liability), corporation (transferable shares, limited liability, separate legal entity).
  • Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always. Equity = Share Capital—Ordinary + Retained Earnings.
  • Revenues increase retained earnings; expenses and dividends decrease them. Dividends are NOT expenses. Net income = revenues − expenses.
  • A transaction changes assets, liabilities or equity, and has a dual effect. Paying a payable is not an expense; collecting a receivable is not revenue.
  • Statements: income statement → retained earnings statement → statement of financial position → statement of cash flows. The statement of financial position is at a date; the others cover a period.
  • Cash flows: operating (daily business), investing (the company buys equipment, land…), financing (shares issued, dividends, loans).
Top traps on the test
  • Subtracting dividends when computing net income.
  • Recording revenue when cash is collected from a customer who already owed it.
  • Recording an expense when an account payable is paid.
  • Thinking the cost of an asset is raised when its market value goes up.
  • Calling regulatory or taxing authorities "internal users".
  • Classifying shareholders' investments as "investing" activities (they are financing).
  • Forgetting the sign in change questions (a decrease is negative): −50,000 = −90,000 + change in equity, so equity +40,000.
  • Saying that buying an asset (such as a machine) on credit on the last day affects the income statement. It affects only the statement of financial position, because there is no revenue or expense. (Careful: an expense bought on credit, such as an advertising or electricity bill, does go on the income statement.)

Glossary (English → French)

Accounting
(comptabilité) An information system that identifies, records and communicates the economic events of an organization to interested users.
Bookkeeping
(tenue des comptes) The recording of economic events; only one part of accounting.
Financial statements
(états financiers) The accounting reports given to users: income statement, retained earnings statement, statement of financial position, statement of cash flows (plus the comprehensive income statement).
Internal users
(utilisateurs internes) Managers who plan, organize and run the business.
External users
(utilisateurs externes) People and organizations outside the company: investors, creditors, tax authorities, regulators, customers, unions.
Managerial accounting
(comptabilité de gestion) Accounting that gives internal reports to managers.
Financial accounting
(comptabilité financière) Accounting that answers the questions of external users.
Investors
(investisseurs) Owners or future owners; they decide to buy, hold or sell shares.
Creditors
(créanciers) Suppliers and lenders to whom the business owes money.
Ethics
(éthique) Standards of conduct by which actions are judged right or wrong, honest or dishonest, fair or not fair.
Stakeholders
(parties prenantes) Persons or groups who may be harmed or benefited by a decision.
GAAP
(principes comptables généralement admis) Generally Accepted Accounting Principles: a common set of standards for reporting economic events.
IASB / IFRS
International Accounting Standards Board (London), which issues International Financial Reporting Standards (normes IFRS), used in more than 115 countries.
FASB / U.S. GAAP
Financial Accounting Standards Board, which issues the U.S. accounting standards.
Convergence
(convergence) Efforts to reduce the differences between IFRS and U.S. GAAP.
Relevance
(pertinence) Information that would make a difference in a business decision.
Faithful representation
(représentation fidèle) Information that shows what really happened: factual, complete, neutral, free from error.
Historical cost principle
(principe du coût historique) Companies record assets at their cost, at acquisition and over the time they hold them.
Fair value principle
(principe de la juste valeur) Assets and liabilities reported at the price received to sell them or paid to settle them; used for certain investment securities.
Monetary unit assumption
(hypothèse de l'unité monétaire) Only transaction data that can be expressed in money is recorded.
Economic entity assumption
(hypothèse de l'entité économique) The entity's activities are kept separate from those of its owner and of other entities.
Proprietorship
(entreprise individuelle) A business owned by one person, with unlimited personal liability.
Partnership
(société de personnes) A business owned by two or more partners, generally with unlimited liability; governed by a partnership agreement.
Corporation
(société de capitaux, for example an SA) A separate legal entity whose ownership is divided into transferable shares; shareholders have limited liability.
Shareholders
(actionnaires) The owners of a corporation.
Accounting equation
(équation comptable) Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
Assets
(actif) Resources a business owns or controls that provide future services or benefits.
Liabilities
(passif / dettes) Claims against assets: existing debts and obligations.
Equity
(capitaux propres) The ownership claim on total assets: Assets − Liabilities (residual equity).
Share capital—ordinary
(capital social) The amount shareholders invested in exchange for ordinary shares.
Retained earnings
(résultats non distribués / réserves) Net income kept in the business: total net income minus total dividends.
Revenues
(produits / chiffre d'affaires) Gross increases in equity from business activities entered into to earn income.
Expenses
(charges) The cost of assets consumed or services used to earn revenue; decreases in equity.
Dividends
(dividendes) A distribution of cash or other assets to shareholders; reduces retained earnings but is NOT an expense.
Net income / net loss
(résultat net: bénéfice / perte) Revenues − expenses; a loss when expenses are greater than revenues.
Accounts receivable
(créances clients) Amounts customers owe the business (an asset).
Accounts payable
(dettes fournisseurs) Amounts the business owes suppliers (a liability).
Notes payable
(effets à payer / emprunts) Written promises to repay, for example a bank loan (a liability).
Transaction
(opération) An economic event recorded by accountants because it changes the financial position; external or internal.
Expanded accounting equation
Assets = Liabilities + Share Capital—Ordinary + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends.
Income statement
(compte de résultat) Revenues, expenses and net income or loss for a period.
Retained earnings statement
(état des résultats non distribués) Changes in retained earnings for a period.
Statement of financial position
(bilan) Assets, liabilities and equity at a specific date.
Statement of cash flows
(tableau des flux de trésorerie) Cash receipts and payments for a period, in operating, investing and financing activities.
Comprehensive income statement
(état du résultat global) Reports other comprehensive income items that are not part of net income.
Auditing
(audit) Examining financial statements and giving an opinion on them; a service of public accountants.
Forensic accounting
(comptabilité judiciaire / juricomptabilité) Accounting and investigation skills used to investigate fraud.
Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX)
A U.S. law requiring strong internal controls for large public companies listed on U.S. exchanges.
