Debits and credits are the alphabet of accounting, so we practise them again and again until they become automatic.

Remember from Ch 1

Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Equity = Share Capital—Ordinary + Retained Earnings. Retained earnings go up with revenues and go down with expenses and dividends. Every transaction has a dual effect (at least two items change), and the equation always stays in balance. In Chapter 2 we record exactly the same kind of transactions, but with a faster and safer tool: accounts with debits and credits.

Think back to the Softbyte table in Chapter 1. It had one column for Cash, one for Accounts Receivable, one for Supplies, and so on. Each column was really a small record of one item: every increase and every decrease of that item. Accountants call such a record an account.

Why do we need it? Imagine a supermarket in Tunis. It has hundreds of sales, purchases and payments every day. Nobody can rewrite the whole equation after each one. Instead, the accountant keeps a separate account for each item. At any moment, the manager can look at one account (for example Cash) and see its balance.

DefinitionAccount (compte)

An individual accounting record of the increases and decreases in one specific asset, liability, equity, revenue or expense item. Examples: Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Accounts Payable, Share Capital—Ordinary, Service Revenue, Rent Expense.

A company has a separate account for every specific item, not one big account for "assets" or for "equity". For example, equity alone uses several accounts: Share Capital—Ordinary, Retained Earnings, Dividends, and every revenue and expense account (because revenues and expenses change equity). A small company may have 30 accounts; a large one may have thousands.

The T-account

The simplest form of an account looks like the capital letter T, so we call it a T-account (compte en T). It has three parts:

  1. the title of the account (its name), on top;
  2. a left side, called the debit side;
  3. a right side, called the credit side.
DefinitionDebit (débit) and credit (crédit)

Debit (abbreviation Dr.) simply means left. Credit (abbreviation Cr.) simply means right. To debit an account = to enter an amount on its left side. To credit an account = to enter an amount on its right side.

Debit = left. Credit = right. Nothing more. They do NOT mean "good" or "bad", and they do NOT always mean "increase" or "decrease".

Whether a debit increases or decreases an account depends on the type of account. For Cash, a debit is an increase. For Accounts Payable, a debit is a decrease. You will learn these rules in section 02.

The balance of an account

After several amounts are entered, we want to know what is left in the account: its balance (solde).

DefinitionAccount balance (solde du compte)

The difference between the total debits and the total credits of an account. If debits are bigger, the account has a debit balance (solde débiteur). If credits are bigger, it has a credit balance (solde créditeur). The balance is written on the side of the bigger total.

Method in 4 steps
  1. Add all the amounts on the debit (left) side.
  2. Add all the amounts on the credit (right) side.
  3. Subtract the smaller total from the bigger total.
  4. Write the result on the side of the bigger total: that is the balance.

Common mistakes
  • Adding the two sides together (18,000 + 3,000 = 21,000). The balance is a difference, not a sum.
  • Writing the balance on the wrong side. It always goes on the side with the bigger total.
  • Thinking "debit = bad". Your bank uses these words from its own point of view. When the bank writes "we credited your account with 500 TND", it means the bank owes you more (your deposit is a liability for the bank). In your company's books, the cash received is a debit to Cash.

02

Debits and credits: the rules for every account

In Chapter 1 you learned that every transaction has a dual effect. The recording system uses this idea. It is called the double-entry system, and every company in the world uses it.

DefinitionDouble-entry system (comptabilité en partie double)

A system in which each transaction affects at least two accounts, so that the accounting equation stays in balance. Each transaction is recorded with at least one debit and at least one credit, and the total of the debits must equal the total of the credits.

In every transaction: total debits = total credits. Always.

This rule is very useful. It gives an automatic check: if the debits and credits of a transaction are not equal, you know at once that something is wrong. And because every transaction is balanced, the sum of all the debits in all the accounts equals the sum of all the credits. (This is the idea behind the trial balance in section 09.)

Now, the key question: for each type of account, is an increase a debit or a credit? The answer comes from the position of the account in the accounting equation.

1. Assets and liabilities

Assets are on the left side of the equation, so they increase on the left side of the account: debit to increase, credit to decrease. Liabilities are on the right side of the equation, so they increase on the right side: credit to increase, debit to decrease. The two rules are mirror images of each other.

Assets (Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Equipment…)DebitCreditDebit

DefinitionNormal balance (solde normal)

The side (debit or credit) where an account usually has its balance. It is always the increase side: increases are normally bigger than decreases. Assets have a normal debit balance; liabilities have a normal credit balance.

A balance on the "wrong" side is a warning. A credit balance in Cash means the company paid out more than it had: its bank account is overdrawn. A debit balance in Accounts Payable means the company paid a supplier more than it owed. Usually, an abnormal balance means an error.

2. Equity: share capital and retained earnings

Equity is on the right side of the equation, like liabilities. So equity accounts follow the same rule as liabilities: credit to increase, debit to decrease.

  • Share Capital—Ordinary (capital social): the company issues ordinary shares to shareholders in exchange for cash or other assets. When it issues shares, Share Capital—Ordinary is credited. Normal balance: credit.
  • Retained Earnings (résultats non distribués / réserves): the net income kept in the company (not paid out as dividends). It increases with a credit. Normal balance: credit.

3. Dividends

A dividend (dividende) is a distribution of cash (or other assets) by the company to its shareholders. Dividends reduce retained earnings, so they reduce equity. Accountants record them in a separate Dividends account, which increases with a debit. Normal balance: debit. (Why a separate account? So that the company can see the total dividends of the period for the retained earnings statement.)

4. Revenues and expenses

Revenues (produits) increase equity. So revenue accounts behave like equity: credit to increase, debit to decrease. Normal balance: credit.

Expenses (charges) decrease equity. So they have the opposite rule: debit to increase, credit to decrease. Normal balance: debit.

Read this twice, because it surprises many students: when Rent Expense goes up, we debit it. The debit to the expense is what lowers equity. Why not simply debit Retained Earnings directly? Because the company wants to see each type of revenue and expense separately, to prepare the income statement.

Memory trick · DEALER

Read the word DEALER in two halves:

  • D · E · A = Dividends, Expenses, Assets → increase with a DEBIT (normal debit balance).
  • L · E · R = Liabilities, Equity (share capital, retained earnings), Revenues → increase with a CREDIT (normal credit balance).

To decrease an account, use the other side. That is all you need.

Second trick · Start with Cash

Most transactions involve cash. Find the cash side first: cash comes in → debit Cash; cash goes out → credit Cash. Then the other account must take the opposite side, because debits must equal credits. Example: you pay rent in cash → credit Cash, so debit Rent Expense.

Common mistakes
  • Crediting Cash when cash comes in. Cash is an asset: cash received = debit Cash.
  • Debiting a revenue "because money came in". The money is the debit to Cash; the revenue is the credit.
  • Treating dividends as an expense. Both have a debit balance, but dividends are not a cost of earning revenue. They are a separate account.
  • Mixing up Supplies (asset) and Supplies Expense (expense). Supplies bought for later use are an asset.
  • Thinking Unearned Service Revenue is a revenue. Despite its name, it is a liability (the company still owes a service). Normal balance: credit.

Equity relationships and the expanded equation

Equity is where most students get confused, because it is made of several accounts that move in different directions. Let us put them in order.

On the statement of financial position (bilan), equity has two parts: Share Capital—Ordinary (what shareholders invested) and Retained Earnings (profits kept in the company). During the period, the company does not change Retained Earnings directly every day. Instead, it uses three separate "helper" accounts: Revenues, Expenses and Dividends. At the end of the period, their effect is added to Retained Earnings.

DefinitionEquity relationships (Illustration 2-11)

Equity = Share Capital—Ordinary + Retained Earnings. Retained Earnings are increased by revenues and decreased by expenses and dividends. Revenues − Expenses = Net income (or net loss).

Share Capital—Ordinary+ (investment by shareholders)CreditCreditStatement of financial position
Retained Earnings+ (profits kept)CreditCreditRetained earnings statement and statement of financial position
Dividends− (paid out to shareholders)DebitDebitRetained earnings statement
Revenues+ (earned from customers)CreditCreditIncome statement

How the pieces connect: revenues and expenses go to the income statement and give net income. Net income and dividends go to the retained earnings statement and give the ending retained earnings. Share capital and ending retained earnings go to the statement of financial position.

The expanded equation, with debits and credits

Assets = Liabilities + Share Capital—Ordinary + Retained Earnings − Dividends + Revenues − Expenses

Look at the signs. Every account with a + on the right side of the equation increases with a credit. Every account with a − on the right side (Dividends, Expenses) works the other way: it increases with a debit, exactly like assets on the left. This is why the system always stays balanced: each entry has equal debits and credits, so the two sides of the equation move by the same amount.

Example · Why the equation stays balanced

Café Medina pays 600 TND of electricity. Entry: debit Utilities Expense 600, credit Cash 600.

  • Left side: Cash (asset) is credited, so assets go down 600.
  • Right side: Utilities Expense goes up 600. Expenses have a minus sign in the equation, so equity goes down 600.
  • Both sides fall by 600. The equation is still balanced.

Now the café earns 1,000 TND cash from customers. Entry: debit Cash 1,000, credit Service Revenue 1,000. Assets go up 1,000; revenues (with a plus sign) go up, so equity goes up 1,000. Balanced again.

Investor insight · Keeping score

The Brothers Elephants, a professional baseball team in Taiwan, keep accounts like any company. Their main revenues are admissions (ticket sales), concessions (food and drinks sold in the stadium), television and radio rights, and advertising. Their main expenses are players' salaries, administrative salaries, travel and stadium maintenance.

Would Manchester United have the same accounts? Mostly yes: ticket sales, broadcasting rights, sponsorship and advertising, and merchandise on the revenue side; players' and staff salaries, travel and stadium costs on the expense side. A Tunisian club such as Étoile Sportive du Sahel would use similar accounts too. The account names change from business to business, but the debit and credit rules never change.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking "Dividends is equity, so it has a credit balance". No: Dividends reduce equity, so their normal balance is a debit.
  • Thinking "expenses decrease equity, so we credit them". No: an expense increases with a debit; that debit is what reduces equity.
  • Putting dividends on the income statement. Dividends are not an expense; they go on the retained earnings statement.

The steps in the recording process

Every company, small or large, records its transactions with the same basic steps. It does not matter if the work is done on paper or with accounting software: the logic is identical.

It all starts with a business document (pièce justificative): a sales receipt (ticket de caisse), a cheque (chèque), a bill or invoice (facture), a bank statement. The document is the evidence that the transaction really happened, and it gives the date and the amount. No document, no entry.

DefinitionThe three steps of the recording process
  1. Analyze each transaction: which accounts change, does each one go up or down, debit or credit, and by how much?
  2. Enter (journalize) the transaction in a journal.
  3. Transfer (post) the journal information to the correct accounts in the ledger.
Analyze → Journalize → Post. The trial balance comes after the recording process; it is not one of its steps.

In a computerized system, the software does steps 2 and 3 very fast: you type the entry once, and it is posted at once. But the software cannot do step 1 for you. If the analysis is wrong (wrong account, wrong side, wrong amount), the computer posts the error perfectly. So you must understand the analysis.

The journal: the book of original entry

Transactions are not written directly into the accounts. They are first written in a journal, one after the other, in date order. Think of it as the company's diary of money events.

DefinitionJournal (journal)

The book of original entry: the first place where a transaction is recorded, in chronological order (date order). Every company has at least the most basic form, the general journal (journal général).

Why the journal helps

  1. It shows in one place the complete effect of a transaction (all the accounts debited and credited together).
  2. It gives a chronological record of all transactions: you can find what happened on any date.
  3. It helps to prevent and locate errors, because for each entry you can easily compare the debit and credit amounts.
DefinitionJournalizing (passer une écriture au journal)

Entering transaction data in the journal. Each transaction written in the journal is a journal entry (écriture comptable).

  2. The debit account title comes first, at the far left of the titles column.
  3. The credit account title goes on the next line, indented (moved to the right).
  4. The debit amount goes in the Debit column; the credit amount in the Credit column.
  5. A brief explanation goes on the line below the entry.
  6. Skip a line between two entries (the empty row in the table).
  7. Leave the Ref. column blank for now. It is filled in later, when the entry is posted (section 07).
  8. Use the exact account titles of the company's list of accounts. If the account is "Cash", do not write "Money" or "Bank" in another entry.
  9. No currency sign (no €, no TND, no ₺) in the journal: only numbers.
Memory trick · credit moves right

Credit means right. So in the journal the credit title also moves a little to the right (it is indented), and its amount sits in the column on the far right. The debit stays on the left: first line, far left title, left amount column.

Simple and compound entries

DefinitionSimple entry (écriture simple) and compound entry (écriture composée)

A simple entry involves only two accounts: one debit and one credit. A compound entry involves three or more accounts. In a compound entry, the total of the debit amounts must still equal the total of the credit amounts. All the debits are listed first, then all the credits.

When there is NO entry

Only transactions are journalized: events that change assets, liabilities or equity. Many business events are not transactions:

  • hiring an employee who will start work later (no work done, no salary owed yet);
  • signing a contract for work to be done next month, with no money received;
  • ordering supplies that have not yet been received or paid;
  • interviewing job applicants, or a customer asking for a price.
Test question to ask yourself

  • Writing currency signs in the journal.

The ledger and the chart of accounts

The journal lists transactions by date. That is useful, but it does not answer a simple question like "How much cash do we have today?" To answer it, you would have to read the whole journal and add every cash amount. So the information is also organized by account, in the ledger.

DefinitionLedger (grand livre)

The entire group of accounts maintained by a company. The general ledger (grand livre général) contains all the asset, liability and equity accounts (share capital, retained earnings, dividends), and all the revenue and expense accounts.

Picture it

The journal is like your phone's message history: everything in the order it happened. The ledger is like your contacts list: one page per person (here, one page per account), with everything about that account in one place. Same information, organized two different ways.

Why it helps: the ledger shows the balance of every account. A manager can see at once how much cash is available, how much customers owe (Accounts Receivable), and how much the company owes its suppliers (Accounts Payable).

The accounts are kept in a fixed order: first the statement of financial position accounts (assets, then liabilities, then equity), then the income statement accounts (revenues, then expenses).

Accounting across the organization · What would Sam do?

When Sam Walton opened his first stores (the beginning of Walmart), his "accounting system" was very simple. Each store had a pigeonhole on the office wall for its cash receipts and papers, and its own blue binder that served as its ledger. Once a month, Sam and his bookkeeper sat down, entered the month's merchandise, sales and cash for each store, and balanced the books by hand.

The idea has not changed: one record per account, updated regularly, then balanced. Today the company has thousands of stores and the work is done by computers, but the logic is the same as Sam's blue binders.

The standard form of account (three-column form)

The T-account is perfect for learning, but real ledgers use a more detailed format: the three-column form of account (compte à solde progressif). It has three money columns: Debit, Credit and Balance. After every entry, the new balance is computed. This is called a running balance (solde progressif).

Posting: from the journal to the ledger

After an entry is in the journal, each of its amounts must be copied into the right ledger account. This is the third step of the recording process.

DefinitionPosting (report au grand livre)

The procedure of transferring journal entries to the ledger accounts. Every debit in the journal becomes a debit in an account; every credit becomes a credit in an account. Posting always comes after journalizing.

The four steps of posting (Illustration 2-18)

  1. In the ledger, in the account debited, enter the date, the journal page (for example J1) and the debit amount shown in the journal.
  2. In the Ref. column of the journal, write the account number to which the debit amount was posted.
  3. In the ledger, in the account credited, enter the date, the journal page and the credit amount.
  4. In the Ref. column of the journal, write the account number to which the credit amount was posted.

Cross-referencing

After posting, the Ref. columns point to each other. This is called cross-referencing (référence croisée):

  • In the journal, the Ref. column shows the account number (101, 311). It proves that the line has been posted, and tells you where.
  • In the ledger, the Ref. column shows the journal page (J1). It tells you where the amount came from.

So you can follow any amount in both directions. And if a journal line has an empty Ref. column, you know it has not been posted yet. This is very useful when posting is interrupted (a phone call, the end of the day).

The trial balance

At the end of the month, after all the entries are posted, the accountant wants to check the ledger. Are the total debits still equal to the total credits? The tool for this check is the trial balance.

DefinitionTrial balance (balance de vérification)

A list of accounts and their balances at a given time, usually at the end of the month. Accounts are listed in ledger order. Debit balances go in the left (debit) column; credit balances go in the right (credit) column.

Why prepare a trial balance?

  1. It proves the mathematical equality of debits and credits after posting.
  2. It helps to uncover errors in journalizing and posting.
  3. It is useful for preparing the financial statements: all the balances are in one list.

How to prepare it: three steps

  1. List the account titles and their balances, in the order of the ledger. (Accounts with a zero balance can be left out.)
  2. Total the debit column and the credit column.
  3. Prove that the two columns are equal.

Currency signs and underlining

  • Currency signs (€, ₺, TND, $) do not appear in the journal or the ledger. They are used only in the trial balance and the financial statements.
  • Even there, the sign is shown only for the first item in each column and for the total. Look at the Yazici trial balance: ₺ appears on Cash, on Notes Payable and on the totals.
  • A single line is drawn under a column of numbers to be added or subtracted. Totals are double-underlined. (In the tables of this page, the thick line above the totals plays the role of the single line; on paper, draw a double line under the totals.)

Limitations: what a trial balance does NOT prove

A balanced trial balance only proves that total debits = total credits. It does not prove that every transaction was recorded, or that the ledger is correct. The trial balance can balance even when these five errors are made:

ErrorExample (Yazici)Why it still balances
1. A transaction is not journalizedThe ₺900 rent payment is forgotten completely.No debit and no credit are recorded. Rent Expense is 900 too low and Cash is 900 too high: the columns stay 28,700 = 28,700.
2. A correct journal entry is not postedThe supplies entry (₺2,500) is in the journal but never posted.Both the debit and the credit are missing: 26,200 = 26,200.
3. A journal entry is posted twiceThe supplies entry is posted two times.An equal extra debit and extra credit: 31,200 = 31,200.
4. Wrong accounts are used in journalizing or postingCopa's ₺10,000 is credited to Unearned Service Revenue instead of Service Revenue.The amount and the side are right; only the account is wrong: 28,700 = 28,700.
5. Offsetting errors in the amountThe ₺500 dividend is recorded as a ₺50 debit and a ₺50 credit.The debit and the credit are wrong by the same amount, so they cancel out.
A trial balance that balances is necessary, but it does not prove that the accounts are correct.

Locating errors when it does not balance

If the two totals are different, there is at least one error. Work backwards through the process:

  1. Re-add the two columns of the trial balance.
  2. Check that each balance was copied correctly from the ledger: right amount, right column.
  3. Recompute the balance of each ledger account.
  4. Check the postings from the journal to the ledger.
  5. Check the journal entries themselves.
Three clues from the difference
  • Difference = one amount in the ledger → maybe a balance was left out of the trial balance, or only one side of an entry was posted. Example: Prepaid Insurance (₺600) forgotten → debits 28,100, credits 28,700, difference 600.
  • Difference divisible by 2 → maybe an amount was put on the wrong side. The amount is half the difference. Example: Rent Expense ₺900 written in the credit column → debits 27,800, credits 29,600, difference 1,800; 1,800 ÷ 2 = 900. Look for a 900.
  • Difference divisible by 9 → maybe a transposition (two digits swapped) or a slide (zeros or the decimal point moved). Examples: Cash ₺15,200 copied as ₺12,500 → difference 2,700 = 9 × 300. Supplies ₺2,500 copied as ₺250 → difference 2,250 = 9 × 250.
Investor insight · Why accuracy matters

Germany's finance minister, Wolfgang Schäuble, once had to explain a €55.5 billion error in the accounts of Hypo Real Estate Holding, a German bank taken over by the state after the financial crisis. He blamed "statistical and communication problems" and called it an annoying mistake. It was not small: about 2.6% of Germany's GDP, and it had made the country's federal debt look €55.5 billion bigger than it really was.

The lesson: even very large organizations make recording errors, and some errors are huge. Careful checking at every step (and tools like the trial balance) matter.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking a balanced trial balance proves that everything is correct.
  • Putting Dividends or an expense in the credit column (they have debit balances).
  • Putting Unearned Service Revenue in the debit column (it is a liability: credit).
  • Listing accounts in random order instead of ledger order.
  • Writing currency signs on every line.
Exam alert

"A trial balance will NOT balance if…" → look for the option where the debit amount and the credit amount are different.

A look at U.S. GAAP

This short section compares the recording process under IFRS (your textbook) and under U.S. GAAP. Good news: the recording process is almost identical everywhere. Double-entry accounting is the basis of accounting systems all over the world.

Similar under IFRS and GAAPDifferent
Transactions are recorded with the same double-entry system, the same debit and credit rules, journals and ledgers.Vocabulary. U.S. companies say "stockholders' equity" or "shareholders' equity", "Common Stock" (= Share Capital—Ordinary) and "balance sheet" (= statement of financial position).
The trial balance has the same format and the same purpose.Measurement. IFRS companies rely less on historical cost and use more fair value measurements than GAAP companies.
Currency signs are used only in the trial balance and the financial statements.
Both the IASB and the FASB go beyond the textbook's basic definitions of the elements (assets, liabilities, equity, revenues, expenses).

Before allowing U.S. companies to use IFRS, the U.S. regulator (the SEC) listed several questions to study: Are IFRS developed and consistent enough? Is the IASB independent? Are IFRS written for the benefit of investors? How will investors be educated about IFRS? What is the effect on U.S. laws and on companies (their systems, contracts, governance and litigation)? How will accountants be trained?

All these questions are new. Do them like a real test: about 75 minutes (20 questions in 25 minutes, then the two problems), no notes, a calculator and paper. Then check your score in the top bar, and reread the sections where you made mistakes.

T1Debit or credit?

The company pays for a one-year insurance policy in advance. To record the increase in Prepaid Insurance, you…

Prepaid Insurance is an asset (a future benefit), so it increases with a debit.
T2Debit or credit?

The company finally performs a service that a customer had paid for in advance. To record the decrease in Unearned Service Revenue, you…

Unearned Service Revenue is a liability. Liabilities increase with credits, so they decrease with debits. (You will see this entry in Chapter 3: debit Unearned Service Revenue, credit Service Revenue.)
T3Debit or credit?

To record an increase in Retained Earnings, you…

Retained Earnings is equity: credit to increase.
T4Debit or credit?

The company sells an old machine. To record the decrease in Equipment, you…

Equipment is an asset: assets increase with debits and decrease with credits.
T5Debit or credit?

The normal balance of Accounts Payable is a…

A liability: normal credit balance (L in L · E · R).
T6Which entry is correct?

A customer pays 1,300 TND that it owed for repairs done last month. The correct entry is:

The revenue was already recorded last month (when the work was done and the customer was billed). Today, one asset replaces another: Cash up (debit), Accounts Receivable down (credit). Recording revenue again (a) would count it twice.
T7Which entry is correct?

The company pays 2,000 TND to a supplier for supplies bought on account last week. The correct entry is:

The supplies were recorded last week (debit Supplies, credit Accounts Payable). Today the company only pays its debt: Accounts Payable down (debit), Cash down (credit). Option (a) would record the supplies a second time.
T8Which entry is correct?

The company declares and pays a cash dividend of €1,000 to its shareholders. The correct entry is:

Dividends increase with a debit; cash goes out (credit). Dividends are not an expense, and paying a dividend does not reduce share capital.
T9Find the error

A customer pays 500 TND today for a repair that will be done next week. A student writes: debit Cash 500, credit Service Revenue 500. What is wrong?

Cash really came in, so there is an entry (c is wrong) and Cash is debited (b is wrong). But the revenue is not earned yet: the company owes the repair → credit the liability Unearned Service Revenue.
T10Find the error

A company buys ink and paper for the next three months, on account, for 800 TND. The bookkeeper writes: debit Supplies Expense 800, credit Accounts Payable 800. What is wrong?

Supplies bought for later use are an asset: debit Supplies. The credit to Accounts Payable is correct, because nothing was paid yet. (Same idea as Sheet 8.)
T11Find the error

A delivery van costs 30,000 TND. The company pays 10,000 TND in cash and signs a note payable for the rest. The bookkeeper writes: debit Equipment 30,000; credit Cash 10,000; credit Notes Payable 30,000. What is wrong?

Debits 30,000 but credits 10,000 + 30,000 = 40,000: unbalanced. The note covers only the unpaid part: 30,000 − 10,000 = 20,000. Compound entries are perfectly allowed.
T12Multiple choice

Which account has a normal credit balance?

Unearned Service Revenue is a liability: credit balance. Prepaid Insurance (asset), Dividends and Rent Expense all have debit balances (D · E · A).
T13Calculation

The Supplies T-account shows debits of 600, 1,400 and 300, and a credit of 900. Its balance is:

Debits 600 + 1,400 + 300 = 2,300; credits 900; 2,300 − 900 = 1,400 debit.
T14Find the error

A trial balance shows total debits of 41,600 TND and total credits of 42,500 TND. Which single error could cause this?

Difference = 900, credits bigger. 900 ÷ 2 = 450: a 450 amount on the wrong side. A debit posted as a credit makes debits 450 smaller and credits 450 bigger → credits higher by 900. (d) would make debits higher. (b) and (c) do not unbalance anything.
T15Will it balance?

Equipment bought on account for 2,000 TND was recorded as: debit Equipment 2,000, credit Cash 2,000. Will the trial balance balance?

The debit equals the credit; only the credited account is wrong (Cash instead of Accounts Payable). The trial balance balances, but Cash is 2,000 too low and Accounts Payable 2,000 too low.
T16Will it balance?

For a 750 TND entry, the debit was posted but the credit was never posted. Will the trial balance balance?

Only one side reached the ledger, so debits are 750 bigger than credits. Clue: the difference (750) equals one single amount.
T17Multiple choice

Which order is correct?

The three recording steps (analyze, journalize, post), then the trial balance to check the ledger.
T18Compound entry

A café in Nabeul pays 1,250 TND in cash: 900 TND for this month's rent and 350 TND for electricity. The correct entry is:

Two expenses increase (two debits, listed first); cash goes out (one credit). 900 + 350 = 1,250. (a) reverses every side; (b) mixes two expenses into one; (c) would mean the bills are not paid yet.
T19Calculation

A company's accounts have these normal balances (TND): Cash 8,200; Accounts Receivable 1,900; Supplies 650; Equipment 14,000; Accounts Payable 2,300; Unearned Service Revenue 1,000; Share Capital—Ordinary 18,000; Dividends 400; Service Revenue 6,850; Rent Expense 1,800; Salaries and Wages Expense 1,200. The total of the debit column is:

Debits: 8,200 + 1,900 + 650 + 14,000 + 400 + 1,800 + 1,200 = 28,150. Credits: 2,300 + 1,000 + 18,000 + 6,850 = 28,150. Traps: 27,750 forgets Dividends; 29,150 puts Unearned Service Revenue in the debit column; 56,300 adds everything.
T20True or false

If the trial balance balances, the accounts are certainly correct.

False. Five kinds of errors leave the trial balance balanced: a transaction not journalized, an entry not posted, an entry posted twice, wrong accounts, offsetting errors.

Recap and key terms

What to remember

  • An account records the increases and decreases of one item. A T-account has three parts: title, left side (debit), right side (credit). The balance = bigger side − smaller side, written on the bigger side.
  • Debit = left, credit = right. Double-entry: every transaction has at least one debit and one credit, and total debits = total credits.
  • DEALER: Dividends, Expenses, Assets increase with a debit (normal debit balance); Liabilities, Equity, Revenues increase with a credit (normal credit balance). The normal balance is always the increase side.
  • Expanded equation: Assets = Liabilities + Share Capital + Retained Earnings − Dividends + Revenues − Expenses.
  • Recording process: analyze → journalize → post, starting from a business document. The trial balance comes after.
  • The journal is the book of original entry, in date order. Debit first, credit indented, explanation below, Ref. blank until posting. Simple entry = 2 accounts; compound entry = 3 or more. No entry when nothing is exchanged (hiring, ordering, interviewing).
  • The ledger is the whole group of accounts; the three-column form shows a running balance. The chart of accounts lists the accounts and their numbers (100s assets, 200s liabilities, 300s equity, 400s revenues, 600s+ expenses).
  • Posting transfers journal amounts to the ledger. Cross-reference: account number in the journal's Ref. column, journal page (J1) in the ledger's Ref. column.
  • The trial balance lists all balances at a date and proves that debits = credits. It does not prove the books are correct (missing entry, entry not posted, entry posted twice, wrong account, offsetting errors).
  • Error clues: difference ÷ 2 → an amount on the wrong side; difference ÷ 9 → transposition or slide.
  • Currency signs only in the trial balance and statements (first item of each column and totals); totals double-underlined.
  • Prepaid (you paid in advance) = asset, debit. Unearned (the customer paid you in advance) = liability, credit.
Top traps on the test
  • Crediting Cash when cash is received (it is a debit).
  • Thinking expenses are credited because they "reduce" something. An expense increases with a debit.
  • Putting Dividends in the credit column, or treating them as an expense.
  • Recording a customer's advance as Service Revenue instead of Unearned Service Revenue.
  • Debiting Supplies Expense for supplies bought for later use (debit Supplies).
  • Recording revenue again when a customer pays an old receivable, or an expense again when a payable is paid.
  • Making an entry for hiring an employee.
  • Saying the trial balance is part of the recording process, or that a balanced trial balance proves everything is correct.
  • "NOT / FALSE" questions: read the stem twice before answering.

Glossary (English → French)

Account
(compte) An individual accounting record of increases and decreases in a specific asset, liability, equity, revenue or expense item.
T-account
(compte en T) The simplest form of an account: title, debit (left) side, credit (right) side.
Debit (Dr.)
(débit) The left side of an account; to debit = to enter an amount on the left side.
Credit (Cr.)
(crédit) The right side of an account; to credit = to enter an amount on the right side.
Account balance
(solde) Total debits − total credits: a debit balance (solde débiteur) or a credit balance (solde créditeur).
Normal balance
(solde normal) The side where an account normally has its balance: its increase side.
Double-entry system
(comptabilité en partie double) Each transaction affects at least two accounts, with equal debits and credits.
Business document
(pièce justificative) Evidence of a transaction: sales receipt, cheque, bill or invoice.
Journal
(journal) The book of original entry: transactions recorded in chronological order. The basic form is the general journal (journal général).
Journalizing
(passer une écriture au journal) Entering transaction data in the journal.
Journal entry
(écriture comptable) One transaction as written in the journal.
Simple entry
(écriture simple) A journal entry with only two accounts.
Compound entry
(écriture composée) A journal entry with three or more accounts; total debits still equal total credits.
Ledger
(grand livre) The entire group of accounts maintained by a company. The general ledger (grand livre général) contains all of them.
Three-column form of account
(compte à solde progressif) A ledger account with Debit, Credit and Balance columns.
Running balance
(solde progressif) The balance computed again after every entry.
Chart of accounts
(plan comptable) The list of a company's accounts and account numbers.
Posting
(report au grand livre) Transferring journal entries to the ledger accounts.
Cross-reference
(référence croisée) The Ref. columns linking journal and ledger: account number in the journal, journal page in the ledger.
Trial balance
(balance de vérification) A list of accounts and their balances at a given time, proving that total debits = total credits.
Transposition error
(inversion de chiffres) Two digits swapped, e.g. 1,250 written 1,520. The difference is divisible by 9.
Slide error
(erreur de virgule ou de zéros) The number moves by a place, e.g. 2,500 written 250. The difference is divisible by 9.
Prepaid Insurance / prepaid expense
(charges constatées d'avance) A cost paid in advance for future benefits; an asset.
Unearned Service Revenue
(produits constatés d'avance) Cash received before the service is performed; a liability.
Notes Payable
(effets à payer) A written promise to pay, often with interest; a liability.
Accounts Payable
(dettes fournisseurs) Amounts owed to suppliers; a liability.
Accounts Receivable
(créances clients) Amounts customers owe the company; an asset.
Supplies
(fournitures) Items bought for use in future periods; an asset until used.
Share Capital—Ordinary
(capital social) Amount invested by shareholders in exchange for ordinary shares; equity, credit balance.
Retained Earnings
(résultats non distribués / réserves) Net income kept in the company; equity, credit balance.
Dividends
(dividendes) Distributions to shareholders; reduce retained earnings; debit balance; not an expense.
Revenues
(produits) Increases in equity from the company's business activities; credit balance.
Expenses
(charges) Costs of assets consumed or services used to earn revenue; decrease equity; debit balance.
