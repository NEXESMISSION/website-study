A company lives for many years. Its owners, its bank and the tax office cannot wait until the company closes to learn whether it made a profit. They need reports regularly: every year, and often every quarter or every month. So accountants cut the life of the business into short periods.

DefinitionTime period assumption (hypothèse de périodicité)

Accountants divide the economic life of a business into artificial time periods, usually a month, a quarter (three months) or a year, and prepare reports for each period. It is also called the periodicity assumption.

Why "artificial"? Because the business does not stop on 31 October. Work continues on 1 November. The end of a period is a line that we draw on the calendar, so that we can measure the results of each piece of time.

Fiscal year, calendar year, interim periods

DefinitionFiscal year (exercice comptable) and calendar year (année civile)

A fiscal year is an accounting period that is one year long. It can start on any date. A calendar year is the special case of a fiscal year that runs from 1 January to 31 December.

DefinitionInterim periods (périodes intermédiaires)

Accounting periods shorter than one year: monthly and quarterly periods. Most large companies prepare both quarterly and annual financial statements.

Why this creates the main problem of the chapter

Many transactions affect more than one period. Look at Yazici:

  • On October 4 it paid ₺600 for insurance that protects it for 12 months.
  • On October 1 it bought equipment that it will use for many years.
  • On October 2, R. Knox paid ₺1,200 for work that Yazici will do in October, November and December.

To measure October's profit correctly, the accountant must decide how much of each amount belongs to October and how much belongs to later months. The tools for this are accrual-basis accounting (section 02) and adjusting entries (sections 03–10).

Cutting time into periods creates the key question of this chapter: in which period does each revenue and each expense belong?

PART A · 02

Accrual-basis vs cash-basis accounting

There are two possible ways to decide when a revenue or an expense "happens". The first looks at the event (the work done, the resource used). The second looks only at the cash. IFRS uses the first one.

DefinitionAccrual-basis accounting (comptabilité d'engagement)

Transactions that change a company's financial statements are recorded in the periods in which the events occur, even if cash was not exchanged. Revenues are recognized when services are performed (not when cash is received). Expenses are recognized when they are incurred (not when they are paid).

DefinitionCash-basis accounting (comptabilité de caisse)

Revenues are recorded only when cash is received, and expenses only when cash is paid. It is simple, but it can give a very wrong picture of a period. Cash-basis accounting is not in accordance with IFRS.

"To recognize" (comptabiliser) a revenue or an expense means to record it in the accounts and report it in the income statement of a period. Two principles tell us when to recognize.

The revenue recognition principle

DefinitionRevenue recognition principle (principe de comptabilisation des produits)

Recognize revenue in the accounting period in which the performance obligation is satisfied.

A performance obligation (obligation de prestation) is the company's promise to give a good or a service to a customer. It is satisfied when the company has done what it promised. Only then is the revenue earned.

Examples
  • A dry cleaner in Ariana cleans a suit on 30 June. The customer pays when she collects it on 3 July. The service was performed in June → June revenue.
  • Yazici received ₺1,200 from R. Knox on October 2 for work to be done by December 31. On October 2 nothing was done yet → no revenue on October 2 (a liability). Revenue is recognized month by month as the work is done.
  • Copa Company paid Yazici ₺10,000 on October 31 for work done in October → October revenue (here the cash and the work happen in the same month).

The expense recognition principle

DefinitionExpense recognition principle (principe de rattachement des charges aux produits)

Match expenses with revenues in the period when the company makes efforts to generate those revenues. In short: "let the expenses follow the revenues." It is also called the matching principle.

The work of Yazici's employees on October 29, 30 and 31 helped to produce October's revenue. So their salary for those days is an October expense, even though the cash is paid on November 9. The effort (expense) goes in the same period as the result (revenue).

Time period assumption + Revenue recognition principle + Expense recognition principle → Accrual-basis accounting

This is Illustration 3-1 of the slides: once time is cut into periods, the two recognition principles tell you in which period each revenue and each expense belongs. The result is accrual-basis accounting.

Ethics insight · Cooking the books?

Some companies have been accused of breaking the revenue recognition principle to look better. Krispy Kreme allegedly doubled its doughnut shipments to wholesale customers on the last day of a quarter, so that the quarter's sales looked higher. The customers could return the unsold doughnuts the next quarter for a refund. Computer Associates was accused of backdating sales: recording sales in an earlier quarter than the one in which they really happened.

In both cases, revenue was put in a period in which the performance obligation was not really satisfied. The result: the profit of one quarter looked bigger, and the next quarter paid the price.

Common mistakes
  • Recording revenue when cash comes in, even though the work is not done yet. An advance is a liability.
  • Recording an expense only when it is paid. A salary earned this month is this month's expense, even if paid next month.
  • Thinking accrual accounting ignores cash. Cash is still recorded when it moves; accrual accounting only decides in which period the revenue or expense goes.

PART A · 03

Why we need adjusting entries

On October 31, Yazici's trial balance balances: ₺28,700 = ₺28,700. Can the accountant now prepare the financial statements? Not yet. A balanced trial balance only proves that debits equal credits. Some balances are out of date or incomplete.

DefinitionAdjusting entries (écritures de régularisation)

Journal entries made at the end of an accounting period to make sure that the revenue recognition and expense recognition principles are followed. They bring the balances of revenues, expenses, assets and liabilities up to date before the financial statements are prepared.

Why the trial balance is not up to date

  1. Some events are not recorded every day, because it would not be efficient. Example: nobody writes a journal entry each time an employee uses a sheet of paper (use of supplies), or each day an employee works (salaries earned).
  2. Some costs expire with the passage of time, not through a daily transaction. Examples: insurance that expires day by day, rent, and the depreciation of equipment.
  3. Some items are simply unrecorded at the end of the period. Example: the bill for the electricity used in October arrives only in November.

Adjusting entries are needed every time financial statements are prepared. Yazici prepares monthly statements, so it adjusts its accounts at the end of every month.

Golden rules: every adjusting entry involves ONE income statement account and ONE statement of financial position account. An adjusting entry NEVER involves Cash.

Why never Cash? Because cash is recorded at the moment it moves (receipts, payments, bank statements), so the Cash balance is already correct. Adjusting entries deal only with the timing difference between the cash and the revenue or expense: either the cash moved before (deferrals) or it will move later (accruals). If an entry contains Cash, it is a normal transaction, not an adjusting entry.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking that a balanced trial balance means the accounts are ready for the statements.
  • Putting Cash in an adjusting entry ("Dr Supplies Expense, Cr Cash"). Cash already moved earlier or will move later.
  • Thinking adjusting entries are made only once a year. They are made every time statements are prepared (monthly, quarterly or yearly).
  • Thinking adjusting entries "correct errors". They are not corrections: they record events that are not yet in the accounts.

PART A · 04

The four types of adjusting entries

All adjusting entries fall into two families, and each family has an expense version and a revenue version. So there are exactly four types (Illustration 3-2).

DefinitionDeferrals (charges et produits constatés d'avance)

The cash moves first; the expense or revenue is recognized later.

  1. Prepaid expenses (charges constatées d'avance): expenses paid in cash before they are used or consumed.
  2. Unearned revenues (produits constatés d'avance): cash received before services are performed.
DefinitionAccruals (produits à recevoir et charges à payer)

The expense or revenue happens first; the cash moves later.

  1. Accrued revenues (produits à recevoir): revenues for services performed but not yet received in cash or recorded.
  2. Accrued expenses (charges à payer): expenses incurred but not yet paid in cash or recorded.

Now look at the same four types on a time line. The red dashed line is October 31, the date of the statements. Above each line: when the cash moves. Below: when the expense or revenue is recognized.

Memory tricks
  • To defer = to postpone. In a deferral the cash came first and the recognition is postponed. The account already exists (Supplies, Prepaid Insurance, Unearned Service Revenue): the adjusting entry reduces it.
  • To accrue = to build up. In an accrual the revenue or expense builds up before any cash moves (often before any invoice, too). Nothing is recorded yet: the adjusting entry creates a receivable or a payable.
  • The expense side always takes a debit (expense up); the revenue side always takes a credit (revenue up). Only the statement of financial position account changes from type to type.

Adjusting deferrals (1): prepaid expenses

We start with the deferrals. In a deferral, the cash already moved, and the amount is waiting in an asset (a prepaid expense) or in a liability (an unearned revenue). At the end of the period, the accountant moves the part that is used up (or earned) to the income statement.

DefinitionPrepaid expenses (charges constatées d'avance)

Payments of expenses that will benefit more than one accounting period. The cash is paid before the expense is recorded, so the payment is first recorded as an asset. Common examples: insurance, supplies, advertising, rent, and buildings and equipment. Prepaid expenses expire either with the passage of time (rent, insurance) or through use (supplies).

3. Depreciation

Buildings, equipment and vehicles are recorded as assets at their cost when they are bought. They give service for many years. Each period uses up a part of that service, so a part of their cost must become an expense of that period. This is a long-term version of a prepaid expense.

DefinitionDepreciation (amortissement)

The process of allocating the cost of an asset to expense over its useful life (durée d'utilité). Depreciation is a cost allocation method. It is NOT an attempt to report the real change in the value of the asset.

Yazici estimates the depreciation of its ₺5,000 equipment at ₺480 a year, which is 480 ÷ 12 = ₺40 per month.

Here is something new. We do not credit the Equipment account directly. We credit a special account, Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment. Why? So that the Equipment account keeps showing the original cost (₺5,000), and the second account shows the total depreciation to date.

DefinitionAccumulated depreciation (amortissements cumulés)

A contra asset account (compte correcteur d'actif): an account that offsets (reduces) an asset account. It has a normal credit balance and is shown in the statement of financial position just after the asset it relates to. It collects all the depreciation recorded since the asset was bought.

Helpful hint · contra accounts

All contra accounts have increases, decreases and normal balances opposite to the account they relate to. Equipment (asset): increases with a debit, normal debit balance. Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment (contra asset): increases with a credit, normal credit balance.

Adjusting accruals (2): accrued expenses

DefinitionAccrued expenses (charges à payer)

Expenses incurred but not yet paid or recorded at the statement date. Common examples: interest, taxes, salaries, and utility bills that arrive after the period.

Until the adjustment, the expense is understated and the liability is understated (Illustration 3-16). An accrued expense of one company is often an accrued revenue of another: the interest Yazici owes is interest that its lender has earned.

Accrued expense adjustment: DEBIT an expense (increase) · CREDIT a liability (increase)

1. Accrued interest

When a company borrows money with a note, interest builds up every day, but it is usually paid only at the end (when the note matures). At the end of each month, the interest of the month must be recorded.

Interest = Face value of the note × Annual interest rate × Time in terms of one year

The face value (valeur nominale) is the amount written on the note. The rate is always an annual rate, so the time must be expressed as a fraction of a year: one month = 1/12, four months = 4/12 (Illustration 3-17).

Appendix 3B: financial reporting concepts

Why do accountants follow all these rules? The IASB's Conceptual Framework (cadre conceptuel) explains it. It describes the qualities that make accounting information useful, and the assumptions, principles and one constraint behind every rule you learn. You met some of them in Chapter 1; here is the complete picture.

1. Qualities of useful information

QualityMeaning
Fundamental qualities
Relevance (pertinence)The information makes a difference in a decision. It has predictive value (it helps to predict the future) and/or confirmatory value (it confirms or corrects past expectations).
 Materiality (importance relative)A company-specific part of relevance. An item is material when its size is likely to influence the decision of an investor or a creditor. Immaterial items can be handled in the simplest way.
Faithful representation (représentation fidèle)The information shows what really happened. It is complete (nothing important is missing), neutral (not biased in favour of anyone) and free from error.
Enhancing qualities
Comparability (comparabilité)Different companies use the same accounting principles, so users can compare them. Consistency (permanence des méthodes) = a company uses the same principles and methods from year to year.
Verifiability (vérifiabilité)Independent observers using the same methods would obtain similar results.
Timeliness (rapidité)The information is available to decision-makers before it loses its capacity to influence decisions.
Understandability (compréhensibilité)The information is presented clearly and concisely, so that users with reasonable knowledge can understand it.

2. Assumptions

AssumptionMeaning
Monetary unit (unité monétaire)Only things that can be expressed in money are recorded.
Economic entity (entité économique)The activities of the business are kept separate from those of its owners and of other businesses.
Time period (périodicité)The life of the business can be divided into artificial periods (section 01).
Going concern (continuité d'exploitation)The business will remain in operation for the foreseeable future. This is why assets are kept at cost and depreciated over their useful lives: if the company were closing, they would be shown at what they could be sold for.

3. Principles and the cost constraint

PrincipleMeaning
Measurement: historical cost (coût historique)Assets are recorded at the price paid to acquire them.
Measurement: fair value (juste valeur)Some assets and liabilities are reported at the price they would sell for in the market today.
Revenue recognitionRecognize revenue when the performance obligation is satisfied (section 02).
Expense recognitionMatch expenses with the revenues they help to produce (section 02).
Full disclosure (information complète)Disclose all circumstances and events that would make a difference to users, in the statements or in the notes.
Cost constraint (contrainte du coût)Standard-setters weigh the cost that companies bear to provide information against the benefit users get from it. Information that costs more than it is worth is not required.
Examples
  • Materiality. A waste-paper basket costing 25 TND will last five years, but a company with sales of 50 million TND simply records it as an expense. Depreciating it would change no decision.
  • Going concern. Yazici depreciates its equipment over its useful life because it expects to keep operating. If it were about to close next month, the equipment would be reported at its selling price.
  • Full disclosure. A big lawsuit against the company is not yet an amount in the accounts, but it must be explained in the notes.

PART B · 14

The income statement: usefulness, limitations and quality of earnings

Part B begins

Part A taught you how to get every number right at the end of a period. Part B comes from the professor's lecture notes "The Income Statement" (based on Kieso, Intermediate Accounting, IFRS edition, chapter 4). It shows how IFRS companies present their revenues and expenses, with much more structure than Yazici's simple statement. The Chapter 3 quizzes test this part too. Note: the Tunisian companies used as examples in Parts B and C (Médina Digital, Carthage Home Appliances, and the others) are invented for practice, and the 20% income tax rate they use is a simplifying assumption chosen to keep the numbers easy, not a rule.

DefinitionIncome statement (compte de résultat; in Tunisia: état de résultat)

The report that measures the success of a company's operations for a period of time. It reports revenues, expenses, gains and losses, and the resulting net income (or net loss). It is also called the statement of income or statement of earnings.

What the income statement is useful for

  1. Evaluating past performance. Investors and creditors compare revenues and expenses with earlier years and with competitors. Did sales grow? Did costs grow faster than sales?
  2. Predicting future performance. Past results are the best starting point to estimate next year's results.
  3. Assessing the risk or uncertainty of future cash flows. By showing the different parts of income separately (regular operations, unusual gains, discontinued activities), the statement helps users see which parts are likely to continue.
Example · using the trend

Médina Digital SA (a web agency in Tunis that you will meet in section 17) had revenue of 750,000 TND and operating expenses of 680,000 TND last year. This year: revenue 900,000 TND, expenses 740,000 TND. Operating income went from 70,000 TND to 160,000 TND: it more than doubled, because revenue grew faster than costs. An investor uses this kind of trend to predict next year and to judge the risk.

Its limitations

  1. Items that cannot be measured reliably are not reported. Example: the value of a brand that a company built itself over the years, or the skills of its staff, never appear as income.
  2. Income numbers are affected by the accounting methods used. Example: two companies with identical machines may choose different depreciation methods and so report different net incomes for the same reality.
  3. Income measurement involves judgment. Many amounts are estimates: the useful life of equipment, the part of a project already performed, the receivables that will never be collected. Yazici estimated that ₺400 of Knox's work was done and that its equipment depreciates ₺480 a year: other accountants could estimate differently.

Quality of earnings and earnings management

Because many amounts depend on estimates and timing, managers can influence the reported income. They have strong incentives to do so: to meet earnings targets (the forecasts of analysts, the level that triggers their bonus) and to make earnings look less risky (smooth from year to year).

DefinitionEarnings management (gestion des résultats)

The planned timing of revenues, expenses, gains and losses to smooth out bumps in earnings. Often it raises this year's income at the expense of future years (for example by recognizing sales too early), or it lowers income in a very good year to "save" profit for later (for example by recording too much expense now).

DefinitionQuality of earnings (qualité des résultats)

Earnings are of high quality when they give a reliable basis for predicting future earnings and cash flows. The quality of earnings is reduced when earnings management makes the information less useful for that purpose.

Example · why managers like smooth profits
Profit (thousand TND)Year 1Year 2Year 3Year 4Total

Both earn 400 in total, but investors see Company A as much less risky. A manager of B might be tempted to move profit between years: delay a repair expense, record a December sale that really happens in January, or pick a longer useful life to lower depreciation. The real business does not change, only the reported timing. That is earnings management, and it lowers the quality of earnings. Adjusting entries are where it often happens, because they rely on estimates.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking net income is an exact, objective fact. It depends on methods and estimates.
  • Thinking every form of earnings management is illegal. Some of it stays inside the rules but still makes the numbers less useful; when it breaks the rules (like the Krispy Kreme case in section 02), it becomes fraud.

The elements of the income statement

Under IFRS, the income statement is built from two broad elements: income and expenses. Each of them has two parts.

DefinitionIncome (produits, au sens large)

Increases in economic benefits during the period, in the form of inflows or enhancements of assets, or decreases of liabilities, that increase equity, other than contributions from shareholders. Income includes both revenues and gains.

DefinitionExpenses (charges, au sens large)

Decreases in economic benefits during the period, in the form of outflows or depletions of assets, or incurrences of liabilities, that decrease equity, other than distributions to shareholders. Expenses include both expenses (in the narrow sense) and losses.

Income = Revenues + Gains · Expenses = Expenses + Losses · Net income = Income − Expenses
ElementWhere it comes fromExamples
Revenues (produits des activités ordinaires)The company's ordinary activitiesSales, fees, interest, dividends, rent
Gains (profits)May or may not come from ordinary activitiesGain on the sale of long-term assets; unrealized gain on trading securities
Expenses (charges)The company's ordinary activitiesCost of goods sold, depreciation, interest, rent, salaries and wages, taxes
Losses (pertes)May or may not come from ordinary activitiesLoss on the sale of long-term assets; unrealized loss on trading securities

Two details matter in exercises:

  • Gains and losses are reported as a net amount (the price received minus the book value), while revenues and expenses are reported in full.
  • Shareholders' contributions are not income, and dividends are not expenses: both are transactions with the owners, excluded by the definitions above.
Worked example · A bakery in Sousse

The bakery sells bread and cakes for 420,000 TND in the year: that is revenue (its ordinary activity), reported in full. It also sells its old delivery van. The van cost 30,000 TND and has accumulated depreciation of 22,000 TND, so its book value is 8,000 TND.

  • If the van is sold for 9,500 TND: gain on sale = 9,500 − 8,000 = 1,500 TND. The income statement shows 1,500 TND, not 9,500 TND.
  • If the van is sold for 7,200 TND: loss on sale = 8,000 − 7,200 = 800 TND.

Selling vans is not the bakery's business, so the result is a gain or a loss, not revenue. You will see in section 16 that gains and losses go in "other income and expense".

PART B · 16

The intermediate components: a multi-step income statement

Yazici listed all its revenues, then all its expenses, then net income. Larger IFRS companies present their income statement in steps, with several intermediate subtotals. Each subtotal answers a question that investors ask. The components always come in this order:

  1. Sales or revenue section (chiffre d'affaires): sales, minus discounts, allowances and returns → net sales.
  2. Cost of goods sold section (coût des marchandises vendues) → subtotal gross profit (marge brute).
  3. Selling expenses (frais de commercialisation): expenses of the efforts to make sales (sales staff, advertising, delivery…).
  4. Administrative or general expenses (charges administratives): expenses of general administration (office staff, office building…).
  5. Other income and expense (autres produits et charges): most other items, such as rent revenue, gains and losses on the sale of long-term assets, write-downs, restructuring costs → subtotal income from operations (résultat d'exploitation).
  6. Financing costs (charges financières): mainly interest expense → subtotal income before income tax (résultat avant impôt).
  7. Income tax (impôt sur le résultat) → subtotal income from continuing operations (résultat des activités poursuivies).
  8. Discontinued operations (activités abandonnées): results of a major part of the business that was sold or closed, net of tax → net income (résultat net).
  9. Non-controlling interest (participations ne donnant pas le contrôle): the part of net income that belongs to the minority owners of subsidiaries, if there are any.
  10. Earnings per share (résultat par action), section 18.
Gross profit = Net sales − Cost of goods sold
Income from operations = Gross profit − Selling expenses − Administrative expenses ± Other income and expense
Income before income tax = Income from operations − Financing costs
Income from continuing operations = Income before income tax − Income tax
Net income = Income from continuing operations ± Discontinued operations (net of tax)

Why each subtotal matters

  • Gross profit shows how much the company earns on the goods it sells, before its operating costs. It is useful to evaluate performance and to predict future earnings. Many analysts look at the gross margin = gross profit ÷ net sales.
  • Income from operations highlights the company's regular business activities, which helps to predict the amount, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows. The IASB asks companies to include "other income and expense" in income from operations.
  • Financing costs must be reported on the face of the income statement. Keeping them below income from operations separates how the business performs from how it is financed.
  • Net income is the income after all revenues and expenses. Many people see it as the most important measure of a company's success.

Some items are big or unusual enough to be shown separately (on the face of the statement or in the notes): write-downs of inventories or of property, plant and equipment (and their reversals); restructurings (and reversals); gains or losses on the disposal of property, plant and equipment or of investments; litigation settlements; other reversals of liabilities. In practice, many companies publish a condensed income statement and give the details in supporting schedules in the notes.

PART B · 17

Classifying expenses: by nature or by function

IFRS lets a company group its operating expenses in one of two ways. The total is the same; only the grouping changes.

DefinitionClassification by nature (par nature)

Expenses are grouped by what they are: cost of materials used, direct labor incurred, delivery expense, advertising expense, employee benefits, depreciation expense, amortization expense.

DefinitionClassification by function (par fonction, or par destination)

Expenses are grouped by the activity they serve: cost of goods sold (or cost of services), selling expenses, administrative expenses.

The function method is the one most often used in practice. It gives more insight (for example, it shows gross profit), but it requires allocating each cost to a function, which involves judgment. The nature method is simpler and needs no allocation. A company that uses the function method must also give information by nature in the notes (at least depreciation, amortization and employee benefits).

PART B · 18

Earnings per share

Investors who own a few shares want to know how much profit belongs to each share. That is the job of earnings per share, probably the most quoted number in the financial press.

DefinitionEarnings per share, EPS (résultat par action)

The net income earned by each ordinary share during the period. It must be disclosed on the face of the income statement.

EPS = (Net income − Preference dividends) ÷ Weighted-average number of ordinary shares outstanding
  • Why subtract preference dividends? Preference shares (actions de préférence) receive their dividend before ordinary shareholders. That part of net income is not available to the ordinary shareholders. Ordinary dividends are not subtracted: EPS measures what the ordinary shareholders earned, whether or not it is paid out.
  • Why a weighted average? If new shares are issued during the year, they were outstanding (and used to earn income) only for part of the year. The weighted-average number of shares (nombre moyen pondéré d'actions) counts each share for the fraction of the year it was outstanding.
Example · Lancer, Inc. (lecture notes)

PART B · 19

When a company sells or closes a major part of its business, the results of that part will not continue next year. To help users predict the future, IFRS requires these results to be shown separately, below income from continuing operations.

DefinitionDiscontinued operation (activité abandonnée)

A component of an entity (a part whose operations and cash flows can be clearly separated from the rest) that has been disposed of or is classified as held-for-sale, and that:

  1. represents a major line of business or a major geographical area of operations, or
  2. is part of a single co-ordinated plan to dispose of a major line of business or geographical area, or
  3. is a subsidiary acquired exclusively to be resold.

DefinitionIntraperiod tax allocation (répartition de l'impôt entre les composantes du résultat)

Relating the income tax expense of the period to the items that cause it: income from continuing operations and discontinued operations. The rule in four words: "let the tax follow the income." A gain carries its own tax; a loss carries its own tax saving.

A loss reduces the company's taxable income, so it creates a tax saving of 30% × €100,000 = €30,000. The loss "costs" the company only €70,000 after tax.

Why bother? Look at case 2 without allocation. The total tax would be 30% × (250,000 − 100,000) = €45,000. If all of it were put next to continuing operations, "income from continuing operations" would look like 250,000 − 45,000 = €205,000 instead of €175,000: the tax saving created by the discontinued loss would wrongly flatter the continuing business. Common mistakes

  • Calling the sale of one machine or one shop a "discontinued operation".
  • Forgetting that a loss has a tax saving (it reduces tax), while a gain has a tax cost.
  • Forgetting the per-share amounts for the discontinued operations.

The statement of financial position: usefulness, limitations and elements

Part C begins

This part comes from the professor's lecture notes "The Statement of Financial Position" (based on Kieso, Intermediate Accounting, IFRS edition, chapter 5). Yazici's statement listed its assets, equity and liabilities in one block. Real companies classify every item into groups. That is what you learn here.

DefinitionStatement of financial position (bilan / état de la situation financière)

The statement that reports the assets, liabilities and equity of a business at a specific date. It is also called the balance sheet. It gives information about the company's resources (assets), its obligations to creditors (liabilities) and the owners' equity in the net resources. It helps users predict the amounts, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows.

What it is useful for

Users rely on it to compute rates of return (for example, net income compared with total assets), to evaluate the capital structure (how much of the assets is financed by debt and how much by equity), and to assess the company's risk and future cash flows. In particular, it helps to judge three things:

DefinitionLiquidity, solvency and financial flexibility
  • Liquidity (liquidité): the time expected to pass until an asset is turned into cash, or until a liability has to be paid. The more liquid a company, the less risk it fails to pay its short-term bills.
  • Solvency (solvabilité): the ability of a company to pay its debts as they mature (come due), including long-term debts.
  • Financial flexibility (flexibilité financière): the ability of a company to take effective actions to change the amounts and timing of its cash flows, so that it can respond to unexpected needs and opportunities.
Examples
  • Liquidity. Company X has current assets of 120,000 TND and current liabilities of 60,000 TND: it could pay its short-term debts twice. Company Y has current assets of 50,000 TND and current liabilities of 80,000 TND: it may struggle to pay next month's bills.
  • Solvency. Yazici's liabilities are ₺9,550 for total assets of ₺21,910: about 44% of its assets are financed by debt. The higher this share, the higher the risk that the company cannot pay its debts as they mature.
  • Financial flexibility. A company with little debt and an unused bank credit line can quickly borrow to buy a competitor, or to survive a bad year. A company already loaded with debt cannot.

Its limitations

  1. Most assets and liabilities are reported at historical cost. Land bought in 2005 may still be shown at its 2005 price, far below its value today. The amount is reliable but may be less relevant.
  2. Judgments and estimates are used. How many receivables will be collected? How long will the equipment be used? How much will warranties cost?
  3. Many items of financial value are omitted. The know-how of the employees, the company's reputation, or a brand it built itself are worth a lot but do not appear.

The three elements

DefinitionAsset (actif), liability (passif) and equity (capitaux propres)
  • Asset: a resource controlled by the entity, as a result of past events, from which future economic benefits are expected to flow to the entity.
  • Liability: a present obligation of the entity arising from past events, whose settlement is expected to result in an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits.
  • Equity: the residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities (Equity = Assets − Liabilities).

Classification in the statement of financial position

A classified statement of financial position groups similar items together, with subtotals. Under IFRS, the main groups are below (Illustration 5.1). IFRS does not fix the order; many IFRS companies start with non-current assets, and many others start with current assets (section 22).

Current or non-current? The key test

DefinitionCurrent assets (actifs courants)

Cash and other assets expected to be converted into cash, sold or consumed within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. All other assets are non-current assets (actifs non courants).

The operating cycle (cycle d'exploitation) is the average time between paying cash for inventory and collecting cash from the customers who buy it. For most companies it is shorter than a year, so the one-year rule applies. For some it is longer: the cheese or wine of a producer may age for two or three years, and it is still a current asset, because it is sold within the normal operating cycle.

1. Current assets

ItemWhat it is and what must be disclosed
Inventories (stocks)Goods held for sale (or materials to produce them). Disclose the basis of valuation (for example, lower of cost and net realizable value) and the cost flow assumption (for example, FIFO or average cost).
Receivables (créances)Amounts owed to the company: accounts receivable from customers, notes receivable, loans to employees… Identify the anticipated uncollectibles (the part that will probably never be collected) and the non-trade receivables (those not from sales).
Prepaid expenses (charges constatées d'avance)Insurance, supplies, advertising, rent, taxes paid in advance: cash paid before the expense is recorded (section 05).
Short-term investments (placements à court terme)Investments the company expects to sell soon, such as trading shares.
Cash (trésorerie)Currency and demand deposits (bank accounts). Usually reported as cash and cash equivalents. Cash equivalents (équivalents de trésorerie) are short-term, highly liquid investments that mature within three months or less. Any restrictions on cash (for example, cash blocked as a guarantee) must be disclosed.

2. Non-current assets

GroupWhat it contains
Long-term investments (placements à long terme)(1) Securities held for the long term: bonds, ordinary shares of other companies, long-term notes. (2) Tangible assets not used in operations, such as land held for speculation.
Property, plant and equipment (immobilisations corporelles)Tangible, long-lived assets used in the regular operations: land, buildings, machinery, furniture, tools, and wasting resources such as mineral deposits. All of them except land are depreciated (or depleted, for minerals).
Intangible assets (immobilisations incorporelles)Assets that lack physical substance and are not financial instruments: patents, copyrights, franchises, goodwill, trademarks, trade names, customer lists. Intangibles with a limited life are amortized (the word used instead of "depreciated").
Other assetsItems that fit nowhere else: long-term prepaid expenses, non-current receivables, property held for sale.

3. Equity

  • Share capital (capital social): the par or stated value of the shares issued, ordinary shares and preference shares.
  • Share premium (prime d'émission): the amount paid by shareholders in excess of the par value.
  • Retained earnings (résultats non distribués, réserves): the company's undistributed earnings.

4. Non-current liabilities

Obligations that are not expected to be settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. They come from three sources:

  1. Specific financing: bonds payable, long-term notes payable, long-term bank loans.
  2. Ordinary operations: pension obligations, deferred tax liabilities.
  3. Future events: provisions (provisions), such as the estimated cost of warranties on products already sold.

5. Current liabilities

Obligations expected to be settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer:

  1. Payables from buying goods and services: accounts payable, wages payable, taxes payable.
  2. Collections received in advance: unearned revenues.
  3. Other liabilities due within the year or cycle: the current portion of long-term debt, short-term notes, interest payable.
Example · the current portion of long-term debt

Carthage Home Appliances owes 300,000 TND on a bank loan, repaid 60,000 TND each year. On December 31, 2026, the 60,000 TND due in 2027 is a current liability ("current portion of bank loan"); the remaining 240,000 TND is a non-current liability. One loan, two lines.

Common mistakes
  • Classifying Accumulated Depreciation as a liability. It is a contra asset, subtracted inside property, plant and equipment.
  • Classifying Unearned Revenue as revenue or equity. It is a (usually current) liability.
  • Depreciating land. Land is property, plant and equipment, but it is never depreciated.
  • Mixing land used in operations (property, plant and equipment) with land held for speculation (long-term investment).
  • Putting the whole of a long-term loan in non-current liabilities: the part due within a year is current.
  • Calling every bank deposit a "cash equivalent": only investments maturing within three months or less qualify.
Exam alert

Classification questions are very common: "Which of the following is NOT a current asset?" (look for a patent, land, or a long-term investment); "Share premium is part of…" (equity); "Cash equivalents mature within…" (three months or less). Learn the five groups of current assets and the four groups of non-current assets by heart.

Recap and key terms

What to remember

  • Time period assumption: the life of a business is cut into artificial periods (month, quarter, year). Fiscal year = any one-year period; calendar year = 1 January–31 December; interim periods = months and quarters.
  • Accrual basis (IFRS): record events in the period they occur. Cash basis: only when cash moves; not IFRS. Revenue recognition: when the performance obligation is satisfied. Expense recognition: let the expenses follow the revenues.
  • Adjusting entries are made every time statements are prepared. Each one has one income statement account and one statement of financial position account, and never Cash.
  • Four types: prepaid expense (Dr Expense, Cr Asset); unearned revenue (Dr Liability, Cr Revenue); accrued revenue (Dr Asset, Cr Revenue); accrued expense (Dr Expense, Cr Liability). Deferrals reduce an existing balance; accruals create a new receivable or payable.
  • Depreciation allocates cost (it is not a valuation): Dr Depreciation Expense, Cr Accumulated Depreciation (a contra asset, credit balance). Book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.
  • Interest = face value × annual rate × time in years. Accrued salaries = unpaid days × daily rate; on payday, debit the payable for the old days and the expense for the new days.
  • Forgotten adjustment: a missing expense makes net income too high; a missing revenue makes it too low. Prepaid → assets too high; unearned → liabilities too high; accrued revenue → assets too low; accrued expense → liabilities too low.
  • Yazici: unadjusted trial balance ₺28,700 → 7 adjustments (₺3,440) → adjusted trial balance ₺30,190 → net income ₺2,860 → retained earnings ₺2,360 → total assets ₺21,910.
  • Appendix 3A: if a prepayment was put in an expense (or an advance in a revenue), adjust for the unused / unearned part. Same final balances.
  • Appendix 3B: relevance (with materiality) and faithful representation; comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability; monetary unit, economic entity, time period, going concern; historical cost, fair value, revenue and expense recognition, full disclosure; cost constraint.
  • Income statement: useful to evaluate past and predict future performance and to assess risk; limited by unmeasurable items, method choices and judgment. Earnings management lowers the quality of earnings. Income = revenues + gains; expenses = expenses + losses.
  • Order: sales → COGS → gross profit → selling, administrative, other income and expense → income from operations → financing costs → income before income tax → tax → income from continuing operations → discontinued operations (net of tax) → net income → non-controlling interest → EPS.
  • Expenses by nature (depreciation, employee benefits, materials…) or by function (COGS, selling, administrative); same total, only function shows gross profit; function users disclose nature in the notes.
  • EPS = (net income − preference dividends) ÷ weighted-average ordinary shares. Discontinued operations = a major line of business or area, shown net of tax after continuing operations. Intraperiod tax allocation: let the tax follow the income.
  • Statement of financial position: helps assess liquidity, solvency and financial flexibility; limited by historical cost, estimates and omitted items. Current = within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer; cash equivalents mature within 3 months. Non-current assets: long-term investments, PP&E, intangibles, other. Equity: share capital, share premium, retained earnings. Account form or report form; IFRS fixes no order.
Top traps on the quizzes
  • Putting Cash in an adjusting entry.
  • Using the supplies on hand instead of the supplies used as the expense.
  • Crediting Equipment instead of Accumulated Depreciation, or calling Accumulated Depreciation a liability.
  • Mixing up unearned revenue (a liability, cash received first) and accrued revenue (an asset, cash later).
  • Counting months wrongly in interest and insurance (September 1 to December 31 = 4 months; June 1 to December 31 = 7 months).
  • Recording the full payroll as expense on payday after an accrual (double counting).
  • Subtracting dividends in the income statement; forgetting preference dividends in EPS.
  • Taxing discontinued operations twice (they are already net of tax), or calling the sale of one machine a discontinued operation.
  • Interest expense in operating expenses (it is a financing cost); a gain on sale in revenue (it is other income).
  • "NOT / FALSE / EXCEPT / incorrect" questions: read the stem twice before answering.

Glossary (English → French)

Time period assumption
(hypothèse de périodicité) The economic life of a business can be divided into artificial time periods.
Fiscal year
(exercice comptable) An accounting period one year long, starting on any date.
Calendar year
(année civile) The fiscal year from January 1 to December 31.
Interim periods
(périodes intermédiaires) Monthly or quarterly accounting periods.
Accrual-basis accounting
(comptabilité d'engagement) Recording transactions in the periods in which the events occur, even without cash.
Cash-basis accounting
(comptabilité de caisse) Recording revenue when cash is received and expense when cash is paid; not IFRS.
Revenue recognition principle
(principe de comptabilisation des produits) Recognize revenue when the performance obligation (obligation de prestation) is satisfied.
Expense recognition principle
(principe de rattachement des charges aux produits) Match expenses with the revenues they help to produce.
Adjusting entries
(écritures de régularisation) End-of-period entries that make revenues and expenses follow the recognition principles.
Prepaid expenses
(charges constatées d'avance) Expenses paid in cash before they are used; an asset until used.
Unearned revenues
(produits constatés d'avance) Cash received before services are performed; a liability.
Accrued revenues
(produits à recevoir) Revenues earned but not yet received in cash or recorded.
Accrued expenses
(charges à payer) Expenses incurred but not yet paid or recorded.
Depreciation
(amortissement) Allocating the cost of an asset to expense over its useful life (durée d'utilité).
Accumulated depreciation
(amortissements cumulés) Contra asset account (compte correcteur d'actif) holding all depreciation to date; credit balance.
Book value
(valeur nette comptable) Cost minus accumulated depreciation.
Face value
(valeur nominale) The amount written on a note; the basis of the interest calculation.
Adjusted trial balance
(balance après régularisation) Trial balance after all adjusting entries; the primary basis for the statements.
Materiality
(importance relative) An item is material if its size is likely to influence a decision.
Going concern
(continuité d'exploitation) The business will remain in operation for the foreseeable future.
Full disclosure
(information complète) Disclose all circumstances and events that would make a difference to users.
Cost constraint
(contrainte du coût) The cost of providing information is weighed against its benefit.
Income statement
(compte de résultat; in Tunisia, état de résultat) Revenues, expenses, gains and losses for a period, and net income.
Earnings management
(gestion des résultats) Planned timing of revenues, expenses, gains and losses to smooth earnings.
Quality of earnings
(qualité des résultats) How reliably earnings predict future earnings and cash flows.
Gains and losses
(profits et pertes) Increases or decreases in equity that may or may not come from ordinary activities; reported net.
Gross profit
(marge brute) Net sales − cost of goods sold (coût des marchandises vendues).
Income from operations
(résultat d'exploitation) Gross profit − selling and administrative expenses ± other income and expense.
Financing costs
(charges financières) Mainly interest expense; shown after income from operations.
Income from continuing operations
(résultat des activités poursuivies) Income before income tax − income tax.
Discontinued operations
(activités abandonnées) A major line of business or geographical area disposed of or held for sale; reported net of tax.
Intraperiod tax allocation
(répartition de l'impôt entre les composantes du résultat) Relating the tax to continuing and discontinued operations: let the tax follow the income.
Non-controlling interest
(participations ne donnant pas le contrôle) The part of net income that belongs to minority owners of subsidiaries.
Earnings per share
(résultat par action) (Net income − preference dividends) ÷ weighted-average ordinary shares (nombre moyen pondéré d'actions).
By nature / by function
(par nature / par fonction) Expenses grouped by what they are, or by the activity they serve.
Statement of financial position
(bilan / état de la situation financière) Assets, liabilities and equity at a specific date.
Liquidity · solvency · financial flexibility
(liquidité · solvabilité · flexibilité financière) Time to turn assets into cash or pay liabilities · ability to pay debts as they mature · ability to change the amounts and timing of cash flows.
Current / non-current assets
(actifs courants / actifs non courants) Assets turned into cash, sold or used within one year or the operating cycle (cycle d'exploitation), whichever is longer / all other assets.
Property, plant and equipment
(immobilisations corporelles) Tangible long-lived assets used in operations; all except land are depreciated.
Intangible assets
(immobilisations incorporelles) Assets without physical substance: patents, trademarks, franchises, goodwill.
Cash equivalents
(équivalents de trésorerie) Short-term, highly liquid investments maturing within three months or less.
Equity
(capitaux propres) Residual interest in the assets after deducting all liabilities.
Share capital · share premium
(capital social · prime d'émission) Par value of the shares issued · amount paid above par value.
Current / non-current liabilities
(passifs courants / passifs non courants) Obligations settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer / all other obligations, including provisions.
Account form / report form
(présentation en tableau / en liste) Assets left and claims right / sections one above the other.
