01

The time value of money

Imagine your uncle says: "I will give you 1,000 TND. Do you want it today, or in one year?" Almost everyone answers "today". This is not because people are impatient. There are good, logical reasons, and the whole of financial mathematics is built on them.

  • With 1,000 TND today, you can put it in a bank account. In one year you will have more than 1,000 TND.
  • Prices go up. In one year, 1,000 TND will buy less than today.
  • A promise is not cash. In one year, your uncle may forget, or may not have the money.
DefinitionTime value of money, TVM (la valeur temps de l'argent)

A sum of money available today is worth more than the same sum received in the future, because money today can be invested and earn interest.

1 dinar today is worth more than 1 dinar tomorrow.
DefinitionInterest (l'intérêt)

The price paid for using someone else's money for a period of time. For the lender (le prêteur), interest is an income. For the borrower (l'emprunteur), it is a cost.

Why does interest exist? The three reasons

When you lend money, you accept three problems. Interest is the compensation (payment) the lender receives for them.

ReasonWhat the lender acceptsEveryday example
1. Risk (le risque)The borrower might not pay back (risk of default).A bank asks a higher rate from a new small business than from the State, because the business is more likely to fail.
2. Liquidity / opportunity cost (la liquidité, le coût d'opportunité)The lender cannot use the money while it is lent, and gives up other opportunities.Money locked in a 6-month deposit cannot pay for an urgent car repair or a good deal you see next week.
3. Inflation (l'inflation)Prices rise, so the money paid back buys less (loss of purchasing power, pouvoir d'achat).If prices rise by 6% in a year, 1,000 TND next year buys only what about 943 TND buys today (1,000 / 1.06 = 943.40).
Common mistake

"Interest pays for the borrower's convenience." No. Interest compensates the lender for risk, lost liquidity and inflation. The borrower's convenience is not one of the three reasons. This exact trap is in the slide MCQ below.

Warm-up from class · build your own persona

In class you created a "persona": a job, an annual salary, some goals (a car in 2 years, a house in 5 years, retirement at 65), your starting savings and an expected interest rate. Then you asked three TVM questions:

  • Car: if the car costs X in 2 years, how much must I put aside today? (present value, this chapter)
  • Retirement: if I save X every year, how much will I have at 65? (annuities, later chapter)
  • Mortgage (crédit immobilier): what is my monthly payment over 30 years? (later chapter)

Mini example. Yasmine, 26, is a junior accountant in Sfax. She wants a car that will cost 23,200 TND in 2 years. Her bank pays 8% simple interest. How much must she deposit today? Answer: 23,200 / (1 + 0.08 × 2) = 23,200 / 1.16 = 20,000 TND. The 3,200 TND difference is the interest the bank will add. By the end of section 05 you will know exactly why this works.

Example · a TVM decision in a business

A supplier sends a bill of 20,000 TND. You can pay the full amount in 60 days, or pay today with a 2% discount (19,600 TND). This is a time value of money decision: you compare money at two different dates. To compare correctly, you must bring both amounts to the same date. You will solve this exact case in the practice test (Q52).

The slides also show the formula that links today's money and future money. You will study it in detail in section 05:

PV = FV / (1 + r × n)

PV (present value, valeur actuelle) is the value today of a sum FV (future value, valeur future) that you will receive later. r is the annual rate, n the time in years.

In the formula PV = FV / (1 + r × n), what does PV represent?

02

What is simple interest?

There are two ways to calculate interest. In this chapter we use the simplest one. With simple interest, the interest is always calculated on the original amount. The interest earned is not added to the capital to earn more interest. So each year (or each month) brings exactly the same interest. The slides say it in one line: "just principal × rate × time".

DefinitionSimple interest (l'intérêt simple)

Interest calculated only on the original principal (le capital), for the whole period, without compounding. There is no reinvestment of interest, so there is no "interest on interest".

I = P × r × n

Here are the words you will meet in every exercise:

  • P = principal (le capital, capital initial): the amount invested or borrowed at the start. It is also called the present value.
  • r = annual interest rate (le taux d'intérêt annuel), written as a decimal: 7% = 0.07.
  • n = time (la durée), always in years.
  • I = interest (l'intérêt), in TND.
  • A = acquired value (la valeur acquise): P + I, what you have at the end.

When is simple interest used?

Mostly for short-term operations (less than one year): short loans, Treasury bills, short deposits. It is easy to calculate, and it is preferred when the agreement is short, small or straightforward.

Simple vs compound interest

Definition · preview of a later chapterCompound interest (l'intérêt composé)

At the end of each period, the interest is added to the capital, and the next interest is calculated on this bigger amount. You earn interest on interest.

Worked example from the slides · 1,000 TND at 10% for 3 years

Simple: each year brings 1,000 × 0.10 = 100 TND. After 3 years: I = 1,000 × 0.10 × 3 = 300 TND, so A = 1,000 + 300 = 1,300 TND.

Compound: year 1: 1,000 × 1.10 = 1,100. Year 2: 1,100 × 1.10 = 1,210. Year 3: 1,210 × 1.10 = 1,331 TND. In one line: 1,000 × 1.1³ = 1,331.

The 31 TND difference is the "interest on interest".

P1Problem · try it first

Your uncle lends 2,500 TND to a friend at 8% simple interest for 3 years. (a) How much interest is paid each year? (b) What is the total interest and the amount repaid? (c) How much would the friend repay with compound interest (1.08³ = 1.259712)?

Show the full solution

(a) Each year: 2,500 × 0.08 = 200 TND, always the same.

(b) I = 2,500 × 0.08 × 3 = 600 TND. A = 2,500 + 600 = 3,100 TND.

(c) Compound: 2,500 × 1.259712 = 3,149.28 TND. That is 49.28 TND more, the interest on interest.

03

Four short-term instruments that use simple interest

A financial instrument (instrument financier) is a contract that involves money: a loan, a deposit, a bond. The slides present four short-term instruments. In all of them, the money is lent or invested for a short time, usually one year or less, so simple interest is used.

Definition 1Short-term loan (le prêt à court terme)

A loan repaid within one year, used to meet an immediate need. It is often unsecured (sans garantie): no collateral, no house or car given as a guarantee. Types: personal loans, payday loans, business loans. Uses: urgent expenses (medical bills, car repairs) or buying stock (inventory) for a business.

Definition 2Treasury bill, T-bill (le bon du Trésor à court terme)

A short-term security issued by the State (the Treasury) to finance government spending. Its maturity (l'échéance: the date when the money is paid back) is from a few days to one year. It is sold below its face value (valeur nominale); at maturity you receive the face value. The difference is your interest.

Definition 3Certificate of deposit, CD (le compte à terme, le bon de caisse)

A time deposit at a bank: you leave your money for a fixed term (months to years) at a fixed rate, usually simple interest. The rate depends on the term and the amount. If you withdraw early, you may pay a penalty.

Definition 4Bank savings account (le compte d'épargne)

A deposit account that pays interest on the balance. It is for saving, not for daily payments. It is liquid: you can withdraw or transfer easily. Good for short-term goals and an emergency fund.

Memory trick · the liquidity–return trade-off

The easier it is to get your money back, the lower the rate. Savings account (very liquid, low rate) → CD (locked, higher rate). This is reason 2 of section 01 in action: the bank pays you more when you give up more liquidity.

Worked example 1 · a crédit de trésorerie

A café in Sousse borrows 15,000 TND from its bank for 4 months at 11% to buy stock before the summer season.

n = 4/12 year. I = 15,000 × 0.11 × 4/12 = 550 TND. At the end of the 4 months, the café repays 15,000 + 550 = 15,550 TND.

Worked example 2 · a T-bill bought below face value

An investor buys a T-bill with a face value of 20,000 TND, maturing in 90 days, for 19,700 TND.

Interest = what you receive − what you paid = 20,000 − 19,700 = 300 TND.

Rate earned: the money really invested is P = 19,700 TND, for n = 90/360. So r = I / (P × n) = 300 / (19,700 × 90/360) = 300 / 4,925 = 0.06091, about 6.09% per year.

Common mistake · T-bills

A T-bill does not "pay" interest as a separate amount. You buy it cheaper than its face value and receive the face value at maturity: the gap is the interest. In this chapter, compute the rate on the price you paid (the money actually invested), not on the face value. (In the bank discount chapter you will see a different rate based on the face value.)

False. According to the slides, the main disadvantage of short-term loans is their higher interest rates. You pay for the speed and for the lack of collateral.
04

Measuring time: the 360-day year and counting days

In I = P × r × n, the rate r is an annual rate (per year). So n must also be in years. But short operations are given in months or days. You must convert:

n = months / 12   ·   n = days / 360

So when the time is in days, the interest formula becomes:

I = P × r × d / 360
DefinitionCommercial year (l'année commerciale)

A conventional year of 360 days (12 months × 30 days), used by banks for short-term interest. All the slide solutions use it. Use 360 in every exercise unless the question says otherwise.

Ordinary interest vs exact interest

The course syllabus names two ways to turn days into years:

MethodYear usedFormulaUsed by
Ordinary interest (intérêt commercial, ordinaire), also called banker's interest360 daysI = P × r × d / 360Banks, and this course
Exact interest (intérêt exact, civil)365 days (366 in a leap year)I = P × r × d / 365Some governments and contracts

In both methods, d is the exact number of days on the calendar. Counting the real days and dividing by 360 is called the banker's rule. It is what your slides do.

Worked example · 10,000 TND at 7% for 108 days

Ordinary: I = 10,000 × 0.07 × 108/360 = 10,000 × 0.07 × 0.3 = 210 TND.

Exact: I = 10,000 × 0.07 × 108/365 = 75,600 / 365 = 207.123 TND.

Ordinary interest gives the lender 2.877 TND more. Why? Dividing by a smaller number (360) gives a bigger fraction of a year, so more interest. That is why banks like the 360-day year when they lend.

Careful · another method in some books

Some textbooks also count every month as 30 days ("approximate time"). Your slides do not do this. They count the real days of each month (31 for May, 30 for June, 28 for February...) and divide by 360.

How to count days, step by step

Do not count the start day. Count the end day.

Think of a hotel: you arrive on 10 March and leave on 13 March. You pay for 3 nights (10→11, 11→12, 12→13), not 4. Money works the same way: the day you deposit does not earn interest, the day you withdraw does.

  1. First month: days left = (number of days in that month) − (start date).
  2. Months in between: add the full length of each month.
  3. Last month: add the end date (its day number).
  4. Same month? Just do end date − start date.

Memory trick · the knuckles

Make a fist and look at your knuckles. Say the months: a knuckle = 31 days, a valley between knuckles = 30 days (or February). January (knuckle), February (valley), March (knuckle), April (valley), May (knuckle), June (valley), July (last knuckle). Then go back to the first knuckle: August (knuckle), September (valley), October (knuckle), November (valley), December (knuckle). July and August are both 31.

The reverse: finding a date from a number of days

Sometimes you know the number of days (for example from n = I / (P × r)) and you must find the date. Go forward month by month, subtracting:

  1. Subtract the days left in the start month.
  2. Subtract full months while the remainder is bigger than the next month.
  3. The remainder is the day number in the final month.
Worked example · 60 days after 15 March

Days left in March: 31 − 15 = 16 → 60 − 16 = 44 still to go.

April has 30 days: 44 − 30 = 14 still to go. May has 31, more than 14, so we stop in May.

Answer: 14 May. Check: 16 + 30 + 14 = 60 ✓.

Common mistakes with time
  • Counting the start day (you get 1 day too many).
  • Giving every month 30 days, or forgetting that February has 28 days.
  • Dividing days by 365 when the course uses 360.
  • Dividing months by 360, or days by 12. Months → ÷ 12. Days → ÷ 360.
Exam alert

Always write the day count in full, like the teacher does: "7 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 9 = 108 days". If the total is wrong by one day, you still get the method marks, and the teacher can see where the slip is.

The formulas F1 to F6

The slides give seven formulas. Good news: they all come from one, I = P × r × n. Each formula just isolates a different letter. If you understand F1, you can rebuild the others in an exam even if you forget them. F7 (the average rate) has its own section later.

F1 · Total interest

I = P × r × n

Use it when you know the capital, the rate and the time, and you want the interest.

Example F1

Mehdi lends 6,000 TND to his cousin's shop at 8% for 9 months. I = 6,000 × 0.08 × 9/12 = 6,000 × 0.08 × 0.75 = 360 TND.

F2 · Interest rate

r = I / (P × n)

Use it when you know the interest, the capital and the time, and you want the rate. With days: r = I × 360 / (P × d).

Example F2

A deposit of 5,000 TND earned 175 TND in 6 months. r = 175 / (5,000 × 6/12) = 175 / 2,500 = 0.07 = 7%.

F3 · Term to maturity (time)

n = I / (P × r)

Use it when you want the time. The answer is in years. Multiply by 12 to get months, by 360 to get days. With days directly: d = I × 360 / (P × r).

Example F3

12,000 TND at 6% earned 240 TND. n = 240 / (12,000 × 0.06) = 240 / 720 = 0.3333 year. In months: 0.3333 × 12 = 4 months. In days: 240 × 360 / 720 = 120 days.

F4 · Future value (acquired value)

A = P × (1 + r × n) = P + I

The acquired value (valeur acquise) is what you have at the end: capital plus interest. Where does the formula come from? A = P + I = P + P × r × n = P × (1 + r × n). We just factor out P.

Example F4

Ines deposits 4,000 TND at 7.5% for 144 days. A = 4,000 × (1 + 0.075 × 144/360) = 4,000 × (1 + 0.03) = 4,000 × 1.03 = 4,120 TND. (So I = 120 TND.)

F5 · Present value

P = A / (1 + r × n)
DefinitionPresent value (la valeur actuelle)

The value today of a sum to be received or paid in the future. It is the amount you must invest today, at rate r, to have A at the end. To find it, we divide A by (1 + r × n): we "remove" the future interest.

Example F5

A company must pay its supplier 2,575 TND in 6 months. Money earns 6%. How much must it place today to have exactly 2,575 TND then? P = 2,575 / (1 + 0.06 × 6/12) = 2,575 / 1.03 = 2,500 TND. Check: 2,500 × 1.03 = 2,575 ✓.

F6 · Rate or time from present and future values

r = [(FV / PV) − 1] / n   ·   n = [(FV / PV) − 1] / r

Where does it come from? FV = PV × (1 + r × n). Divide both sides by PV: FV / PV = 1 + r × n. Subtract 1: r × n = (FV / PV) − 1. Then divide by n (to get r) or by r (to get n).

Example F6

Rate: 3,200 TND became 3,320 TND after 5 months. FV / PV = 3,320 / 3,200 = 1.0375. r = 0.0375 / (5/12) = 0.0375 × 12/5 = 0.09 = 9%.

Time: 3,200 TND at 6% became 3,280 TND. FV / PV = 1.025. n = 0.025 / 0.06 = 0.41667 year = 0.41667 × 12 = 5 months (= 150 days).

Tip: you can also do it in two steps: I = FV − PV, then F2 or F3. Same answer.

The average rate of several investments (F7)

Sometimes money is split into several placements, each with its own capital, rate and duration. Is the whole package better than one simple placement at, say, 4%? To compare, we replace all the different rates by one single rate: the average rate.

DefinitionAverage rate (le taux moyen de placement)

The single rate that, applied to all the placements over their own durations, gives the same total interest as the real, different rates. It is used to compare alternatives.

rA = Σ(Pi × ri × ni) / Σ(Pi × ni)

Σ (the Greek letter sigma) means "the sum of". So: add up all the P × r × n on top, add up all the P × n on the bottom, and divide.

Where does it come from?

We want a rate rA such that the total interest stays the same: Σ(Pi × rA × ni) = Σ(Pi × ri × ni). Since rA is the same for every placement, we can take it out: rA × Σ(Pi × ni) = Σ(Pi × ri × ni). Divide, and you get F7.

This means rA is a weighted average: each rate counts in proportion to its "capital × time" (Pi × ni). A big placement for a long time counts a lot; a small, short one counts little.

Shortcut · the 360 disappears

If every ni = di/360, the 360 appears on top and on the bottom, so it cancels. You can put the number of days directly in the table. (Same with months: the 12 cancels.) Just use the same unit for all the placements.

Worked example · why we can't just average the rates

1,000 TND at 5% for 30 days, and 9,000 TND at 8% for 90 days.

rA = 66,300 / 840,000 = 0.07893 ≈ 7.89%.

The simple average of the rates, (5% + 8%) / 2 = 6.5%, is wrong: it ignores that most of the money (9,000 TND) is at 8%, and for longer.

Common mistakes with F7
  • Averaging the rates directly: (r1 + r2 + r3) / 3.
  • Weighting only by the capitals and forgetting the durations.
  • Mixing units: some ni in days and others in months.

Sanity check: the average rate is always between the smallest and the largest rate.

Equations with two capitals (Exercises 3, 4 and 6)

In these problems, some numbers are missing. Instead, the text gives a relationship: "the two capitals produce the same interest", "their sum is 7,000 TND", "the second started 6 months later". You turn each sentence into an equation, then solve it.

DefinitionSetting up the equation (la mise en équation)

Translating the words of a problem into algebra. The most common sentence is "the interests are equal": I1 = I2, so P1 × r1 × n1 = P2 × r2 × n2.

  1. Name the unknowns (P1, P2, r, or n).
  2. Write each interest with I = P × r × n, keeping fractions like 3/12 exact.
  3. Translate each sentence into an equation.
  4. Solve (substitute one equation into the other).
  5. Check by recalculating both interests.
Worked example · a quick one

Nour places 5,000 TND at 6% for 6 months. Her brother places 4,000 TND for the same 6 months and earns the same interest. What is his rate?

I1 = I2 → 5,000 × 0.06 × 6/12 = 4,000 × r × 6/12. The 6/12 is on both sides, so it cancels: 300 = 4,000 r → r = 0.075 = 7.5%.

Check: 5,000 × 0.06 × 0.5 = 150; 4,000 × 0.075 × 0.5 = 150 ✓. Smaller capital → it needs a higher rate to earn the same interest.

Other way to see it: 19,600 kept in the bank for 60 days becomes 19,600 × 1.01 = 19,796 TND, not enough to pay 20,000. So pay today. (d) is wrong: comparing different dates is exactly what TVM does.
Q53F6

Sami invested 8,000 TND and received 8,360 TND after 9 months. What was the annual rate?

FV / PV = 8,360 / 8,000 = 1.045. r = (1.045 − 1) / (9/12) = 0.045 / 0.75 = 6%. (b) forgets to divide by n. (c) multiplies by n instead of dividing.
Q54True or false

When the interest rate is positive, the present value of a future sum is always smaller than that sum.

True. P = A / (1 + r × n), and (1 + r × n) is bigger than 1, so P is smaller than A. This is the time value of money in one line: money in the future is worth less today.
Q55Short-term loan

Youssef borrows 3,000 TND to buy a laptop for his studies, at 12% simple interest for 10 months. How much does he repay in total?

I = 3,000 × 0.12 × 10/12 = 300 TND, so he repays 3,000 + 300 = 3,300 TND. (b) is 12 months. (c) divides 10 months by 360. (d) uses 10 years.
P14Problem · find n from PV and FV

Show the full solution

F6: FV / PV = 12,450 / 12,000 = 1.0375. n = (1.0375 − 1) / 0.05 = 0.0375 / 0.05 = 0.75 year.

In months: 0.75 × 12 = 9 months. In days: 0.75 × 360 = 270 days.

P15Problem · equal interest

Leila places 4,000 TND at 6% for 10 months. Her brother places 6,000 TND at 5%. For how many months must he place it to earn the same interest as Leila?

Show the full solution

Leila: I = 4,000 × 0.06 × 10/12 = 200 TND.

Brother: 6,000 × 0.05 × m/12 = 25 m (m in months).

Equal interest: 25 m = 200 → m = 8 months.

Check: 6,000 × 0.05 × 8/12 = 200 ✓. He has more capital, so he needs less time.

P16Problem · present value

Yassine wants 1,800 TND on 1 July for a summer trip to Tabarka. On 1 January he can put money in a savings account at 4%. How much must he deposit? (Take 6 months.)

Show the full solution

Recap and formula sheet

What to remember for Thursday

  • TVM: 1 dinar today is worth more than 1 dinar in the future. Interest exists because of risk, liquidity (opportunity cost) and inflation, not the borrower's convenience.
  • Simple interest is always calculated on the original principal: a straight line. Compound = interest on interest: a curve.
  • Four instruments: short-term loans (crédit de trésorerie), T-bills (bought below face value), CDs (compte à terme), savings accounts (liquid, low rate).
  • Time in years: days ÷ 360, months ÷ 12. Ordinary interest (360) > exact interest (365).
  • Counting days: don't count the start day, count the end day. Days left in the first month + full months + end date.
  • Rate as a decimal: 7% = 0.07. Don't round in the middle of a calculation.
  • Average rate: weighted by P × n, never the simple average of rates. Always end with a decision.
  • Equations: I1 = I2, cancel what appears on both sides, solve, check.

Glossary (English → French)

Time value of money
La valeur temps de l'argent. Money today is worth more than the same sum in the future.
Interest
L'intérêt. The price paid for using someone else's money for a period of time.
Lender / borrower
Le prêteur / l'emprunteur. The one who gives the money / the one who receives it and pays interest.
Simple interest
L'intérêt simple. Interest calculated only on the original principal: I = P × r × n.
Compound interest
L'intérêt composé. Interest added to the capital each period, so it earns interest too.
Principal (P)
Le capital. The amount invested or borrowed at the start.
Interest rate (r)
Le taux d'intérêt. The annual percentage, used as a decimal (7% = 0.07).
Time, term (n)
La durée. The length of the operation, in years (d/360 or m/12).
Acquired value (A, FV)
La valeur acquise (valeur future). Capital + interest at the end: P(1 + r × n).
Present value (P, PV)
La valeur actuelle. Today's value of a future sum: A / (1 + r × n).
Commercial year
L'année commerciale. A year of 360 days, used by banks and in this course.
Ordinary interest
L'intérêt commercial (ordinaire). Interest using a 360-day year.
Exact interest
L'intérêt exact (civil). Interest using a 365-day year (366 in a leap year).
Banker's rule
La règle du banquier. Exact number of calendar days divided by 360.
Leap year
L'année bissextile. A year with 29 days in February (2024, 2028...).
Average rate
Le taux moyen de placement. The single rate giving the same total interest as several placements: F7.
Short-term loan, cash-flow loan
Le prêt à court terme, le crédit de trésorerie. A loan repaid within one year.
Collateral
La garantie. An asset given as security for a loan. Unsecured loans have none.
Treasury bill (T-bill)
Le bon du Trésor (à court terme, BTCT). A short-term State security sold below face value.
Face value
La valeur nominale. The amount written on a security, paid at maturity.
Maturity
L'échéance. The date when the money is paid back.
Certificate of deposit (CD)
Le compte à terme, le bon de caisse. A bank deposit for a fixed term at a fixed rate.
Savings account
Le compte d'épargne. A liquid deposit account that pays a low interest rate.
Liquidity
La liquidité. How quickly and easily money can be used or withdrawn.
Inflation, purchasing power
L'inflation, le pouvoir d'achat. Rising prices reduce what a sum of money can buy.
Risk of default
Le risque de défaut. The risk that the borrower does not repay.
