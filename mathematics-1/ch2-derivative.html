Rate of Change: The Derivative

In Chapter 1 you measured how fast something changes on average between two points. This chapter zooms in to one single moment: how fast is it changing right now? That number is the derivative. It is the most important idea of the whole course, and in business it becomes marginal cost and marginal revenue.

  • Find marginal cost, marginal revenue and marginal profit, and decide whether a firm should produce one more unit.

Instantaneous rate of change and the derivative at a point

In Chapter 1 you learned the average rate of change. It tells you how fast a quantity changes on average over an interval. But an average can hide a lot. In this section we shrink the interval again and again until we get the rate of change at one single point. That rate has a special name: the derivative.

Reminder from Chapter 1: the average rate of change

Average rate of change of f from a to b = (f(b) − f(a)) / (b − a)

On a graph, this is the slope of the straight line that joins the two points (a, f(a)) and (b, f(b)). Its units are always "units of the output per unit of the input" (for example, dinars per kilogram, or people per year).

Example from the notes · the car and the speedometer

A car drives 300 miles in 3 hours.

Average speed = 300 miles ÷ 3 hours = 100 miles per hour.

But the driver did not drive at exactly 100 mph the whole time. Sometimes the car stopped at a red light (speed 0). Sometimes it went faster than 100. The number on the speedometer at one moment is the instantaneous speed, the speed "right now".

Local example · Tunis to Sfax on the A1

A louage leaves Tunis and arrives in Sfax, about 270 km away, 3 hours later.

Average speed = 270 km ÷ 3 h = 90 km/h.

At the péage the speedometer shows 0 km/h. On the open autoroute it may show 110 km/h. The average (90 km/h) is one number for the whole trip. The speedometer gives a different number at every moment. This chapter is about that "speedometer number" for any function, not only for cars.

DefinitionInstantaneous rate of change (taux de variation instantané)

The instantaneous rate of change of f at x = a is the limit of the average rates of change of f over smaller and smaller intervals around a.

DefinitionLimit (limite)

The single number that a list of values gets closer and closer to as the interval gets smaller. You may never reach the limit exactly with a calculator, but you can see where the values are heading.

The derivative at a point

Now we give the instantaneous rate of change its official name and symbol.

DefinitionDerivative of f at a (nombre dérivé de f en a)

The derivative of f at x = a, written f′(a) and read "f prime of a", is the instantaneous rate of change of f at a:

f′(a) = limit, as h → 0, of (f(a + h) − f(a)) / h

Let's read this formula piece by piece:

  • h is a small step away from a. It can be positive (we look to the right of a) or negative (to the left), but it is never exactly 0.
  • f(a + h) − f(a) is how much the output changes when the input moves from a to a + h.
  • Dividing by h gives the average rate of change on the small interval between a and a + h. This fraction is called the difference quotient (taux d'accroissement).
  • "limit as h → 0" means: make h smaller and smaller and see which number the difference quotients approach.

Method · estimating a derivative numerically
  1. Compute f(a).
  2. Choose a small h (0.1, then 0.01, then 0.001) and compute f(a + h).
  3. Compute the difference quotient (f(a + h) − f(a)) / h.
  4. Repeat with smaller h, and with negative h. Write the number that the results approach.

Seeing the derivative: the slope of the tangent line

Look again at the difference quotient (f(a + h) − f(a)) / h. On the graph it is the slope of the line through two points: P = (a, f(a)) and Q = (a + h, f(a + h)). A line that cuts a curve at two points is called a secant line (droite sécante).

When h gets smaller, the point Q slides along the curve towards P. The secant line turns little by little, and in the end it becomes the line that just "touches" the curve at P. This line is the tangent line (la tangente).

DefinitionSlope of a curve at a point = slope of its tangent line

f′(a) is the slope of the graph of f at the point (a, f(a)). It is also the slope of the tangent line at that point.

If you zoom in a lot on a smooth curve near a point, the curve looks almost like a straight line. That line is the tangent line. This is called local linearity.

The equation of the tangent line

A line is fixed by one point and its slope. The tangent line at x = a goes through the point (a, f(a)) and has slope f′(a). So:

Tangent line at x = a: y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a)
Worked example · tangent line of the cube function (from the notes)

Point: f(2) = 8, so the line passes through (2, 8).

Slope: f′(2) = 12 (we estimated it above).

Equation: y = 8 + 12(x − 2).

Expand: y = 8 + 12x − 24.

➜ y = 12x − 16.

Check that the tangent stays close to the curve near x = 2: at x = 2.1 the line gives 12(2.1) − 16 = 9.2 and the curve gives 2.13 = 9.261. Very close. At x = 1.9: the line gives 6.8 and the curve gives 6.859.

Worked example · tangent line of the square function

Point: f(3) = 32 = 9.

Slope, numerically: with h = 0.01, (3.012 − 9) / 0.01 = (9.0601 − 9) / 0.01 = 6.01. With h = −0.01, (2.992 − 9) / (−0.01) = (8.9401 − 9) / (−0.01) = 5.99. So f′(3) ≈ 6.

Equation: y = 9 + 6(x − 3) = 9 + 6x − 18.

➜ y = 6x − 9.

How to read a slope from a grid

Imagine the tangent line at the point. Count how many squares it goes up (or down) for one square across to the right. Up 3 → slope 3. Down 1 → slope −1. Half a square up → slope 0.5.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing f(a) and f′(a). f(a) is the height of the graph at a. f′(a) is the slope (steepness) at a. A point can be high on the graph with a slope of 0 (the top of a hill).
  • Forgetting to divide by h. f(a + h) − f(a) is only the change in output. The rate needs the division.
  • Stopping too early. One value of h gives an estimate, not the derivative. Use smaller h (and negative h) to see the limit.
Exam alert

A classic exam question gives a table and asks you to estimate f′ at a value in the middle. Use the closest values on both sides. Example: if f(1) = 5, f(2) = 8, f(3) = 12, then f′(2) ≈ (12 − 5) / (3 − 1) = 3.5. (Using only the right side gives 4; only the left side gives 3. Averaging them also gives 3.5.)

The derivative function

In Section 1 we computed the derivative at one point. But we can do the same at every point x. Each x gives its own slope f′(x). So the slopes themselves form a new function, called the derivative function. It is like a "slope machine": you give it an x, it gives you how steep f is there.

DefinitionDerivative function (fonction dérivée)

The derivative function f′ gives, for every x, the slope of the graph of f at x (the instantaneous rate of change of f at x):

f′(x) = limit, as h → 0, of (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h

Worked example · the derivative of f(x) = x2

Step by step

We estimate f′(x) at five points with h = 0.001, using (f(x + 0.001) − f(x)) / 0.001.

x−2−1012
Difference quotient (h = 0.001)−3.999−1.9990.0012.0014.001
Estimate of f′(x)−4−2024

Pattern: every estimate is 2 times x. So f′(x) = 2x.

The graph of f′ is a straight line through the origin:

  • for x < 0, f′(x) < 0: the parabola is going down;
  • at x = 0, f′(0) = 0: the bottom of the parabola is flat;
  • for x > 0, f′(x) > 0: the parabola is going up, and more steeply the further right you go.

What the sign of f′ tells you about f

This is the most useful link of the chapter. You can read the shape of f from the sign of f′, and the other way round.

On an interval, if …then the graph is …
f′ > 0 (positive)increasing (croissante): going up from left to right
f′ < 0 (negative)decreasing (décroissante): going down from left to right
f′ = 0 at every pointconstant: a horizontal line
f′ = 0 at one point, with a smooth turna peak (local maximum) or a valley (local minimum) is possible there
f going up ⇔ f′ above the x-axis. f going down ⇔ f′ below the x-axis. Peaks and valleys of f ⇔ f′ crosses zero.

How to sketch f′ from the graph of f (Examples 1–2 in the notes)

The notes ask you to choose or sketch the graph of f′ from the graph of f. Your class handout has its own graphs; the method is always the same:

Method · sketch the derivative in 4 steps
  1. Find the flat points of f (tops of hills, bottoms of valleys). Mark them on the x-axis of your f′ sketch: there f′ = 0.
  2. Where f goes up, draw f′ above the x-axis.
  3. Where f goes down, draw f′ below the x-axis.
  4. Where f is steep, draw f′ far from the axis. Where f is almost flat, draw f′ close to the axis.
Worked example · a hill-shaped profit curve

A firm's profit f(p) depends on the price p it charges. At low prices profit rises with the price, it reaches a maximum, and at high prices profit falls (customers leave). The graph of f is a hill.

Step 1: the top of the hill is the only flat point → f′ = 0 there.

Step 2: on the left of the top, f goes up → f′ is positive.

Step 3: on the right of the top, f goes down → f′ is negative.

Step 4: far from the top the hill is steeper → f′ is far from 0. Near the top the hill is almost flat → f′ is close to 0.

➜ The graph of f′ starts high and positive, comes down, crosses the x-axis exactly at the price of maximum profit, and continues below the axis. (If f is a parabola-shaped hill, f′ is a decreasing straight line.)

Local example · water in the Sidi Salem dam

Let W = f(t) be the volume of water in the Sidi Salem dam (on the Medjerda, the biggest dam in Tunisia), in millions of m³, t months after the start of September, in a typical year.

Autumn and winter: rain fills the dam, W goes up → f′(t) > 0 (units: millions of m³ per month).

Spring, when the rain stops: the water reaches its highest level → f′(t) = 0 at that moment.

Summer: irrigation, drinking water and evaporation empty the dam, W goes down → f′(t) < 0.

So the graph of W is a hill, and the graph of W′ is positive, then zero at the peak, then negative: exactly the shape of the profit example.

Common mistakes
  • Drawing f instead of f′. The graph of f′ shows slopes, not heights. A hill in f does not give a hill in f′.
  • "Decreasing" is not "negative". f can be decreasing while its values are still positive (a full dam that is emptying).
  • f′ = 0 does not always mean a peak or valley. f(x) = x3 is flat at x = 0 (f′(0) = 0) but keeps increasing on both sides. It just pauses.
Exam alert

In "match f with f′" questions, start with the flat points of f: they must be exactly the places where the graph of f′ crosses (or touches) the x-axis. This eliminates most wrong options in a few seconds.

Q13True or false · Moodle T/F #5

False. "f increasing" only tells you f′ is positive, not that f′ is growing. Example: √x (or ln x) always goes up, but it gets flatter and flatter, so its slope f′ decreases. Don't confuse the sign of f′ with the direction of f′.
Q14True or false · Moodle T/F #6

The derivative of the function f(x) = 5 is 0.

True. The graph of f(x) = 5 is a horizontal line: its slope is 0 everywhere. With the formula: (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h = (5 − 5) / h = 0 for every h.
Q15True or false · Moodle T/F #7

If f′(x) = 0 at all points of an interval, the graph of f is a horizontal line on that interval.

True. Slope 0 at every point means the function never goes up or down there, so it is constant: a horizontal line. (This is different from f′ = 0 at only one point.)
Q16Multiple choice

The derivative of a function is f′(x) = 2x − 6. On which interval is f decreasing?

f decreases where f′ < 0: 2x − 6 < 0 ⇔ 2x < 6 ⇔ x < 3. For x > 3, f′ > 0 and f increases. At x = 3, f′ = 0: that is the bottom of a valley.

For the Sidi Salem dam, W′(t) = −15 million m³ per month in July. In July, is the water level rising or falling?

A negative derivative means the function is decreasing: the dam is losing about 15 million m³ of water per month in July (summer irrigation and evaporation).

Interpretations of the derivative

On the exam you will not only compute derivatives. You will have to explain what they mean, in a sentence, with units. This section gives you the notation, the units, and three tools: interpretation sentences, the local linear approximation, and the relative rate of change.

Leibniz notation: dy/dx

If y = f(x), a small change in x is written Δx ("delta x") and the change it causes in y is Δy. The average rate of change is Δy/Δx. When Δx becomes very small, the mathematician Leibniz wrote the limit as dy/dx.

DefinitionLeibniz notation (notation de Leibniz)
f′(x) = dy/dx

Read "dy/dx" as "the derivative of y with respect to x" ("dee y by dee x"). It is one symbol, not a normal fraction, but it reminds you that the derivative is a limit of Δy/Δx.

Units of the derivative

Units of dy/dx = (units of y) ÷ (units of x) → "units of y PER unit of x"

Worked example · Facehooked (Example 1 in the notes)

Let L = f(q) be the number of hours a student spends on the social site "Facehooked" when she has q friend requests.

Step by step

(a) Interpret f(8) = 3.

Input q = 8 friend requests; output L = 3 hours.

➜ When the student has 8 friend requests, she spends 3 hours on Facehooked.

(b) What are the units of f′(8)?

Units of L ÷ units of q = hours per friend request.

(c) Interpret f′(8) = 1.

➜ When she has 8 friend requests, each additional friend request adds about 1 more hour on the site.

(d) Estimate f(9) and f(10).

From 8 to 9 requests: about +1 hour, so f(9) ≈ 3 + 1 = 4 hours.

From 8 to 10 requests: about +1 hour each, so f(10) ≈ 3 + 2 × 1 = 5 hours.

Sentence template · interpreting a derivative

"When [input] is a [units of input], [output] is increasing (if b > 0) / decreasing (if b < 0) by about |b| [units of output] per [unit of input]."

Or, the business version: "When [input] is a, one more [unit of input] adds about b [units of output]."

The local linear approximation

Look at what we did in (d): we started from a known value f(a) and added "rate × change in input". That is the local linear approximation. It uses the tangent line instead of the curve.

DefinitionLocal linear approximation (approximation affine locale)
f(x) ≈ f(a) + f′(a) · Δx, where Δx = x − a

In words: new value ≈ old value + (rate of change) × (change in input). In particular, f′(a) ≈ the change in f when x increases by 1 unit.

The approximation is good when x is close to a. The further you go, the more the curve can bend away from the tangent line.

DefinitionDifferential (différentielle)

Your notes' objectives say "use differentials for estimates". This is the local linear approximation under another name. For a small change dx = Δx in the input, the estimated change in the output is the differential dy:

dy = f′(a) · dx so Δy ≈ dy = f′(a) · Δx

Example (the drug, Example 2): f′(10) = 0.5 hour per mg and dx = 1 mg give dy = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5 hour, so f(11) ≈ 6 + 0.5 = 6.5 hours.

Relative rate of change

In Chapter 1 you met the relative change (P1 − P0)/P0, a percentage. The instantaneous version compares the rate of change to the size of the quantity.

DefinitionRelative rate of change (taux de variation relatif)

The relative rate of change of y = f(t) at t = a is

Relative rate = (dy/dt) / y = f′(a) / f(a)

It is usually given as a percent per unit of time (for example, % per year). It has no other units.

Why is it useful? A growth of 2 million per year is huge for a small town and tiny for China. The relative rate tells you how fast something grows compared to its size.

The second derivative

f′ is a function, so it has its own rate of change. The derivative of the derivative is called the second derivative. It answers a very practical question: is the change speeding up or slowing down? Sales are growing, fine. But are they growing faster and faster, or more and more slowly? That is what f″ tells you.

DefinitionSecond derivative (dérivée seconde)

The second derivative of f, written f″ ("f double prime"), is the derivative of f′. In Leibniz notation:

f″(x) = d2y/dx2 (the rate of change of the rate of change)

Its units are: units of y per unit of x, per unit of x. For a distance in km and a time in hours, f″ is in km/h per hour: an acceleration.

Concavity: the shape of the graph

The sign of f″ tells you how the graph bends.

DefinitionConcave up (convexe, in French)

If f″ > 0 on an interval, then f′ is increasing there: the slopes get bigger as you move right. The graph bends upward, like a smile ∪. We say f is concave up.

DefinitionConcave down (concave, in French)

If f″ < 0 on an interval, then f′ is decreasing there: the slopes get smaller as you move right. The graph bends downward, like a frown ∩. We say f is concave down.

French trap · read this twice

In French maths, a graph shaped like ∪ is called convexe, and a graph shaped like ∩ is called concave. In English (our textbook and our exams), ∪ is "concave up" and ∩ is "concave down". So "concave up" = convexe. Never translate "concave up" as just "concave".

f″ > 0 → f′ increasing → concave up ∪ (smile). f″ < 0 → f′ decreasing → concave down ∩ (frown).

Concavity and increasing/decreasing are independent: f′ gives the direction (up or down), f″ gives the bending. So there are four possible combinations.

Worked example · the parabola is concave up everywhere

In Section 2 we found the slopes of x2: at x = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 they are −4, −2, 0, 2, 4.

The slopes go up by 2 for each step of 1 in x. So f′ is increasing, and f″ = 2 > 0 everywhere.

➜ The parabola is concave up everywhere, even on the left side where it is decreasing. This is case (c) on the left of 0 and case (a) on the right.

DefinitionInflection point (point d'inflexion)

A point where the graph changes concavity: from concave up to concave down, or the other way. At an inflection point f″ changes sign. (You will need this word in Problem 11.)

Marginal cost and marginal revenue

This is where the derivative becomes a business tool. A manager rarely asks "should we produce or not?". The real question is: "should we produce one more?" Economists call this thinking at the margin.

Story from the notes · one more flight

An airline already runs 100 flights a week and thinks about adding a 101st flight. The useful numbers are not the total cost of 101 flights. They are:

the additional cost of the 101st flight: C(101) − C(100);

the additional revenue of the 101st flight: R(101) − R(100).

If the extra revenue is bigger than the extra cost, the new flight increases profit. Notice that C(101) − C(100) is an average rate of change over an interval of length 1, so it is close to the derivative C′(100).

DefinitionMarginal cost (coût marginal), MC
MC = C′(q) ≈ C(q + 1) − C(q)

The approximate cost of producing one more unit after q units.

DefinitionMarginal revenue (recette marginale), MR
MR = R′(q) ≈ R(q + 1) − R(q)

The approximate revenue earned from selling one more unit after q units.

DefinitionMarginal profit (bénéfice marginal)
Profit π(q) = R(q) − C(q) → marginal profit = π′(q) = MR − MC

The approximate change in profit from producing and selling one more unit.

Units: MC and MR are in money per unit (dollars per item, TND per chair, TND per kg). They have the same units.

Link to Chapter 1: for the radio factory, C(q) = 24,000 + 7q was a straight line, so the marginal cost was always the slope, $7 per radio. Real cost curves bend, so the marginal cost changes with q. The derivative gives the marginal cost at each q.

The picture: marginal cost is a slope

The decision rule

If MR > MC, producing one more unit increases profit. If MR < MC, it decreases profit. The best quantity is where MR = MC.
Same idea as Microeconomics Ch 1

In micro you learned: do one more unit of an activity when the marginal benefit MB is bigger than the marginal cost MC. Here the "benefit" of one more unit is the marginal revenue MR. Same rule, now with derivatives.

P11Problem · sketch from the first and second derivatives (probable typo)

Sketch a graph of f with: f′(x) > 0 for all x; f″(x) < 0 for x < 2; and f″(x) < 0 for x > 2 (as printed on the handout).

Show the full solution

Reading 1, exactly as printed. f′ > 0 everywhere → f is always increasing. f″ < 0 on both sides of 2 → concave down on both sides. So f is increasing and concave down everywhere: it rises more and more slowly (like ln x or √x). Nothing special happens at x = 2.

Reading 2, the probable intended version. It is strange to write two separate conditions that say the same thing. In the textbook version of this problem the first condition is f″ > 0 for x < 2. Then:

f′ > 0 everywhere → always increasing.

x < 2: f″ > 0 → concave up (rising faster and faster).

x > 2: f″ < 0 → concave down (rising more and more slowly).

At x = 2 the concavity changes: an inflection point. The graph is an S-shape.

On an exam, if a statement looks like a typo, answer what is printed and add one line: "If the first condition was meant to be f″ > 0, then …". Ask your teacher which version she wants.

Recap, formula sheet and key terms

What to remember

  • Average rate of change on [a, b] = (f(b) − f(a)) / (b − a): the slope of the secant line.
  • Derivative at a point f′(a) = the instantaneous rate of change = the limit of average rates over smaller and smaller intervals = the slope of the tangent line.
  • Estimate f′(a) numerically with (f(a + h) − f(a)) / h for h = 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 and negative h. From a table, use the values on both sides.
  • Tangent line: y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a).
  • f′ > 0 → f increasing; f′ < 0 → f decreasing; smooth peaks and valleys have f′ = 0 (but f′ = 0 does not always give a peak or valley).
  • Units of f′ = units of output per unit of input. Interpret with a full sentence: at which input, how much, per what.
  • Local linear approximation: f(x) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)Δx. Reliable only close to a.
  • Relative rate = f′/f, a percentage per unit of time.
  • f″ > 0 → concave up ∪ (f′ increasing); f″ < 0 → concave down ∩ (f′ decreasing). Concave up = "convexe" in French.
  • MC = C′(q) ≈ C(q + 1) − C(q), MR = R′(q), marginal profit = MR − MC. MR > MC → produce more; MR < MC → produce less.
