Shortcuts to Differentiation

In Chapter 2 you found derivatives the slow way: tables of h values and zooming in on graphs. This chapter gives you the shortcuts. A small set of rules turns any formula for f into a formula for f′ in a few lines. With them you can find tangent lines, marginal cost and revenue, growth rates and average cost in seconds. These rules are the heart of the midterm.

On the midterm (20%) ≈ 5 h 30 study time · do it in 3 sessions From Moodle: 8-page notes + 9 problems Textbook: Hughes-Hallett, Applied Calculus, Ch 3

By the end you can

  • Find a derivative (dérivée) with formulas, and values of derivatives such as f′(4).
  • Differentiate a constant and a constant times a function, and use the power rule and the sum and difference rule, also with negative and fractional powers (1/x3, √x, ∛x).
  • Find the equation of a tangent line, and the points where the tangent line has a given slope (for example a horizontal tangent).
  • Differentiate exponential and logarithmic functions: ex, ekx, ax and ln x.
  • Use the chain rule and the extended power rule for functions inside functions.
  • Use the product rule and the quotient rule, and choose the right rule for any function.
  • Use the quotient rule to differentiate average cost, average revenue and average profit, and compare marginal cost with average cost.
  • Use differentials to estimate a change.
  • Explain every result in a sentence, with units, as the Rule of Four asks. A full answer gives the formula, the number, the units and the words.

Derivative formulas for powers and polynomials

In Chapter 2 you estimated f′(2) for f(x) = x3 with a table: 12.61, then 12.0601, then 12.006, … so f′(2) ≈ 12. It worked, but it was long, and it gave only one number. Now we want much more: a formula for f′(x), found directly from the formula for f(x).

Finding a derivative is called differentiating (dériver), and the derivative itself is la dérivée. Each rule in this chapter was proved once with the limit definition from Chapter 2. You don't need to repeat the limit every time: you just apply the rule.

Before you start · what you need from Chapters 1 and 2
  • f′(a) is the slope of the tangent line at x = a, and the instantaneous rate of change (Ch 2 · Section 1).
  • The tangent line at x = a is y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a) (Ch 2 · Section 1).
  • Units of f′ = units of the output per unit of the input; marginal cost is C′(q) (Ch 2 · Section 3 and Section 5).
  • Rewriting powers: 1/x3 = x−3 and √x = x1/2 (Ch 1 · Section 10).

New notation: d/dx

The symbol d/dx means "the derivative with respect to x of". So d/dx(x2) means "the derivative of x2". It is the same thing as f′(x) when f(x) = x2. If the variable is called t or q, we write d/dt or d/dq.

d/dx [ f(x) ] = f′(x)

Rule 1 · The derivative of a constant

RuleDerivative of a constant (dérivée d'une constante)

If f(x) = k, where k is a fixed number, then f′(x) = 0.

d/dx (k) = 0
Example 1 (notes) · why the derivative of a constant is 0

With the graph. The graph of f(x) = k is a horizontal line. A horizontal line has slope 0 at every point. So f′(x) = 0.

With the limit definition.

f(x + h) = k and f(x) = k.

(f(x + h) − f(x)) / h = (k − k) / h

= 0 / h

= 0, for every h ≠ 0.

So the limit is 0, and f′(x) = 0.

➜ In words: a quantity that never changes has a rate of change of zero.

Rule 2 · The derivative of a linear function

RuleDerivative of a linear function

If f(x) = mx + b, then f′(x) = m.

d/dx (mx + b) = m
Example 2 (notes) · why the derivative of mx + b is m

With the graph. The graph is a straight line with slope m. The tangent line to a straight line is the line itself, so the slope is m everywhere.

With the limit definition.

f(x + h) = m(x + h) + b = mx + mh + b.

f(x + h) − f(x) = (mx + mh + b) − (mx + b) = mh.

(f(x + h) − f(x)) / h = mh / h = m.

So f′(x) = m.

Example 3 (notes) · two quick ones, and a link to Chapter 1

d/dx (3) = 0, because 3 is a constant.

d/dx (−(2/7)x + 6) = −2/7. The slope of the line is −2/7, and the constant 6 disappears.

Link to Chapter 1. The radio factory had C(q) = 24,000 + 7q dollars. So C′(q) = 7 dollars per radio, for every q.

➜ Each extra radio costs $7, at any level of production. The fixed cost $24,000 has derivative 0: it does not change when you make one more radio.

Rule 3 · A constant times a function

RuleConstant multiple rule (dérivée de k·f)
d/dx [ c · f(x) ] = c · f′(x)

A number in front waits outside while you differentiate the function.

Why? Multiplying f by c stretches the graph vertically by c. Every rise is multiplied by c, so every slope is multiplied by c too.

Example

d/dx (5x2) = 5 · d/dx (x2)

= 5 · 2x   (in Chapter 2 we found that the derivative of x2 is 2x)

= 10x.

Rule 4 · The power rule

RulePower rule (règle de la puissance)

For any constant number n (positive, negative, a whole number or a fraction):

d/dx (xn) = n · xn − 1
Bring the power down in front. Then subtract 1 from the power.

Check with Chapter 2. For f(x) = x3 the rule gives f′(x) = 3x2. So f′(2) = 3 × 22 = 3 × 4 = 12. That is exactly the number our table approached in Chapter 2. For x2 the rule gives 2x, the same pattern we found numerically.

(d) j(x) = √x

Two subtractions students get wrong
  • −3 − 1 = −4, not −2. Going down from −3 takes you further from zero.
  • 1/2 − 1 = −1/2, not 1/2 and not −1.

Rewrite first: roots and fractions become powers

The power rule works only on xn. So before you differentiate, rewrite every root and every "1 over" as a power of x. Two facts from Chapter 1 do all the work:

Rule 5 · Sums and differences

RuleSum and difference rule (dérivée d'une somme)
d/dx [ f(x) ± g(x) ] = f′(x) ± g′(x)

In words: differentiate term by term.

With Rules 1 to 5 together you can differentiate any polynomial (polynôme) in one line. Example: p(x) = 4x3 − 2x + 7 gives p′(x) = 12x2 − 2 + 0 = 12x2 − 2.

Example 5 (notes) · term by term

(a) f(x) = 3x5 − 2x3 + 1/x2

Rewrite: f(x) = 3x5 − 2x3 + x−2.

Term 1: 3 · 5x4 = 15x4.

Term 2: −2 · 3x2 = −6x2.

Term 3: −2x−3.

f′(x) = 15x4 − 6x2 − 2x−3 = 15x4 − 6x2 − 2/x3.

(b) g(x) = ∛x + x3/3 + 19x

Rewrite: g(x) = x1/3 + (1/3)x3 + 19x.

Term 1: (1/3)x1/3 − 1 = (1/3)x−2/3.

Term 2: (1/3) · 3x2 = x2.

Term 3: 19.

g′(x) = (1/3)x−2/3 + x2 + 19 = 1/(3∛(x2)) + x2 + 19.

Reading note: in the PDF of the notes, the small 3 in front of the root is printed separately, so we read it as a cube root, ∛x. If your printed copy shows 3√x (three times √x), only the first term changes: 3 · 1/(2√x) = 3/(2√x).

Values of the derivative: f′(a)

To find f′(a): first find the formula f′(x), then put x = a into f′. Always in this order.

Example · f(x) = x3 − 3x (the function of Figure 3 in Chapter 2)

f′(x) = 3x2 − 3.

f′(2) = 3 × 22 − 3 = 12 − 3 = 9.

f′(0) = 3 × 0 − 3 = −3.

f′(1) = 3 × 1 − 3 = 0: the graph is flat at x = 1 (the bottom of the valley).

Do not put the number in first

f(2) = 23 − 3 × 2 = 8 − 6 = 2 is just a number, and the derivative of a number is 0. That is not f′(2) = 9. Differentiate first, substitute after.

Tangent lines with the rules (Example 6)

You know the tangent line formula from Chapter 2. The only difference now: f′(a) comes from a formula, not from a table.

Tangent line at x = a:  y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a)
Method · the tangent line (tangente) in 5 steps
  1. Point: compute f(a) with the formula of f.
  2. Derivative: find the formula f′(x).
  3. Slope: compute f′(a).
  4. Write y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a).
  5. Simplify to y = mx + b and check: at x = a, the line must give f(a).
Example 6 (notes) · tangent line to f(t) = 6t − t2 at t = 4

Step 1, point: f(4) = 6 × 4 − 42 = 24 − 16 = 8. The point is (4, 8).

Step 2, derivative: f′(t) = 6 − 2t.

Step 3, slope: f′(4) = 6 − 2 × 4 = 6 − 8 = −2.

Step 4: y = 8 + (−2)(t − 4) = 8 − 2(t − 4).

Step 5: y = 8 − 2t + 8 = 16 − 2t.

Check: at t = 4, y = 16 − 8 = 8 ✓.

➜ The tangent line is y = 16 − 2t. At t = 4 the function is decreasing at a rate of 2 units of y per unit of t.

Points where the tangent has a given slope

Sometimes the exam gives you the slope and asks for the point. Then you solve the equation f′(x) = slope. The most common case is a horizontal tangent (tangente horizontale): the slope is 0, so you solve f′(x) = 0. These points are the tops of hills and the bottoms of valleys.

Example · f(t) = 6t − t2 again

Horizontal tangent: f′(t) = 0

6 − 2t = 0

2t = 6

t = 3. Point: f(3) = 18 − 9 = 9, so (3, 9). This is the top of the hill in Figure 1.

Slope 2: f′(t) = 2

6 − 2t = 2

2t = 4

t = 2. Point: f(2) = 12 − 4 = 8, so (2, 8).

Notice: (2, 8) and (4, 8) are at the same height, with slopes +2 and −2. The parabola is symmetric around t = 3.

Example · f(x) = x3 − 3x

f′(x) = 3x2 − 3.

Horizontal tangent: 3x2 − 3 = 0

3x2 = 3

x2 = 1, so x = 1 or x = −1.

Points: f(1) = 1 − 3 = −2 → (1, −2), and f(−1) = −1 + 3 = 2 → (−1, 2).

Slope 9: 3x2 − 3 = 9

3x2 = 12

x2 = 4, so x = 2 or x = −2.

Points: f(2) = 8 − 6 = 2 → (2, 2), and f(−2) = −8 + 6 = −2 → (−2, −2).

Don't lose a solution

x2 = 4 has two solutions, 2 and −2. Forgetting the negative one loses half the marks.

Revenue and marginal revenue (Example 7)

In business, the derivative of revenue is the marginal revenue (recette marginale): approximately the extra revenue from selling one more unit (Ch 2 · Section 5).

Example 7 (notes) · R(q) = 1000q − 3q2 dollars for q units

(a) Find R(125) and explain it.

R(125) = 1000 × 125 − 3 × 1252

= 125,000 − 3 × 15,625

= 125,000 − 46,875

= $78,125.

➜ Selling 125 units brings in $78,125 of revenue.

(b) Find R′(125) and explain it.

R′(q) = 1000 − 6q.

R′(125) = 1000 − 6 × 125 = 1000 − 750 = 250.

Units: dollars per unit.

➜ When 125 units are sold, selling one more unit brings in about $250 more revenue.

Check: R(126) = 126,000 − 3 × 15,876 = 126,000 − 47,628 = 78,372. So R(126) − R(125) = 78,372 − 78,125 = $247, very close to $250.

(c) Going further. R′(q) = 0 when 1000 − 6q = 0, so q = 1000/6 ≈ 166.7 units. Before that, each extra unit still adds revenue (R′ > 0). After it, selling more lowers revenue (R′ < 0), because the price has to fall too much. The maximum revenue is R(166.7) ≈ $83,333.

Four parts in every answer

Give the formula (R′(q) = 1000 − 6q), the number (R′(125) = 250), the units (dollars per unit) and the words ("one more unit brings in about $250"). On the exam, the sentence earns marks.

Common mistakes
  • (x−3)′ = −3x−2 ✗. Correct: −3x−4, because −3 − 1 = −4.
  • (√x)′ = √(1/x) or 1/√x ✗. Correct: 1/(2√x). The 1/2 comes down in front.
  • (1/x2)′ = 1/(2x) ✗. You cannot differentiate the bottom alone. Rewrite: x−2 → −2x−3 = −2/x3.
  • (7)′ = 7 ✗ → 0. (5x)′ = 5x ✗ → 5.
  • (x3 + 10)′ = 3x2 + 10 ✗. The constant disappears: 3x2.
  • (2x)′ = x · 2x − 1 ✗. The power rule needs x in the base and a number in the exponent. 2x is an exponential: see Section 2.
Exam alert

Two classic questions: (1) "Find f′(x) and f′(a)": show the rewrite step and name the rule. (2) "Find the equation of the tangent line at x = a": f(a) comes from f, f′(a) comes from f′. Write the final answer as y = mx + b and check it at x = a. Problem 2 of the Moodle set is exactly this.

7 is a constant. Its graph is a horizontal line, with slope 0 everywhere. (a) gives the function instead of its derivative. (b) is the derivative of x, not of 7. (d) goes in the wrong direction: 7x is the function whose derivative is 7.

Write 1/x3 as a power of x.

Write ∛x as a power of x.

The cube root is the power 1/3: ∛x = x1/3 (just as √x = x1/2). Check: (x1/3)3 = x1 = x ✓.

Q11True or false

d/dx (x3 + 10) = 3x2 + 10.

False. Differentiate term by term: (x3)′ = 3x2 and (10)′ = 0. So the derivative is just 3x2. Adding 10 moves the graph up but does not change any slope.
Q12Multiple choice

f(x) = 4x5 − 3x2 + 2x − 9. Then f′(x) = ?

Term by term: 4 · 5x4 = 20x4; −3 · 2x = −6x; (2x)′ = 2; (−9)′ = 0. So f′(x) = 20x4 − 6x + 2. (b) keeps the constant; (c) forgets to bring the powers down; (d) adds 1 to the powers instead of subtracting 1.

Where does f(x) = x2 − 6x + 1 have a horizontal tangent?

Q16Interpretation · marginal revenue

A shop's revenue is R(q) = 500q − 2q2 TND for q units. Which sentence about R′(100) is correct?

R′(q) = 500 − 4q, so R′(100) = 500 − 400 = 100 TND per unit: the 101st unit adds about 100 TND. (a) confuses R′(100) with R(100) = 50,000 − 20,000 = 30,000 TND. (c) differentiates 2q2 as 2q (the 2 was not brought down). (d) is false: R′ = 0 at q = 125, not 100.
Q17True or false

To find f′(4), you can first compute f(4) and then differentiate that number.

False. f(4) is a single number, and the derivative of any number is 0. You must find the formula f′(x) first and then put x = 4 into it.

A shop sells hand-painted plates (illustrative numbers). Its daily revenue is R(q) = 60q − 0.5q2 TND when it sells q plates. (a) Find R(40) and explain it. (b) Find R′(q) and R′(40), and explain R′(40) with units. (c) Should the shop try to sell more than 60 plates a day?

Show the full solution

Derivatives of exponential and logarithmic functions

In Chapter 1 you met exponential functions (P = P0at, P0ekt) and the natural logarithm ln x. They describe interest, populations, prices and much more. Their derivatives are very short. But they follow different rules from powers: in xn the variable is in the base, in ax the variable is in the exponent. Mixing up the two is the number-one error of this chapter.

Power: x5 → 5x4. Exponential: 5x → (ln 5) · 5x. Same symbols, different rules.

The exponential function ex

RuleDerivative of ex (dérivée de la fonction exponentielle)
d/dx (ex) = ex

ex is its own derivative.

On the graph this means: at every point, the slope equals the height (Figure 2). At x = 0 the height is e0 = 1, so the slope is 1. At x = 1 the height is e ≈ 2.718, so the slope is 2.718. The higher the curve, the steeper it is.

Quick check with the Chapter 2 method: (e0.001 − e0) / 0.001 = (1.0010005 − 1) / 0.001 ≈ 1.0005. So the slope at x = 0 is about 1, which is e0 ✓.

ekx: a number times x in the exponent

RuleDerivative of ekx
d/dx (ekx) = k · ekx

The number k comes down in front. The exponential stays exactly the same.

(In Section 3 you will see why: it is a first, simple use of the chain rule.)

Examples

d/dx (e3x) = 3e3x.

d/dt (e−0.5t) = −0.5e−0.5t. The derivative is negative: this is decay.

d/dt (1000e0.08t) = 1000 × 0.08e0.08t = 80e0.08t.

Other bases: ax

RuleDerivative of ax (a > 0)
d/dx (ax) = (ln a) · ax

Why? From Chapter 1, a = eln a. So ax = (eln a)x = e(ln a)x. This is ekx with k = ln a. So its derivative is (ln a) · e(ln a)x = (ln a) · ax.

Check 1: with a = e, ln e = 1, so (ex)′ = 1 · ex ✓.

Check 2, with Chapter 2: there you estimated the slope of 2x at x = 1 with h = 0.001 and found about 1.386. The rule gives (ln 2) × 21 = 0.6931 × 2 = 1.3863 ✓.

The chain rule

How do you differentiate (3x4 + 1)5? You could expand it, but that means multiplying (3x4 + 1) by itself five times: long, and easy to get wrong. And √(x2 + 3) cannot be expanded at all. These functions are composite functions (fonctions composées): one function sits inside another (Ch 1 · Section 9). To differentiate them we need the chain rule (dérivation des fonctions composées, also called la règle de la chaîne).

Inside and outside

In f(g(x)), g is the inside function (fonction intérieure) and f is the outside function (fonction extérieure). To find them, imagine you compute the function for a number with a calculator. The step you do first is the inside. The step you do last is the outside.

Example · y = √(x2 + 3) at x = 1

First you compute x2 + 3 = 1 + 3 = 4. That is the inside: u = g(x) = x2 + 3.

Last you take the square root: √4 = 2. That is the outside: y = f(u) = √u.

Why do we multiply? Follow a small change through the two machines, starting at x = 1:

QuantityBeforeAfterChangeCompared with the step before
x11.01+0.01the starting change
u = x2 + 344.0201+0.0201about 2 times the change in x (du/dx = 2x = 2)
y = √u22.0050+0.0050about 1/4 of the change in u (dy/du = 1/(2√4) = 1/4)

So y changed by about 0.0050 when x changed by 0.01: a rate of 0.0050 ÷ 0.01 = 0.5 = 2 × 1/4. The rates multiply. It works like gears: if gear A turns gear B 2 times faster, and B turns gear C 3 times faster, then C turns 2 × 3 = 6 times faster than A.

RuleThe chain rule (dérivation des fonctions composées)
d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)

In words: the derivative of the outside (with the inside left unchanged inside it) × the derivative of the inside. With u = g(x), in Leibniz notation:

dy/dx = (dy/du) · (du/dx)
Method · the chain rule in 4 steps
  1. Name the inside u and the outside.
  2. Differentiate the outside as if u were x. Leave u (the whole inside) untouched.
  3. Multiply by the derivative of the inside, u′.
  4. Replace u by the inside and simplify.

The product rule and the quotient rule

Many business functions are products or quotients: revenue = price × quantity, average cost = total cost ÷ quantity. Here is the surprise: the derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. This section gives you the correct rules, then a method to choose the right rule for any function.

First, a warning: (fg)′ ≠ f′g′

Take f(x) = x and g(x) = x.

Then f(x) · g(x) = x2, whose derivative is 2x.

But f′(x) · g′(x) = 1 × 1 = 1.

2x is not 1, so "multiply the derivatives" is wrong. We need a real rule.

The product rule

RuleProduct rule (règle du produit)
(f · g)′ = f′ · g + f · g′

In words: the derivative of the first × the second, plus the first × the derivative of the second.

In symbols: the change in area is Δ(fg) ≈ Δf · g + f · Δg. Divide by Δx: Δ(fg)/Δx ≈ (Δf/Δx) · g + f · (Δg/Δx). When Δx → 0, the two fractions become f′ and g′, and the corner vanishes. That gives f′g + fg′.

Method · product rule without mistakes
  1. Call the two factors u and v.
  2. Write a small table: u, u′, v, v′.
  3. Put them into u′v + uv′.
  4. Simplify, and factor out what the two terms have in common.
Example 1 (notes) · two products

(a) f(x) = x2e2x

u = x2 → u′ = 2x.

v = e2x → v′ = 2e2x  (rule ekx).

f′(x) = u′v + uv′ = 2x · e2x + x2 · 2e2x

= 2xe2x + 2x2e2x

= 2xe2x(1 + x)  (we factored out 2xe2x).

(b) g(x) = x3 ln(x + 1)

u = x3 → u′ = 3x2.

v = ln(x + 1) → v′ = 1/(x + 1)  (chain rule: the inside x + 1 has derivative 1).

g′(x) = 3x2 · ln(x + 1) + x3 · 1/(x + 1)

= 3x2 ln(x + 1) + x3/(x + 1).

Check the rule on a product you can expand

f(x) = (x2 + 1)(x3 − 2x).

With the product rule: u = x2 + 1, u′ = 2x; v = x3 − 2x, v′ = 3x2 − 2.

f′(x) = 2x(x3 − 2x) + (x2 + 1)(3x2 − 2)

= 2x4 − 4x2 + 3x4 − 2x2 + 3x2 − 2

= 5x4 − 3x2 − 2.

By expanding first: f(x) = x5 − 2x3 + x3 − 2x = x5 − x3 − 2x.

f′(x) = 5x4 − 3x2 − 2 ✓. Same answer: the rule works.

Business example · the revenue of a café in La Marsa

A café's monthly revenue is R = p × q, where p is the price of a cappuccino (TND) and q is the number of cups sold per month. Both change over time. Right now (illustrative numbers):

  • p = 3.5 TND, and the price is rising by 0.2 TND per month (p′ = 0.2);
  • q = 1,200 cups, and sales are falling by 30 cups per month (q′ = −30).
Step by step

R′ = p′ · q + p · q′

= 0.2 × 1,200 + 3.5 × (−30)

= 240 − 105

= 135.

➜ The café's monthly revenue is increasing by about 135 TND each month. The higher price adds 240 TND; the lost customers take away 105 TND.

With the wrong rule, p′ · q′ = 0.2 × (−30) = −6, you would think revenue is falling. The product rule shows both effects.

The quotient rule

RuleQuotient rule (règle du quotient)
(f / g)′ = (f′ · g − f · g′) / g2

In words: (derivative of the top × the bottom − the top × derivative of the bottom), all over the bottom squared.

Memory trick

"Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared." Low = the bottom, high = the top, "d" = "the derivative of". Always start with the derivative of the top. Because of the minus sign, the order matters: if you swap the two terms, your answer has the wrong sign.

Examples (notes) · two quotients

(a) f(x) = 5x2 / (x3 + 1)

Top: u = 5x2 → u′ = 10x.

Bottom: v = x3 + 1 → v′ = 3x2.

f′(x) = (u′v − uv′) / v2

= [10x(x3 + 1) − 5x2 · 3x2] / (x3 + 1)2

= [10x4 + 10x − 15x4] / (x3 + 1)2

= (10x − 5x4) / (x3 + 1)2

= 5x(2 − x3) / (x3 + 1)2.

(b) g(x) = ex / x2

Top: u = ex → u′ = ex.

Bottom: v = x2 → v′ = 2x.

g′(x) = (ex · x2 − ex · 2x) / (x2)2

= ex(x2 − 2x) / x4

= ex · x(x − 2) / x4

= ex(x − 2) / x3  (one x cancels).

Leave the bottom squared

Do not expand (x3 + 1)2. Simplify only the top. A squared bottom is a perfectly good final answer.

When you do NOT need the quotient rule

  • A number on the bottom: (x3 + 5x)/4 = (1/4)(x3 + 5x), so the derivative is (3x2 + 5)/4. The constant multiple rule is enough.
  • A number on top, a power on the bottom: 7/x3 = 7x−3, so the derivative is −21x−4 = −21/x4. Power rule.
  • 1 over something: 1/(3x2 + 4) = (3x2 + 4)−1, so the derivative is −1 · (3x2 + 4)−2 · 6x = −6x/(3x2 + 4)2. Chain rule (Problem 1, item 4). The quotient rule gives the same: (0 · (3x2 + 4) − 1 · 6x)/(3x2 + 4)2 ✓.
  • You can divide first: (x2 + 1)/x = x + 1/x = x + x−1, so the derivative is 1 − x−2 = 1 − 1/x2.

The last-operation test

To choose the main rule, imagine computing f(x) for a number, say x = 2. The last operation you do decides:

  • last step × → product rule; last step ÷ → quotient rule;
  • last step is a power, a root, e… or ln(…) of something that is not just x → chain rule;
  • last step + or − → sum rule, then deal with each term.

Example: x2e2x at x = 2: compute 22 = 4, compute e4 ≈ 54.6, then multiply. Last step × → product rule (and inside it, e2x uses the rule ekx).

Example: ln(x3 + 1) at x = 2: compute 23 + 1 = 9, then take ln. Last step ln → chain rule.

Common mistakes
  • (fg)′ = f′g′ ✗ and (f/g)′ = f′/g′ ✗. Both are wrong. Use the two rules above.
  • Swapping the order in the quotient rule: (fg′ − f′g)/g2 gives the opposite sign. Start with the derivative of the top.
  • Forgetting to square the bottom, or squaring the top by mistake.
  • Using the quotient rule when you don't need it (a number on the bottom, or 7/x3). It is not wrong, but it is slower and riskier.

Average cost, marginal cost and differentials

Two tools from the chapter objectives are left. The first is the derivative of an average: this is where the quotient rule becomes a business tool. The second is the differential, a fast way to estimate a change.

Average cost and marginal cost: two different questions

DefinitionAverage cost (coût moyen)
a(q) = C(q) / q

The cost per unit when q units are produced. It answers: "on average, how much did each unit cost?"

ReminderMarginal cost (coût marginal)
MC = C′(q)

The approximate cost of producing one more unit. It answers: "how much will the next unit cost?"

The derivative of average cost

Step by step · the quotient rule on a(q) = C(q)/q

Top: u = C(q), so u′ = C′(q). Bottom: v = q, so v′ = 1.

a′(q) = [C′(q) · q − C(q) · 1] / q2

= [qC′(q) − C(q)] / q2

Divide the top and the bottom by q:

= [C′(q) − C(q)/q] / q

= [C′(q) − a(q)] / q.

a′(q) = ( C′(q) − a(q) ) / q  =  (MC − AC) / q

Since q is positive, a′(q) always has the same sign as MC − AC:

SituationSign of a′(q)So the average cost is …
MC < ACnegativefalling
MC = ACzeroat its minimum (the bottom of the U)
MC > ACpositiverising
Marginal cost pulls the average towards it. Below the average, it pulls the average down. Above the average, it pulls it up.
It works exactly like your grades

After four tests your average is 12/20 (total 48 points). The next test is your "marginal" grade:

next grade 15 (above the average) → new average (48 + 15)/5 = 12.6: it goes up;

next grade 9 (below the average) → new average (48 + 9)/5 = 11.4: it goes down;

next grade 12 (equal to the average) → the average stays at 12.

Worked example · the flashlight factory (Problem 6)

The cost of producing q flashlights is C(q) = 0.0001q3 − 0.08q2 + 40q + 5000 dollars.

Step by step

Step 1, marginal cost: C′(q) = 0.0003q2 − 0.16q + 40.

Step 2, average cost: a(q) = C(q)/q = 0.0001q2 − 0.08q + 40 + 5000/q.

Step 3, one value in full detail, q = 300:

C(300) = 0.0001 × 27,000,000 − 0.08 × 90,000 + 40 × 300 + 5000

= 2,700 − 7,200 + 12,000 + 5,000 = $12,500.

a(300) = 12,500/300 = $41.67 per flashlight.

C′(300) = 0.0003 × 90,000 − 0.16 × 300 + 40 = 27 − 48 + 40 = $19 per flashlight.

a′(300) = (19 − 41.67)/300 = −22.67/300 ≈ −0.0756.

➜ At 300 flashlights, each flashlight costs $41.67 on average, but the next one costs only about $19. So producing one more lowers the average cost, by about 7.6 cents per flashlight.

Average revenue and average profit

The same idea works for any total: average revenue (recette moyenne) R(q)/q and average profit P(q)/q. Their derivatives come from the quotient rule.

Average revenue is the price

Take R(q) = 1000q − 3q2 (Example 7). Average revenue = R(q)/q = 1000 − 3q.

At q = 100: 1000 − 300 = $700 per unit.

This is the price of each unit when 100 units are sold. In general, if every unit is sold at the same price p, then R = pq and R/q = p.

Average profit · the firm of Problem 7

P(q) = −0.02q2 + 300q − 200,000 dollars, and AP(q) = P(q)/q.

Top: u = P(q), u′ = −0.04q + 300. Bottom: v = q, v′ = 1.

AP′(q) = [(−0.04q + 300) · q − (−0.02q2 + 300q − 200,000) · 1] / q2

= [−0.04q2 + 300q + 0.02q2 − 300q + 200,000] / q2

= (−0.02q2 + 200,000) / q2.

At q = 2,000: P(2,000) = −80,000 + 600,000 − 200,000 = 320,000, so AP(2,000) = 320,000/2,000 = $160 per unit.

AP′(2,000) = (−0.02 × 4,000,000 + 200,000) / 4,000,000

= (−80,000 + 200,000) / 4,000,000

= 120,000 / 4,000,000 = 0.03.

➜ At 2,000 units, the firm earns $160 of profit per unit, and this profit per unit is rising by about 3 cents for each extra unit.

Marginal is not average
  • "Marginal" = a derivative = about the next unit. "Average" = total ÷ q = about all the units so far.
  • A falling average does not mean a falling marginal cost. From q = 300 to 400, MC rises (19 → 24) while AC still falls (41.67 → 36.50), because MC is still below AC.
  • MC crosses AC at the minimum of AC, not at the minimum of MC.

Differentials: quick estimates

In Chapter 2 you used the local linear approximation f(x) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)Δx (Ch 2 · Section 3). A differential is the same idea with its own notation. Now that f′ comes from a formula, the estimate takes seconds.

DefinitionDifferential (différentielle)

If y = f(x) and dx is a small change in x, the differential of y is

dy = f′(x) · dx

For a small dx, the real change Δy = f(x + dx) − f(x) is approximately dy: Δy ≈ dy.

Method · estimate with a differential
  1. Choose the "nice" point x and the small change dx.
  2. Compute f′(x).
  3. Compute dy = f′(x) · dx. It has the units of y.
  4. New value ≈ old value + dy. Say it in a sentence.
Four estimates

(1) √25.3 without a calculator. y = √x, x = 25, dx = 0.3.

dy = (1/(2√x)) · dx = (1/(2 × 5)) × 0.3 = 0.1 × 0.3 = 0.03.

√25.3 ≈ √25 + dy = 5 + 0.03 = 5.03. (Calculator: 5.02991. The error is less than 0.0001.)

(2) Flashlights: production rises from 200 to 205, so dq = 5.

dC = C′(200) · dq = 20 × 5 = $100.

➜ The 5 extra flashlights cost about $100. (Exact: C(205) − C(200) = 10,699.51 − 10,600 = $99.51.)

(3) Example 3's cost C(q) = 1000 + 300 ln q, from 500 to 510 units:

dC = (300/500) × 10 = 0.6 × 10 = $6. (Exact: 300 ln(510/500) = $5.94.)

(4) Example 4's bank account during the 3 months after year 10: dt = 0.25 year.

df = f′(10) × 0.25 = 178.04 × 0.25 = $44.51. (Exact: $44.96, a bit more, because the growth keeps speeding up.)

Exam alert

A typical question: "Use differentials to estimate the change in cost when production goes from 1,000 to 1,010 units." Answer: dC = C′(1000) × 10, with units, in a sentence. Do not compute C(1010) − C(1000) unless the question asks for the exact change.

2x − 4 = 0, so 2x = 4, so x = 2.

f(2) = 4 − 8 = −4.

➜ The tangent is horizontal at the point (2, −4), and the tangent line there is y = −4. This point is the vertex (the lowest point) of the parabola.

P4Problem · a shrinking sand dune

The height of a sand dune is f(t) = 700 − 3t2 centimetres, where t is the number of years since 2005. Find f(5) and f′(5). Give the units and explain what each number means.

Show the full solution

f(5) = 700 − 3 × 25 = 700 − 75 = 625 cm.

➜ In 2010 (t = 5), the dune is 625 cm (6.25 m) high.

f′(t) = −6t, so f′(5) = −30 cm per year.

➜ In 2010, the height of the dune is decreasing by about 30 cm per year.

Going further: f′(t) = −6t gets more negative over time, so the dune shrinks faster and faster. The model gives height 0 when 3t2 = 700, t = √(233.3) ≈ 15.3 years, around 2020.

P5Problem · world population

The world population is modelled by f(t) = 5.3(1.018)t billion people, t years after 1990. (a) Find f(0) and f′(0). (b) Find f(30) and f′(30). (c) Explain each answer in words, with units.

Show the full solution

Derivative: rule ax with a = 1.018: f′(t) = 5.3 · (ln 1.018) · (1.018)t, with ln 1.018 ≈ 0.017840.

(a) f(0) = 5.3 × 1 = 5.3 billion.

➜ In 1990 the world population was about 5.3 billion people.

f′(0) = 5.3 × 0.017840 × 1 = 0.0946 billion per year.

➜ In 1990 the population was growing by about 0.0946 billion = 94.6 million people per year.

(b) f(30) = 5.3 × 1.01830 = 5.3 × 1.70779 ≈ 9.05 billion.

➜ The model predicts about 9.05 billion people in 2020.

f′(30) = 9.0513 × 0.017840 ≈ 0.1615 billion per year.

➜ In 2020 the model's population grows by about 161.5 million people per year.

(c) Note that f′(t)/f(t) = ln 1.018 ≈ 0.0178 at every t: the relative growth rate is always about 1.78% per year. The number of people added per year grows only because the population itself grows.

Reality check: the real world population in 2020 was about 7.8 billion, not 9.05 billion. World growth slowed down after 1990, so a model with a fixed 1.8% growth rate overestimates the future.

P6Problem · flashlights

The cost of producing q flashlights is C(q) = 0.0001q3 − 0.08q2 + 40q + 5000 dollars. (1) Find the marginal cost function. (2) Find C′(200), C′(300), C′(400) and C′(600). (3) What do you notice about the marginal cost? Explain it in practical terms.

Show the full solution

(1) C′(q) = 0.0003q2 − 0.16q + 40 dollars per flashlight.

(2) C′(200) = 0.0003 × 40,000 − 0.16 × 200 + 40 = 12 − 32 + 40 = $20 per flashlight.

C′(300) = 27 − 48 + 40 = $19 per flashlight.

C′(400) = 48 − 64 + 40 = $24 per flashlight.

C′(600) = 108 − 96 + 40 = $52 per flashlight.

➜ For example, after 600 flashlights, producing one more costs about $52.

(3) The marginal cost first decreases (20 → 19), then increases (19 → 24 → 52). C′ is a parabola opening upwards, with its lowest point at q = 0.16/(2 × 0.0003) ≈ 267 flashlights, where C′ ≈ $18.67.

➜ In practice: at first, producing more makes each extra flashlight cheaper (machines and workers are used better, materials are bought in bulk). After about 267 flashlights, each extra flashlight costs more (overtime, a crowded factory, machines wearing out): this is called diminishing returns. Section 5 and Figure 6 show this marginal cost with the average cost.

P7Problem · revenue, cost and profit

The demand for a product is p = −0.02q + 400, where p is the price in dollars and q the number of units. (1) Find the revenue function R(q). (2) Find the marginal revenue R′(q). (3) Find R′(2000) and explain it. The cost function is C(q) = 100q + 200,000. (4) Find the profit function P(q). (5) Find the marginal profit P′(q). (6) Find P′(2000) and explain it. Should the company increase production?

Show the full solution

(1) Revenue = price × quantity: R(q) = pq = (−0.02q + 400)q = −0.02q2 + 400q.

(2) R′(q) = −0.04q + 400 dollars per unit.

(3) R′(2000) = −0.04 × 2000 + 400 = −80 + 400 = $320 per unit.

➜ When 2,000 units are sold, selling one more brings in about $320 of extra revenue.

(4) P(q) = R(q) − C(q) = −0.02q2 + 400q − 100q − 200,000 = −0.02q2 + 300q − 200,000.

(5) P′(q) = −0.04q + 300 dollars per unit.

(6) P′(2000) = −80 + 300 = $220 per unit.

➜ Producing and selling the 2,001st unit adds about $220 of profit. The marginal profit is positive, so the company should increase production. (Same result with Chapter 2: MR − MC = 320 − 100 = 220.)

Going further: P′(q) = 0 when q = 300/0.04 = 7,500 units. That is where profit is highest: P(7,500) = $925,000, with a price of p = −150 + 400 = $250.

P8Problem · a fitness centre

The number of members of a new fitness centre t weeks after it opens is N(t) = 100(64 + 4t)2/3, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 52. (1) Find N′(t). (2) How fast was the membership growing when the centre opened? (3) How fast was it growing at the beginning of the 40th week (t = 40)?

(2) N′(0) = (800/3) · 64−1/3 = (800/3) × (1/4) = 200/3 ≈ 66.7 members per week. (∛64 = 4.)

(3) 64 + 4 × 40 = 224 and ∛224 ≈ 6.0732.

P9Problem · a falling ball

A ball is dropped from a height of 1,250 feet. Its height after t seconds is y = f(t) = 1250 − 16t2 feet. (1) Find the velocity v(t) = f′(t). Why is it negative? (2) Find the acceleration a(t) = v′(t). (3) When does the ball hit the ground, and with what velocity?

Show the full solution

(1) v(t) = f′(t) = −32t feet per second.

It is negative because the height is decreasing: the ball is falling. That is what we expect.

(2) a(t) = v′(t) = −32 ft/s2 (feet per second, per second). The acceleration is constant: it is gravity. (This is the second derivative f″, Chapter 2.)

(3) The ball hits the ground when the height is 0:

New questions on the whole chapter, mixed like a real test. Do them without scrolling up, with a calculator and a sheet of paper. Write the rule you use next to each derivative. Aim for at least 16/20 on the quick questions, then write full answers to the three problems at the end.

T1Multiple choice

f(x) = x4 − 3x2 + 5x − 2. Then f′(x) = ?

Term by term: 4x3, then −3 · 2x = −6x, then 5, then 0 for the constant. f′(x) = 4x3 − 6x + 5. (a) forgets to bring the power 2 down in −3x2. (b) brings the powers down but does not subtract 1. (d) keeps the constant.
T2Multiple choice

d/dx (6/x3) = ?

Rewrite 6x−3, then 6 × (−3)x−4 = −18/x4. (b) uses −3 − 1 = −2. (d) is 6/(3x2): it differentiates only the bottom, which is never allowed.
T3Multiple choice

d/dx (4√x) = ?

4 · (√x)′ = 4 · 1/(2√x) = 2/√x. (b) forgets the constant 4. (c) forgets the 1/2 from the power rule. (a) keeps the power 1/2 instead of subtracting 1.
T4Multiple choice

d/dx (e4x) = ?

ekx → k ekx: 4e4x. (a) is the most common mistake: it forgets the derivative of the inside 4x. (c) is the power-rule trap.
T5Multiple choice

d/dx (x2 + 1)5 = ?

Chain rule: 5(x2 + 1)4 × 2x = 10x(x2 + 1)4. (a) forgets the inside derivative 2x. (b) replaces the inside by its derivative. (d) forgets to lower the power.
T6Multiple choice

d/dx (x2ex) = ?

Product rule: 2x · ex + x2 · ex = x ex(2 + x). Answer (b). (a) is f′g′ = 2x · ex, the classic wrong "rule" (fg)′ = f′g′. (d) treats the product like a sum.
T7Multiple choice

d/dx ln(5x) = ?

ln(5x) = ln 5 + ln x, so the derivative is 0 + 1/x = 1/x. With the chain rule: 5/(5x) = 1/x. (b) forgets the inside derivative 5.
T8Multiple choice

d/dx [x / (x2 + 1)] = ?

Quotient rule: [1 · (x2 + 1) − x · 2x] / (x2 + 1)2 = (1 − x2)/(x2 + 1)2. (b) has the order reversed, so its sign is wrong. (c) adds the two terms instead of subtracting. (a) is f′/g′.
T9Multiple choice

d/dx (5x) = ?

(ax)′ = (ln a) ax, so the answer is (ln 5) · 5x ≈ 1.6094 · 5x. (a) applies the power rule to an exponential. (b) would only be true for base e.
T10Tangent line

What is the tangent line to f(x) = x3 − 2x at x = 1?

f(1) = 1 − 2 = −1. f′(x) = 3x2 − 2, so f′(1) = 1. y = −1 + 1(x − 1) = x − 2. Check: at x = 1, y = −1 ✓. (d) passes through (1, −1) but uses the slope 3, from forgetting the −2 in f′. (b) has the right slope but misses the point.
T11Tangent line

What is the tangent line to f(x) = ex at x = 0?

f(0) = e0 = 1 and f′(0) = e0 = 1. y = 1 + 1(x − 0) = x + 1. You can see it in Figure 2: at (0, 1) the tangent has slope 1. (d) is the horizontal line through the point, but ex is not flat there.
T12Horizontal tangent

Where does f(x) = x3 − 12x have a horizontal tangent?

f′(x) = 3x2 − 12 = 0 ⇔ x2 = 4 ⇔ x = 2 or x = −2. The points are (2, −16) and (−2, 16). (a) loses the negative solution. (d) solves f(x) = 0 instead of f′(x) = 0.
T13Interpretation · marginal revenue

A company's revenue is R(q) = 80q − 0.02q2 TND. Which statement about R′(1000) is correct?

R′(q) = 80 − 0.04q, so R′(1000) = 80 − 40 = 40 TND per unit: the 1,001st unit adds about 40 TND. (c) comes from 80 − 0.02 × 1000, forgetting to bring the power 2 down. (d) is false: R′ = 0 at q = 2,000.
T14Marginal cost

C(q) = 2000 + 400 ln q TND. What is the marginal cost at q = 200?

C′(q) = 400/q, so C′(200) = 2 TND per unit: after 200 units, one more costs about 2 TND. (b) divides the wrong way (200/400). (d) is the fixed cost, which has derivative 0.
T15Average vs marginal

At q = 300, the average cost is 12 TND per unit and the marginal cost is 9 TND per unit. What is happening to the average cost?

a′(q) = (MC − AC)/q = (9 − 12)/300 = −0.01 < 0, so the average cost is falling. The next unit (9 TND) is cheaper than the average (12 TND), so it pulls the average down.
T16True or false

At the quantity where the average cost is lowest, the marginal cost equals the average cost.

True. At the lowest point of the average cost, a′(q) = 0, and a′(q) = (MC − AC)/q. So MC − AC = 0, that is MC = AC. For the flashlights: MC = AC = $35 at q = 500.
T17Differentials

Use a differential with x = 100 to estimate √102.

f(x) = √x, f′(x) = 1/(2√x), so f′(100) = 1/20 = 0.05. With dx = 2: dy = 0.05 × 2 = 0.1, so √102 ≈ 10 + 0.1 = 10.1 (calculator: 10.0995). (b) forgets the 1/2 in the derivative. (c) uses 1/(2x) instead of 1/(2√x).
T18Continuous growth

A balance is f(t) = 2000e0.05t dollars after t years. How fast is it growing after 10 years?

f′(t) = 2000 × 0.05e0.05t = 100e0.05t, so f′(10) = 100e0.5 = 100 × 1.648721 = $164.87 per year. (a) is f′(0), the growth at the start. (b) is f(10), the balance itself, not its rate of growth.
T19Chain rule · value

f(x) = √(x2 + 9). What is f′(4)?

f′(x) = 2x / (2√(x2 + 9)) = x/√(x2 + 9). At x = 4: 4/√25 = 4/5 = 0.8. (a) = 1/(2 × 5) forgets the inside derivative 2x. (b) is f(4), the height. (d) is only the inside derivative 2x at x = 4.
T20Average cost · minimum

The cost of q units is C(q) = q2 + 400 dollars. At which quantity is the average cost a(q) = C(q)/q the lowest?

T21Problem · an olive-oil mill in Sfax

A mill's daily cost of producing q litres of olive oil is C(q) = 0.01q2 + 3q + 400 TND (illustrative numbers). (a) Find the marginal cost function, then MC(100), and explain it. (b) Find the average cost a(q) and, with the quotient rule, a′(q). Is the average cost rising or falling at q = 100? (c) Find the quantity with the lowest average cost and check that MC = AC there. (d) Use a differential to estimate the extra cost of going from 100 to 105 litres.

Show the full solution

(a) MC(q) = C′(q) = 0.02q + 3. MC(100) = 2 + 3 = 5 TND per litre.

➜ After 100 litres, producing one more litre costs about 5 TND.

(b) a(q) = C(q)/q = 0.01q + 3 + 400/q.

Quotient rule: a′(q) = [q(0.02q + 3) − (0.01q2 + 3q + 400)] / q2 = (0.01q2 − 400)/q2.

At q = 100: a(100) = 1 + 3 + 4 = 8 TND per litre, and a′(100) = (100 − 400)/10,000 = −0.03.

Check with (MC − AC)/q = (5 − 8)/100 = −0.03 ✓. Negative, so the average cost is falling at q = 100.

(c) a′(q) = 0 ⇔ 0.01q2 = 400 ⇔ q2 = 40,000 ⇔ q = 200 litres.

a(200) = 2 + 3 + 2 = 7 TND per litre, and MC(200) = 4 + 3 = 7 TND per litre. MC = AC ✓.

➜ The lowest average cost is 7 TND per litre, at 200 litres per day.

(d) dC = MC(100) × 5 = 5 × 5 = 25 TND. (Exact: C(105) − C(100) = 825.25 − 800 = 25.25 TND.)

(b) f′(t)/f(t) = 700e0.07t / 10,000e0.07t = 0.07 = 7% per year, at every moment.

Recap, derivative rules sheet and key terms

What to remember

  • Basic rules: a constant → 0; mx + b → m; c · f → c · f′; xn → n xn − 1 (rewrite roots and fractions as powers first); sums and differences term by term.
  • Exponentials and logs: ex → ex; ekx → k ekx; ax → (ln a) ax; ln x → 1/x. A power (x in the base) is not an exponential (x in the exponent).
  • Chain rule: derivative of the outside (inside unchanged) × derivative of the inside. Special cases: [g]n, eg, ln g.
  • Product rule: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′. Quotient rule: (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′)/g2, starting with the derivative of the top. Never (fg)′ = f′g′.
  • Choosing the rule: rewrite first if you can, then use the last-operation test.
  • f′(a): differentiate first, substitute after. Tangent line: y = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a). Horizontal tangent: solve f′(x) = 0 (and don't lose negative solutions).
  • Average cost a(q) = C(q)/q and a′(q) = (MC − AC)/q: MC < AC → AC falls; MC > AC → AC rises; MC = AC at the lowest average cost.
  • Differential: dy = f′(x) dx ≈ Δy, for a small dx.
  • Every answer: formula, number, units, and a sentence in words, as the Rule of Four asks.
